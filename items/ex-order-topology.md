## Example

Let $(L, \le)$ be a totally ordered set ([[def-partial-order]]) with at least two
elements. For $a \in L$ write

$$L_{<a} := \{\, t \in L : t < a \,\}, \qquad L_{>a} := \{\, t \in L : a < t \,\}$$

for the **open rays**, and let $\mathcal{S}_L$ be the family of all open rays.
The **order topology** on $L$ is $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}} := \langle \mathcal{S}_L \rangle$,
the topology generated by $\mathcal{S}_L$ ([[def-topology-basis-subbasis]]).
Then:

1. **A basis.** By [[thm-basis-criterion]] the finite intersections of open rays
   form a basis for $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$, and every such intersection is
   $L$ itself (the empty intersection), an open ray, or an **open interval**
   $(a,b) := L_{>a} \cap L_{<b}$. So
   $$\mathcal{B}_L := \{L\} \cup \mathcal{S}_L \cup \{\, (a,b) : a, b \in L \,\}$$
   is a basis for the order topology.
2. **On $\mathbb{R}$ the order topology is the usual topology.** Taking
   $L = \mathbb{R}$ with its order ([[def-real-order]], [[def-ordered-field]]),
   $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}} = \mathcal{T}_{d_{\mathbb{R}}}$, the metric
   topology of $d_{\mathbb{R}}(x,y) = |x-y|$
   ([[lem-real-line-is-a-metric-space]], [[def-metrizable-space]]). The rays and
   intervals of claim 1 are then exactly the intervals of that shape in the sense
   of [[def-interval]].

Claim 2 identifies the order topology of $\mathbb{R}$ with a topology already in
the library rather than introducing a second one.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A totally ordered set $(L,\le)$ with at least two elements, the family $\mathcal{S}_L$ of its open rays, and $\mathbb{R}$ with its order and its usual metric $d_{\mathbb{R}}(x,y) = |x-y|$.

[A1] $\le$ is reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive and total, and $s < t$ abbreviates $s \le t$ with $s \ne t$ ([[def-partial-order]]); on $\mathbb{R}$ this is the order of [[def-real-order]] and [[def-ordered-field]].

[L1] $\langle \mathcal{S} \rangle$ is the coarsest topology containing $\mathcal{S}$, and a family is a basis for a topology exactly when the topology is the family of unions of its subfamilies ([[def-topology-basis-subbasis]]).

[L2] The finite intersections of $\mathcal{S}$, the empty intersection being the whole set, form a basis for $\langle \mathcal{S} \rangle$ ([[thm-basis-criterion]], claim 2).

[L3] In $\mathbb{R}$ the open ball is $B(x,r) = (x-r,\ x+r)$ and $U$ is open in the usual topology exactly when every $x \in U$ has some $r > 0$ with $(x-r,x+r) \subseteq U$ ([[lem-real-line-is-a-metric-space]], [[def-metric-ball]], [[def-metric-topology]], [[def-metrizable-space]], [[def-interval]]).

[L4] Balls are open in a metric topology, and a topology is closed under arbitrary unions ([[thm-metric-open-set-algebra]], [[def-topological-space]]).

## Verification

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 An intersection of finitely many open rays is $L$ when there are none; when there are some, group them into the lower rays $L_{<b_1}, \dots, L_{<b_m}$ and the upper rays $L_{>a_1}, \dots, L_{>a_n}$. Since $\le$ is total, a nonempty finite set of elements of $L$ has a least and a greatest member, so the intersection of the lower rays is $L_{<b}$ with $b$ least among the $b_i$, and that of the upper rays is $L_{>a}$ with $a$ greatest among the $a_j$; the whole intersection is therefore $L$, a single ray, or $L_{>a} \cap L_{<b} = (a,b)$. [A1, L2]

1.2 In $\mathbb{R}$ every open ray is open in the usual topology: if $x < b$ then $r := b - x > 0$ and $(x - r,\ x + r) \subseteq (-\infty, b)$, since $t < x + r = b$; symmetrically, if $a < x$ then $r := x - a > 0$ and $(x-r,\ x+r) \subseteq (a, \infty)$. [A1, L3]

1.3 In $\mathbb{R}$ every ball is an intersection of two open rays: $(x-r,\ x+r) = \mathbb{R}_{>x-r} \cap \mathbb{R}_{<x+r}$, directly from the definitions of the two rays and of the interval. [A1, L3]

2.1 By step 1.1 and [L2] the family $\mathcal{B}_L$ of claim 1 is a basis for $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$, since it is exactly the family of finite intersections of open rays; this is claim 1. [step 1.1, L1, L2]

2.2 By step 1.2 the usual topology of $\mathbb{R}$ contains $\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}$, so it contains $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}} = \langle \mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}} \rangle$, the latter being the coarsest such topology. [step 1.2, L1]

3.1 Conversely, let $U$ be open in the usual topology of $\mathbb{R}$; for each $x \in U$ there is $r > 0$ with $(x-r, x+r) \subseteq U$, and $(x-r,x+r)$ is an intersection of two open rays by step 1.3, hence a member of $\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{R}}$ and so open in $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$; therefore $U$ is a union of members of $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$ and so lies in $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{ord}}$. [step 1.3, step 2.1, L1, L3, L4]

4.1 Steps 2.2 and 3.1 give the two inclusions, so the order topology of $\mathbb{R}$ is its usual topology, which is claim 2. [step 2.1, step 2.2, step 3.1] ∎

## Remarks

- **All three descriptions of $\mathbb{R}$'s topology name one collection of open sets.** Claim 2 identifies the order topology with the metric topology of $d_{\mathbb{R}}$, and [[rem-r-native-topology-scope]] records that the metric topology and the order-native topology built earlier in this library are in turn the same collection. Nothing below uses that third description; it is named so that a reader moving between the pages knows there is one topology and not two.

- **The hypothesis that $L$ has at least two elements** is what keeps the rays from being useless: on a one-point set every ray is empty and the order topology is the only topology there is. Nothing else in claim 1 uses it.

- **The order topology is not always metrizable**, and the order alone does not decide the matter. Claim 2 is a statement about $\mathbb{R}$ and is proved from the specific fact that the balls of $d_{\mathbb{R}}$ are the bounded open intervals; no general theorem is being invoked, and none is available here.

- **The Sorgenfrey line is not the order topology of the usual order on $\mathbb{R}$** ([[ex-sorgenfrey-line]]). It is generated by the half-open intervals $[a,b)$, which are not unions of open rays and open intervals, so it is strictly finer than the order topology of that order; the order it comes from is the same order, which shows that "generated by intervals" is not the same as "the order topology". Whether some *other* total order on $\mathbb{R}$ has the Sorgenfrey topology as its order topology is a different question, and nothing here or elsewhere in this library answers it.
