## Statement

Let $G=\Delta\times\Gamma$ be a profinite abelian group, where $\Delta$ is finite and $\Gamma\cong\mathbb Z_p$, and let $\Lambda(G)=\varprojlim_U\mathbb Z_p[G/U]$. Let $\epsilon:\Lambda(G)\to\mathbb Z_p$ be the augmentation homomorphism, defined by $\epsilon([g])=1$ for every $g\in G$, and put $I(G)=\ker\epsilon$. Let $Q(\Lambda(G))$ be the localization at the non-zero-divisors of $\Lambda(G)$. If $\mu\in Q(\Lambda(G))$ satisfies $([g]-[1])\mu\in\Lambda(G)$ for every $g\in G$, then $\mu I(G)\subseteq\Lambda(G)$, and $\mu I(G)$ is an ideal of $\Lambda(G)$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A profinite abelian group $G=\Delta\times\Gamma$ with $\Delta$ finite and $\Gamma\cong\mathbb Z_p$, its completed group algebra $\Lambda(G)$, and an element $\mu\in Q(\Lambda(G))$ satisfying the group-difference integrality condition in the Statement.

[F1] $\Lambda(G)$ is the inverse limit of the finite group rings $\mathbb Z_p[G/U]$, and when $\Gamma\cong\mathbb Z_p$ its algebra is identified with $\mathbb Z_p[\![T]\!]$ by sending a topological generator to $1+T$. The finite-coefficient coordinate, including both quotient directions, is proved locally in [[lem-finite-coefficient-cyclotomic-completed-group-algebra-coordinate]]; only the inverse-limit construction is used from [[def-iwasawa-algebra-of-profinite-abelian-group]].

[F2] For every $g\in G$, a Deligne–Ribet pseudomeasure satisfies $([g]-[1])\mu\in\Lambda(G)$. [[def-deligne-ribet-pseudomeasure-on-profinite-abelian-group]]

## Proof

1.1 Choose a topological generator $\gamma$ of $\Gamma$ and a finite generating set $d_1,\ldots,d_r$ of $\Delta$. Since $\Delta$ is finite, the subgroups $1\times V$ for open $V\leq\Gamma$ are cofinal among the open subgroups of $G$. Their quotient rings are $\mathbb Z_p[\Delta]\otimes_{\mathbb Z_p}\mathbb Z_p[\Gamma/V]$. Since $\mathbb Z_p[\Delta]$ is finite free, the inverse limit in [F1] is $\mathbb Z_p[\Delta]\otimes_{\mathbb Z_p}\mathbb Z_p[\![T]\!]$, the formal power-series algebra in $T=[\gamma]-[1]$. Every $f\in\Lambda(G)$ has a unique expansion $f=a_0+Th(T)$ with $a_0\in\mathbb Z_p[\Delta]$. If $\epsilon(f)=0$, then the augmentation of $a_0$ in $\mathbb Z_p$ is zero. Writing $a_0=\sum_{d\in\Delta}a_d[d]$ with $\sum_da_d=0$ gives $a_0=\sum_da_d([d]-[1])$. Each $[d]-[1]$ lies in the ideal generated by $[d_1]-[1],\ldots,[d_r]-[1]$: express $d$ as a word in the generators and their inverses, use $[xy]-[1]=[x]([y]-[1])+([x]-[1])$, and use $[d_j^{-1}]-[1]=-[d_j^{-1}]([d_j]-[1])$. These generators have augmentation zero, so $I(G)=(T,[d_1]-[1],\ldots,[d_r]-[1])$. [F1, given, choose, algebra]

2.1 By [F2], $\mu T=\mu([\gamma]-[1])$ and each $\mu([d_j]-[1])$ belongs to $\Lambda(G)$. [F2, step 1.1]

3.1 Every $x\in I(G)$ is a finite sum $Ta+\sum_{j=1}^r([d_j]-[1])b_j$ with $a,b_j\in\Lambda(G)$ by step 1.1. Therefore $\mu x=(\mu T)a+\sum_j(\mu([d_j]-[1]))b_j$ belongs to $\Lambda(G)$ by step 2.1. Since $I(G)$ is an ideal and $\Lambda(G)$ is commutative, $\mu I(G)$ is closed under addition and multiplication by every element of $\Lambda(G)$; thus it is an ideal. [F1, step 1.1, step 2.1, algebra] ∎
