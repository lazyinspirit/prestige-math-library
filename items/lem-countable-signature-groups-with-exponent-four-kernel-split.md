## Statement

Assume full AC. Let $G$ be a countable abelian group and $s:G\to\bigoplus_{n\ge1}\mathbb Z$ a homomorphism whose kernel $K$ satisfies $4K=0$. Then $K$ is precisely the torsion subgroup of $G$, and for cardinals $r,u,v\le\aleph_0$,
$$G\cong\mathbb Z^{(r)}\oplus(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^{(u)}\oplus(\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z)^{(v)}.$$
If $G$ contains elements $f_i,p_i,q_i$, indexed by positive integers, such that the $f_i$ are integer-linearly independent, the order-two elements $p_i$ have linearly independent images in $G/2G$, and the $2q_i$ are linearly independent order-two elements with $4q_i=0$, then $r=u=v=\aleph_0$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The countable group, homomorphism, and kernel in the Statement; all direct sums consist of finitely supported tuples.

[A1] Full AC is available for vector-space bases and their lifts. [[def-axiom-of-choice]]

## Proof

1.1 The target is torsion-free, since multiplication by a nonzero integer is injective in each integer coordinate. Thus every torsion element of $G$ belongs to $K$. Conversely $4K=0$ makes every element of $K$ torsion, proving $K=\operatorname{Tor}(G)$. Put $H=s(G)$ and $H_n=H\cap\bigoplus_{j=1}^n\mathbb Z$, with $H_0=0$. Projection of $H_n$ to its last coordinate has kernel $H_{n-1}$ and image either zero or $d_n\mathbb Z$ for a positive integer $d_n$, the least positive element of a nonzero subgroup of $\mathbb Z$. In the latter case choose $h_n\in H_n$ with last coordinate $d_n$. Subtracting the unique appropriate multiple of $h_n$ gives $H_n=H_{n-1}\oplus\mathbb Z h_n$; in the zero case $H_n=H_{n-1}$. Every element of $H$ has finite support, so the selected $h_n$ form a basis of $H$. In particular $H$ is free of at most countable rank. [given, A1, construct, algebra]

2.1 Lift each basis element of $H$ to an element of $G$, using [A1], and extend these lifts by finite integer linear combination to $j:H\to G$. Then $s j=\operatorname{id}_H$. For $g\in G$, $g-j(sg)\in K$, and $K\cap j(H)=0$ by applying $s$. Hence $G=K\oplus j(H)$. No assertion about a splitting of an arbitrary torsion-free quotient is being used: the preceding step supplied an actual free basis of this quotient. [A1, step 1.1, construct, algebra]

2.2 The groups $2K$ and $K[2]=\{k\in K:2k=0\}$ are vector spaces over $\mathbb F_2$, with $2K\subseteq K[2]$. Choose a basis $(b_i)_{i\in I}$ of $2K$, lifts $x_i\in K$ with $2x_i=b_i$, and a basis of $K[2]/2K$ represented by $y_j\in K[2]$, $j\in J$. All choices are licensed by [A1]. Every $x_i$ has order four. If a finite relation $\sum a_i x_i+\sum c_j y_j=0$ holds, with $0\le a_i<4$ and $0\le c_j<2$, doubling it first forces each $a_i$ even by independence of the $b_i$. Reduction in $K[2]/2K$ then forces all $c_j=0$, and the remaining relation $\sum(a_i/2)b_i=0$ forces all $a_i=0$. Conversely, for $k\in K$ express $2k$ as a finite sum of the $b_i$ and subtract the corresponding sum of $x_i$. The remainder belongs to $K[2]$; its quotient class is a finite sum of the $y_j$, and after subtracting those it lies in $2K$, generated by the $2x_i$. Thus these elements give $K\cong(\mathbb Z/4)^{(I)}\oplus(\mathbb Z/2)^{(J)}$. [A1, step 1.1, construct, algebra]

3.1 Because $G$ is countable, each linearly independent family in its subgroups or quotients has at most countably many members: a quotient is countable by mapping an enumeration of $G$ onto it, and distinct family members are distinct nonzero elements. The bases in steps 1.1 and 2.2 therefore have cardinalities at most $\aleph_0$. Combining steps 2.1 and 2.2 proves the displayed decomposition with $r=\operatorname{rank}H$, $u=\dim_{\mathbb F_2}K[2]/2K$, and $v=\dim_{\mathbb F_2}2K$. [step 1.1, step 2.1, step 2.2, algebra]

4.1 Under the additional hypotheses, the images $s(f_i)$ are integer-linearly independent: a relation would put an integer combination in $K$, and multiplication by four would give a relation among the $f_i$, forcing every coefficient to vanish. Hence $r$ is infinite. Each $p_i$ belongs to $K[2]$, and independence of their images in $G/2G$ implies independence in $K[2]/2K$, since $2K\subseteq2G$; thus $u$ is infinite. Finally $q_i\in K$ by $4q_i=0$, and the given independent $2q_i\in2K$ make $v$ infinite. The upper bounds in step 3.1 make all three cardinals $\aleph_0$, proving the final assertion. [step 1.1, step 3.1, algebra] ∎
