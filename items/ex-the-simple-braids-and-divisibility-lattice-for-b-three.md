## Example

Take $n=3$, so that $B_3^{+}$ has the two atoms $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ and the
half twist $\Delta=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_1$ of length $N=3$. The example
verifies:

1. the six simple braids of $B_3$ are
   $1,\ \sigma_1,\ \sigma_2,\ \sigma_1\sigma_2,\ \sigma_2\sigma_1,\ \Delta$,
   with the six distinct endpoint permutations
   $\mathrm{id},(1\,2),(2\,3),(1\,2\,3),(1\,3\,2),(1\,3)$;
2. $\sigma_1\wedge_L\sigma_2=1$ while
   $\sigma_1\vee_L\sigma_2=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_1=\Delta$;
3. for $a:=\sigma_1\sigma_2$ and $b:=\sigma_1$ one has
   $a\wedge_Lb=\sigma_1$ but $a\wedge_Rb=1$;
4. consequently the left and the right divisibility orders on $B_3^{+}$ are
   different, even though by
   [[lem-simple-positive-braids-are-indexed-by-permutations]] the two orders
   have the same divisor set on $\Delta$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The natural number $3$, the monoid $B_3^{+}$ with atoms $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$, half twist $\Delta=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_1$ of length $3$, and the elements $a=\sigma_1\sigma_2$, $b=\sigma_1$.

[F1] $B_3^{+}$ is generated by the atoms, $\ell([w])=|w|$ is additive and $\ell(x)=0$ only for $x=1$; the orders are $x\preccurlyeq_Ly\iff y=xc$ and $x\preccurlyeq_Ry\iff y=cx$ for some $c\in B_3^{+}$, with length monotone, so a proper divisor of an atom or of $1$ or $2$ letters has strictly smaller length ([[def-positive-braid-monoid]], [[lem-positive-artin-relations-preserve-homogeneous-length]], [[def-left-and-right-divisibility-for-positive-braids]]).

[F2] $\Delta=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_1=\sigma_2\sigma_1\sigma_2$ by the braid relation, and the simple braids — the left divisors of $\Delta$ — are in bijection with $S_3$: they are exactly $\widehat{\mathrm{id}}=1$, $\widehat{s_1}=\sigma_1$, $\widehat{s_2}=\sigma_2$, $\widehat{s_1s_2}=\sigma_1\sigma_2$, $\widehat{s_2s_1}=\sigma_2\sigma_1$, $\widehat{w_0}=\Delta$ ([[lem-simple-positive-braids-are-indexed-by-permutations]], [[def-garside-half-twist-and-simple-positive-braid]]).

[F3] The homomorphism $\pi\colon B_3^{+}\to S_3$ sends $\sigma_i$ to $s_i$ and products to products; $s_1=(1\,2)$, $s_2=(2\,3)$, $s_1s_2=(1\,2\,3)$, $s_2s_1=(1\,3\,2)$, $s_1s_2s_1=(1\,3)$ in the composition convention of [[def-symmetric-group]] ([[lem-reduced-adjacent-transposition-words-have-well-defined-positive-lifts]]).

[F4] For adjacent atoms, $\sigma_1\vee_L\sigma_2=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_1$ is the least common left multiple and also the least common right multiple, and $\sigma_1\wedge_L\sigma_2$ exists and is unique; more generally every nonempty finite subset of $B_3^{+}$ has unique left and right gcds and lcms ([[lem-artin-atoms-have-explicit-left-and-right-lcms-and-complements]], [[thm-positive-braids-have-left-and-right-gcds-and-lcms]]).

## Verification

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 **The six simple braids and their permutations.** By [F2] there are exactly $3!=6$ simple braids. Applying [F3] to the six displayed elements gives the images $\mathrm{id}$, $s_1=(1\,2)$, $s_2=(2\,3)$, $s_1s_2=(1\,2\,3)$, $s_2s_1=(1\,3\,2)$ and $s_1s_2s_1=(1\,3)$ in this order; these are the six elements of $S_3$, so they are pairwise distinct, and since $\pi$ is a function the six braids $1,\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_1\sigma_2,\sigma_2\sigma_1,\Delta$ are pairwise distinct and exhaust the left divisors of $\Delta$. In particular $\sigma_1\sigma_2$ and $\sigma_2\sigma_1$ are proper simple braids, because $\sigma_1\sigma_2\cdot\sigma_1=\Delta$ and $\sigma_2\sigma_1\cdot\sigma_2=\Delta$ by [F2]. [F2, F3]

1.2 **The atom pair $(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)$.** The left divisors of $\sigma_1$ are $1$ and $\sigma_1$, since a divisor $d$ of $\sigma_1$ has $\ell(d)\le1$, so either $d=1$ or $d$ is an atom equal to $\sigma_1$; the same holds for $\sigma_2$, and the distinct atoms are incomparable, so the common left divisors of $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$ are just $1$: thus $\sigma_1\wedge_L\sigma_2=1$. Their least common left multiple is $\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_1$ by [F4], which is $\Delta$ by [F2], so $\sigma_1\vee_L\sigma_2=\Delta$. [F1, F2, F4]

1.3 **A pair with different left and right meets.** For $a=\sigma_1\sigma_2$ and $b=\sigma_1$: $\sigma_1$ left-divides $b$ trivially and left-divides $a=\sigma_1\sigma_2$, so $\sigma_1$ is a common left divisor; every common left divisor $d$ of $a$ and $b$ divides $b=\sigma_1$, hence is $1$ or $\sigma_1$, so $d\preccurlyeq_L\sigma_1$: therefore $a\wedge_Lb=\sigma_1$. On the right: the right divisors of $b=\sigma_1$ are $1$ and $\sigma_1$ by [F1], while the right divisors of $a=\sigma_1\sigma_2$ are $1,\sigma_2,\sigma_1\sigma_2$ — indeed if $a=dc$ then $\ell(c)\le2$; for $\ell(c)=2$ one has $\ell(d)=0$, so $d=1$ and $c=a$; for $\ell(c)=1$ the element $c$ is an atom, and $d$ then has length $1$ and is an atom as well, so $a$ must be a product of two atoms, the four candidates being $\sigma_1\sigma_1,\sigma_1\sigma_2,\sigma_2\sigma_1,\sigma_2\sigma_2$, which are distinct because every defining relation has length three; only $\sigma_1\sigma_2=a$ occurs, so the only one-letter right divisor is $c=\sigma_2$. Hence the common right divisors of $a$ and $b$ are $\{1,\sigma_2,\sigma_1\sigma_2\}\cap\{1,\sigma_1\}=\{1\}$, and $a\wedge_Rb=1$. [F1, F2, F4]

1.4 **Conclusion.** Steps 1.1--1.3 give the six simple braids, $\sigma_1\wedge_L\sigma_2=1$, $\sigma_1\vee_L\sigma_2=\Delta$, and the pair $a,b$ with $a\wedge_Lb=\sigma_1\ne1=a\wedge_Rb$. So already on the four-element subfamily $\{1,\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_1\sigma_2\}$ the two orders have different meets, even though on $\Delta$ itself the left and right divisor sets coincide by [F2]; the balancedness established in
[[lem-delta-is-the-lcm-of-the-artin-atoms-and-has-the-same-left-and-right-divisors]]
is therefore a property of $\Delta$ and does not identify the two orders. No choice principle is used. ∎ [step 1.1, step 1.2, step 1.3]

## Remarks

- **Reading the six permutations.** In the convention of
  [[def-symmetric-group]] the product acts with the right factor first, so
  $\sigma_1\sigma_2$ maps to $s_1s_2=(1\,2\,3)$ and $\sigma_2\sigma_1$ to the
  inverse cycle $(1\,3\,2)$: the order of the two words is visible in the
  orientation of the $3$-cycle, and this is what separates the two length-two
  simple braids.
- **Why the right meet is the smaller one here.** The right divisors of
  $\sigma_1\sigma_2$ are its suffixes $1,\sigma_2,\sigma_1\sigma_2$, while
  those of $\sigma_1$ are $1,\sigma_1$; the overlap is trivial even though the
  overlap of the corresponding prefix sets is $\{1,\sigma_1\}$. This is the
  smallest instance of the asymmetry between the two orders.
- No choice principle is used; all computations are finite word computations.
