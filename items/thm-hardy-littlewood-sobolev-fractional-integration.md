## Statement

Assume the Axiom of Countable Choice ([[def-countable-choice]]). Let $n\ge1$,
$0<\alpha<n$ and $1<p<n/\alpha$, and set
$$1/q:=1/p-\alpha/n .$$
Then $q$ is finite and $q>p$. For every $f$ in the complex Lebesgue space
$L^p(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)$ the unit-normalized Riesz integral $I_\alpha f$ of
[[def-riesz-potential-of-order-alpha]] exists absolutely for almost every
$x\in\mathbb R^n$, and the resulting almost-everywhere defined function
determines an element of $L^q(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)$. The assignment is
independent of the measurable representative of the class and defines a
bounded linear map
$$I_\alpha:L^p(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)\to L^q(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C),\qquad \|I_\alpha f\|_q\le C_{n,\alpha,p}\|f\|_p,$$
with a constant depending only on $n$, $\alpha$ and $p$. On the dense subspace
$C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)$ the map is the pointwise integral
$x\mapsto\int K_\alpha(x-y)f(y)\,dy$, which is finite at every point there, and
its unique bounded dense-core extension is this same almost-everywhere integral
operator.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Countable Choice, $n\ge1$, $0<\alpha<n$, $1<p<n/\alpha$, the exponent $q$ with $1/q=1/p-\alpha/n$, and a class $f\in L^p(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)$ with a fixed measurable representative, again written $f$.

[F1] The unit Riesz potential is $I_\alpha f(x)=\int K_\alpha(x-y)f(y)\,dy$ at exactly those points where $\int K_\alpha(x-y)|f(y)|\,dy<\infty$, with $K_\alpha(z)=|z|^{\alpha-n}$ for $z\neq0$ and $K_\alpha(0)=0$; where the absolute integral is infinite no value is assigned. ([[def-riesz-potential-of-order-alpha]])

[F2] Complex $L^p$ classes are quotients by almost-everywhere equality carrying the well-defined norm $\|g\|_p=(\int|g|^p)^{1/p}$ and the complex vector operations; a complex function is measurable when its real and imaginary parts are; integration is componentwise, with $\int u=\int u^+-\int u^-$ for integrable real $u$; $C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)$ consists of bounded measurable functions; local integrability means finite absolute integral over every Euclidean ball. ([[def-complex-lp-and-euclidean-test-function-conventions]], [[thm-complex-holder-minkowski-and-the-quotient-norm]], [[def-integrable-real-and-complex-functions-and-their-integrals]], [[def-locally-integrable-function-on-r-n]])

[F3] Near/far splitting: for $f\in L^p$ with $1<p<n/\alpha$ every measurable representative is locally integrable, and at every $x$ with $Mf(x)<\infty$ and every $R>0$ the integrals $N_R(x)=\int_{|x-y|<R}K_\alpha(x-y)|f(y)|\,dy$ and $F_R(x)=\int_{|x-y|\ge R}K_\alpha(x-y)|f(y)|\,dy$ are finite with $N_R(x)\le C_{n,\alpha}R^{\alpha}Mf(x)$ and $F_R(x)\le C_{n,\alpha,p}R^{\alpha-n/p}\|f\|_p$; the far bound holds at every $x$; and if two representatives agree almost everywhere then, at every $x$ and every $R>0$, their integrals $N_R$ and $F_R$ coincide, and the total potential is defined on the common finite set and agrees for the two representatives. ([[lem-riesz-potential-near-far-splitting]])

[F4] Hedberg's pointwise inequality: with $\theta=\alpha p/n\in(0,1)$, at every $x$ with $Mf(x)<\infty$ the defining integral of $I_\alpha f$ converges absolutely and $\bigl|I_\alpha f(x)\bigr|\le C_{n,\alpha,p}\bigl(Mf(x)\bigr)^{1-\theta}\|f\|_p^{\theta}$. ([[lem-hedberg-pointwise-inequality]])

[F5] The centered maximal function is $Mf(x)=M(|f|)(x)=\sup_{r>0}\lambda(B(x,r))^{-1}\int_{B(x,r)}|f|$ with values in $[0,\infty]$; for $1<p<\infty$ there is $C_{n,p}$ with $\|Mh\|_p\le C_{n,p}\|h\|_p$ for every real $h\in L^p(\mathbb R^n)$; and $Mh$ is Borel measurable whenever $h\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb R^n)$. ([[def-centered-and-uncentered-hardy-littlewood-maximal-functions]], [[cor-centered-hardy-littlewood-maximal-operator-is-l-p-bounded]], [[thm-centered-hardy-littlewood-maximal-function-is-borel-measurable]])

[F6] A nonnegative measurable function with finite integral is finite almost everywhere. ([[cor-finite-nonnegative-integral-implies-finite-almost-everywhere]])

[F7] Extended-real measurability is equivalent to measurability of all strict superlevel sets: $h$ is measurable exactly when $\{h>a\}$ is measurable for every real $a$. ([[thm-threshold-characterisations-of-real-and-extended-real-measurability]])

[F8] Tonelli: for sigma-finite measure spaces $(X,\mathcal A,\mu)$ and $(Y,\mathcal B,\nu)$ and a product-measurable $H:X\times Y\to[0,\infty]$, the partial integral $x\mapsto\int_YH(x,y)\,d\nu(y)$ is measurable and the three iterated integrals agree. Euclidean Lebesgue measure on $\mathcal L(\mathbb R^n)$ is sigma-finite, and every bounded measurable set has finite measure. ([[thm-tonelli-theorem-for-sigma-finite-product-spaces]], [[prop-lebesgue-measure-is-sigma-finite-and-finite-on-bounded-sets]])

[F9] Product measurability toolkit: $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n)\otimes\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n)=\mathcal B(\mathbb R^{2n})$ under the usual identification; every Borel subset of $\mathbb R^{2n}$ is Lebesgue measurable and $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n)\subseteq\mathcal L(\mathbb R^n)$, so $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n)\otimes\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n)\subseteq\mathcal L(\mathbb R^n)\otimes\mathcal L(\mathbb R^n)$; continuous Euclidean maps are Borel; the composition of measurable maps is measurable, and composition of a measurable map with a Borel map on its codomain preserves measurability; coordinate projections are measurable; sums, products, scalar multiples, absolute values and positive and negative parts of measurable extended-real functions are measurable. ([[thm-borel-products-of-euclidean-spaces-are-euclidean-borel]], [[thm-borel-sets-are-lebesgue-measurable]], [[cor-continuous-functions-are-borel-measurable]], [[thm-composition-with-borel-functions-preserves-measurability]], [[thm-arithmetic-and-lattice-operations-preserve-measurability]], [[def-borel-and-lebesgue-measurable-function-on-rn]], [[def-measurable-function-between-measurable-spaces]])

[F10] Integral rules: the Lebesgue integral is complex-linear on $L^1$; the nonnegative integral is monotone and additive; a nonnegative measurable function has integral zero exactly when it vanishes almost everywhere; integrable functions equal almost everywhere have equal integrals over every measurable set; a nonnegative measurable function has zero integral over every null set; and finite unions of null sets are null. ([[thm-linearity-of-the-lebesgue-integral-on-l-one]], [[prop-order-and-scalar-rules-for-the-nonnegative-integral]], [[cor-additivity-of-the-nonnegative-lebesgue-integral]], [[thm-the-lebesgue-integral-respects-almost-everywhere-equality]], [[cor-integral-over-a-null-set-vanishes]], [[thm-finite-and-countable-subadditivity-of-measures]])

[F11] Density and completeness: under Countable Choice $C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)$ is dense in the Euclidean Lebesgue space $L^p$ for $1\le p<\infty$, and $L^r(\mu;\mathbb C)$ is complete for every $1\le r\le\infty$, so $L^q$ is a Banach space for its quotient norm. ([[thm-complex-finite-simple-and-smooth-compact-support-density-for-finite-p]], [[thm-complex-lp-completeness-and-almost-everywhere-subsequences]], [[def-banach-space]])

[F12] Extension and continuity: a bounded linear map $T:D\to Y$ on a dense normed subspace $D$ of a normed space $X$ with Banach target $Y$ has a unique bounded linear extension $\widetilde T:X\to Y$ with $\|\widetilde T\|=\|T\|$; bounded linear maps are continuous; limits of convergent sequences in a metric space are unique. ([[thm-extension-of-a-bounded-map-from-a-dense-subspace]], [[def-normed-subspace]], [[def-metric-interior-closure-boundary]], [[thm-bounded-linear-operator-equivalences]], [[lem-metric-limits-unique]])

[F13] Countable Choice is the choice principle assumed by the maximal-function, Tonelli, density, completeness and extension interfaces used below. ([[def-countable-choice]])

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct; the exponent algebra fixes $q$; Tonelli makes the integral measurable; the maximal bound makes $Mf$ finite almost everywhere and Hedberg converts that into almost-everywhere absolute convergence and an $L^q$ estimate; classes, linearity and the dense smooth core are then handled separately.

1.1 The exponents. Put $\theta:=\alpha p/n$, which lies in $(0,1)$ because $0<\alpha p<n$. The exponent $q$ of the statement satisfies $1/q=1/p-\alpha/n=(n-\alpha p)/(pn)=(1-\theta)/p$, so $q=p/(1-\theta)$; hence $q<\infty$, $q>p$, and $(1-\theta)q=p$, while $\theta q=\alpha p q/n=q-p$. [given, algebra]

1.2 Joint product measurability. Identify $\mathbb R^n\times\mathbb R^n$ with $\mathbb R^{2n}$; by [F9] its Borel sigma-algebra is $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n)\otimes\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n)$, which is contained in $\mathcal L(\mathbb R^n)\otimes\mathcal L(\mathbb R^n)$. The difference map $(x,y)\mapsto x-y$ is continuous, hence Borel, so its composition with the Borel function $K_\alpha$ is Borel by [F9] and therefore product measurable. The second coordinate projection is measurable into the Lebesgue sigma-algebra since the inverse image of every Lebesgue set $A$ is $\mathbb R^n\times A$; composing it with measurable $f$ makes $(x,y)\mapsto f(y)$ product measurable. The lattice and product clauses of [F9] then make $(x,y)\mapsto|f(y)|$ and $(x,y)\mapsto(\operatorname{Re}f)^\pm(y)$, $(\operatorname{Im}f)^\pm(y)$ product measurable. Products of measurable finite-valued functions are measurable by [F9], so the five functions $(x,y)\mapsto K_\alpha(x-y)|f(y)|$ and $K_\alpha(x-y)(\operatorname{Re}f)^\pm(y)$, $K_\alpha(x-y)(\operatorname{Im}f)^\pm(y)$ are product measurable and take values in $[0,\infty)$. [F2, F9]

1.3 The maximal function is finite almost everywhere. The function $|f|$ is real, measurable and lies in $L^p$, so the maximal bound of [F5] applied to $|f|$ gives $\|Mf\|_p=\|M(|f|)\|_p\le C_{n,p}\||f|\|_p=C_{n,p}\|f\|_p<\infty$. The power $(Mf)^p$ is nonnegative and measurable by the threshold criterion [F7]: for $a<0$ its superlevel set is all of $\mathbb R^n$, and for $a\ge0$ it equals $\{Mf>a^{1/p}\}$. Applying [F6] to $(Mf)^p$ gives $(Mf)^p<\infty$ almost everywhere, that is, $Mf<\infty$ almost everywhere. [F5, F6, F7, given, algebra]

2.1 Tonelli and measurability of the integral. By [F8] the measure space $(\mathbb R^n,\mathcal L(\mathbb R^n),\lambda_n)$ is sigma-finite, so Tonelli applies to each product-measurable function of step 1.2: the functions $$G(x):=\int K_\alpha(x-y)|f(y)|\,dy,\qquad G_1(x):=\int K_\alpha(x-y)(\operatorname{Re}f)^+(y)\,dy,\quad\ldots,\quad G_4(x):=\int K_\alpha(x-y)(\operatorname{Im}f)^-(y)\,dy$$ are measurable $[0,\infty]$-valued functions of $x$. The set $E:=\{G<\infty\}=\bigcup_{m\ge1}\{G\le m\}$ is measurable, and $0\le G_j\le G$ on all of $\mathbb R^n$ for $j=1,\ldots,4$; hence on $E$ the four numbers $G_j(x)$ are finite and $$h(x):=\bigl(G_1(x)-G_2(x)\bigr)+i\bigl(G_3(x)-G_4(x)\bigr)$$ is a well-defined complex number. Since each $G_j$ is measurable and $E$ is measurable, the product $\widetilde I_\alpha f:=h\cdot1_E$ (with value $0$ off $E$) is a measurable complex-valued function; and at every $x\in E$ it equals $\int K_\alpha(x-y)f(y)\,dy$ by componentwise integration [F2] and the definition [F1], because $K_\alpha(x-\cdot)|f|$ has finite integral there. [F1, F2, F8, step 1.2, algebra]

3.1 Almost-everywhere absolute convergence and the pointwise bound. By [F3] every representative of $f$ is locally integrable, so $Mf$ is defined everywhere; by the Hedberg inequality [F4], at every $x$ with $Mf(x)<\infty$ the defining integral converges absolutely and $\bigl|I_\alpha f(x)\bigr|\le C^{H}_{n,\alpha,p}(Mf(x))^{1-\theta}\|f\|_p^{\theta}$, where $C^{H}_{n,\alpha,p}$ denotes the constant of [F4], renamed to avoid a clash with the constant claimed in the Statement. Let $S:=\{Mf<\infty\}$; step 1.3 makes $S$ conull, and $S\subseteq E$ by the definition of $E$ in step 2.1. Hence $\widetilde I_\alpha f=h\cdot1_E$ agrees with the pointwise potential $I_\alpha f$ of [F1] at every point of $S$ and differs from it only on the null set $\mathbb R^n\setminus S$; in particular the defining integral converges absolutely almost everywhere, and $\widetilde I_\alpha f$ is a measurable representative of the almost-everywhere defined integral. At every point of $S$ the displayed inequality is exactly the Hedberg bound. At a point with $Mf(x)=+\infty$: if $\|f\|_p>0$ then the right-hand side is $+\infty$, because $1-\theta\in(0,1)$ and $\theta\in(0,1)$ are applied to $+\infty$ and to the strictly positive number $\|f\|_p$, so the inequality holds trivially against the finite value $|\widetilde I_\alpha f(x)|$; and if $\|f\|_p=0$ then $f$ is the zero class with $Mf=0$ everywhere by [F4], so the case $Mf(x)=+\infty$ cannot occur. In every case the pointwise bound holds at every $x\in\mathbb R^n$ in the form $$|\widetilde I_\alpha f(x)|\le C^{H}_{n,\alpha,p}(Mf(x))^{1-\theta}\|f\|_p^{\theta}.$$ [F1, F3, F4, step 1.3, step 2.1]

4.1 The $L^q$ estimate. Raising the bound of step 3.1 to the $q$-th power and using $(1-\theta)q=p$ from step 1.1 gives, at every $x$, $$|\widetilde I_\alpha f(x)|^{q}\le \bigl(C^{H}_{n,\alpha,p}\bigr)^{q}(Mf(x))^{p}\|f\|_p^{\theta q},$$ an inequality between nonnegative measurable functions. Monotonicity of the integral [F10], the identity $\|Mf\|_p^p=\int(Mf)^p$, and the maximal bound of step 1.3 give $$\|\widetilde I_\alpha f\|_q^{q}=\int|\widetilde I_\alpha f|^{q}\le\bigl(C^{H}_{n,\alpha,p}\bigr)^{q}\|f\|_p^{\theta q}\|Mf\|_p^{p}\le\bigl(C^{H}_{n,\alpha,p}\bigr)^{q}C_{n,p}^{p}\|f\|_p^{\theta q+p}=\bigl(C^{H}_{n,\alpha,p}\bigr)^{q}C_{n,p}^{p}\|f\|_p^{q},$$ where $\theta q+p=q$ by step 1.1. Hence $\|\widetilde I_\alpha f\|_q\le C_{n,\alpha,p}\|f\|_p$ with $C_{n,\alpha,p}:=C^{H}_{n,\alpha,p}C_{n,p}^{p/q}=C^{H}_{n,\alpha,p}C_{n,p}^{1-\theta}<\infty$, a constant depending only on $n,\alpha,p$. [F10, step 1.1, step 1.3, step 3.1, algebra]

5.1 Representative independence. Let $f$ and $g$ be measurable representatives of the same class, so that $f=g$ almost everywhere; then $|f|=|g|$ almost everywhere, and both $|f|1_{B}$ and $|g|1_{B}$ are integrable for every ball $B$ because the representatives are locally integrable by [F3] and balls have finite measure, so the almost-everywhere-equality clause of [F10] gives $\int_B|f|=\int_B|g|$ for every ball $B$. Hence $Mf=Mg$ as extended-real functions and the two conull sets coincide: writing $S:=\{Mf<\infty\}=\{Mg<\infty\}$, step 1.3 makes $S$ conull. At every $x\in S$ and every $R>0$ the splitting lemma [F3] gives that $N_R$ and $F_R$ are finite for each of the two representatives, and its representative-independence clause gives that the total potential is defined at $x$ and takes the same value for $f$ and for $g$; thus $I_\alpha f(x)=I_\alpha g(x)$ for every $x\in S$. By step 3.1 both $\widetilde I_\alpha f$ and $\widetilde I_\alpha g$ agree with these potentials at every point of $S$; since $S$ is conull, the two measurable functions of step 2.1 define the same class in $L^q$. Therefore the class $[\widetilde I_\alpha f]\in L^q$ depends only on the class $[f]\in L^p$, and step 4.1 gives the bound $\|\widetilde I_\alpha[f]\|_q\le C_{n,\alpha,p}\|[f]\|_p$ for this well-defined assignment. [F3, F10, step 1.3, step 2.1, step 3.1, step 4.1]

6.1 Linearity. Let $f,g$ be measurable representatives of classes in $L^p$ and let $c\in\mathbb C$; then $f+g$ and $cf$ are measurable representatives of the corresponding classes. At every point $x$ of $S_f\cap S_g\cap S_{f+g}\cap S_{cf}$, all of the complex functions $K_\alpha(x-\cdot)f$, $K_\alpha(x-\cdot)g$, $K_\alpha(x-\cdot)(f+g)$ and $K_\alpha(x-\cdot)(cf)$ are integrable, and the linearity of the Lebesgue integral on $L^1$ [F10] gives $$\int K_\alpha(x-y)(f+g)(y)\,dy=\int K_\alpha(x-y)f(y)\,dy+\int K_\alpha(x-y)g(y)\,dy$$ and $\int K_\alpha(x-y)(cf)(y)\,dy=c\int K_\alpha(x-y)f(y)\,dy$. The four sets $S_f,S_g,S_{f+g},S_{cf}$ are conull by step 1.3 and their intersection is conull by the null-union clause of [F10]; on that intersection, where each $\widetilde I_\alpha$ is the corresponding integral by step 3.1, the a.e.-equal functions $\widetilde I_\alpha(f+g)$ and $\widetilde I_\alpha f+\widetilde I_\alpha g$ define the same class in $L^q$, and likewise $\widetilde I_\alpha(cf)$ and $c\,\widetilde I_\alpha f$. So the assignment of step 5.1 is complex-linear. [F10, step 1.3, step 3.1, step 5.1, algebra]

7.1 The smooth core. Let $g\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)$. Then $|g|\le\|g\|_\infty<\infty$ everywhere, so every ball average of $|g|$ is at most $\|g\|_\infty$ and $Mg(x)\le\|g\|_\infty$ for every $x$; also $g\in L^p$ because it is bounded and supported in a bounded measurable set of finite measure by [F8]. By the near/far bounds [F3] the near and far integrals of $g$ are finite at every $x$, so the defining absolute integral is finite everywhere and [F1] defines $I_\alpha g$ everywhere as the pointwise integral $x\mapsto\int K_\alpha(x-y)g(y)\,dy$. Hence $S=\mathbb R^n$ and $E=\mathbb R^n$, and $\widetilde I_\alpha g$ equals this pointwise integral at every point. Let $D\subseteq L^p(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)$ be the image of $C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)$; the assignment of steps 4.1, 5.1 and 6.1 restricts to a bounded linear map $T:D\to L^q$ which is exactly the pointwise-integral map, with $\|T\|$ at most the constant of step 4.1. [F1, F2, F3, F8, step 4.1, step 5.1, step 6.1]

8.1 Density and the abstract extension. By [F11] the subspace $D$ is dense in the normed space $L^p$ and $L^q$ is complete, hence a Banach space; the map $T$ of step 7.1 is bounded and linear. The extension theorem [F12] therefore produces a unique bounded linear map $\widetilde T:L^p(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)\to L^q(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb C)$ with $\widetilde T|_D=T$ and $\|\widetilde T\|=\|T\|$. [F11, F12, step 7.1]

9.1 Identification of the extension with the integral operator. Let $\mathcal I:L^p\to L^q$ denote the bounded linear almost-everywhere integral map of steps 4.1, 5.1 and 6.1. Let $x\in L^p$. By density [F11] there are $d_k\in D$ with $\|d_k-x\|_p\to0$. Both $\mathcal I$ and $\widetilde T$ are bounded linear, hence continuous on the normed space $L^p$ by [F12], and they agree on $D$ because $\mathcal I|_{D}=T=\widetilde T|_{D}$ by steps 7.1 and 8.1. Therefore $$\mathcal I(x)=\lim_k\mathcal I(d_k)=\lim_k T(d_k)=\lim_k\widetilde T(d_k)=\widetilde T(x),$$ the two outer equalities by continuity and the middle one because $d_k\in D$; limits in the normed space $L^q$ are unique by [F12]. Hence the unique bounded dense-core extension of the pointwise-integral map on $C_c^\infty$ is precisely the almost-everywhere integral operator $\mathcal I$, and it satisfies the bound $\|\mathcal I(f)\|_q\le C_{n,\alpha,p}\|f\|_p$ of step 4.1. [F11, F12, step 4.1, step 7.1, step 8.1, algebra]

10.1 Conclusion. For $1<p<n/\alpha$ and the exponent $q$ of the statement, steps 1.1, 4.1, 5.1 and 6.1 prove that the defining integral of $I_\alpha f$ converges absolutely almost everywhere for every $f\in L^p$ and that its class obeys the $L^q$ bound with a constant depending only on $n,\alpha,p$, and that this gives a well-defined bounded linear map on the quotient classes; and steps 7.1, 8.1 and 9.1 prove that on the dense smooth core the map is the pointwise integral and that its unique bounded dense-core extension is this same almost-everywhere integral operator. Countable Choice is spent exactly through the maximal-function, Tonelli and sigma-finiteness, density, completeness and extension interfaces [F5], [F8], [F11], [F12]; no full Axiom of Choice is invoked. [F13, step 1.1, step 4.1, step 5.1, step 6.1, step 7.1, step 8.1, step 9.1] ∎
