## Statement

Let $(X,\mathcal A)$ and $(Y,\mathcal B)$ be measurable spaces. The family of
finite disjoint unions of measurable rectangles in $X \times Y$ is an algebra
of subsets of $X \times Y$, and it generates $\mathcal A \otimes \mathcal B$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Measurable spaces $(X,\mathcal A)$ and $(Y,\mathcal B)$.

[L1] A measurable rectangle has the form $A \times B$ with $A \in \mathcal A$ and $B \in \mathcal B$. ([[def-measurable-rectangle]])

[L2] The product sigma-algebra $\mathcal A \otimes \mathcal B$ is the sigma-algebra generated by the measurable rectangles. ([[def-product-sigma-algebra-and-finite-product-sigma-algebras]])

[A1] For rectangles, $$(A_1 \times B_1) \cap (A_2 \times B_2) = (A_1 \cap A_2) \times (B_1 \cap B_2),$$ and $$ (X \times Y) \setminus (A \times B) = (A^c \times Y) \cup (A \times B^c). $$

[A2] If $R_j=A_j\times B_j$ for $1\le j\le n$, take the nonempty Boolean
atoms generated by $A_1,\dots,A_n$ in $X$ and by $B_1,\dots,B_n$ in $Y$.
The products of an $X$-atom and a $Y$-atom are finitely many pairwise disjoint
measurable rectangles partitioning $X\times Y$, and each $R_j$, hence also
$\bigcup_{j=1}^nR_j$, is the union of a subfamily of these product atoms.

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 By [L1] and [A1], the intersection of two measurable rectangles is again a measurable rectangle, and the complement of a measurable rectangle is a finite union of measurable rectangles. [L1, A1]

2.1 Let $\mathcal R$ be the family of finite disjoint unions of measurable rectangles. It contains $\varnothing$ and $X \times Y$. If $E,F \in \mathcal R$, then [A2] disjointifies the finite union $E \cup F$ into finitely many pairwise disjoint measurable rectangles, so $E \cup F \in \mathcal R$. Likewise $E \setminus F = E \cap F^c$ belongs to $\mathcal R$ by step 1.1. Thus $\mathcal R$ is an algebra. [step 1.1, A2]

3.1 Every measurable rectangle belongs to $\mathcal R$, so [L2] gives $$\mathcal A \otimes \mathcal B = \sigma(\text{measurable rectangles}) \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal R).$$ The reverse inclusion holds because every member of $\mathcal R$ is a finite union of measurable rectangles and hence lies in $\mathcal A \otimes \mathcal B$. Therefore $\sigma(\mathcal R)=\mathcal A \otimes \mathcal B$, and $\mathcal R$ is an algebra generating the product sigma-algebra. [L2, step 2.1] ∎
