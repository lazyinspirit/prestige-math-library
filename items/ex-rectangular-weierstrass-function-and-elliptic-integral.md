## Example

Let $\Lambda=\mathbb Z\alpha+\mathbb Z\beta$ with $\alpha>0$ real and
$\beta=ib$ with $b>0$, put $c_1=\alpha/2$, $c_2=\beta/2$,
$c_3=(\alpha+\beta)/2$ and $e_j=\wp(c_j)$, ordered $e_2<e_3<e_1$. Then
for $z\in\mathbb C\setminus\Lambda$, $\wp(z)\in\mathbb R$ exactly when
$\operatorname{Re}z\in(\alpha/2)\mathbb Z$ or
$\operatorname{Im}z\in(b/2)\mathbb Z$. These are the horizontal and vertical
lines through $\tfrac12\Lambda$; their lattice points are poles of $\wp$, not
finite real values. The restriction to a half-period rectangle maps
conformally onto one half-plane. Every inverse branch satisfies
$dz/d\zeta=(4\zeta^3-g_2\zeta-g_3)^{-1/2}$, giving
$$\frac{\alpha}{2}=\int_{e_1}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{4x^3-g_2x-g_3}}=\int_{e_2}^{e_3}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{4x^3-g_2x-g_3}},\qquad \frac{\beta}{2i}=\int_{-\infty}^{e_2}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{g_3+g_2x-4x^3}}=\int_{e_3}^{e_1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{g_3+g_2x-4x^3}},$$
with positive real square roots. In the corresponding Jacobi example, for
$0<k<1$ put $K(a)=\int_0^1 ds/\sqrt{(1-s^2)(1-a^2s^2)}$, $K=K(k)$ and
$K'=K(\sqrt{1-k^2})$. Then
$I_k(z)=\int_0^z d\zeta/\sqrt{(1-\zeta^2)(1-k^2\zeta^2)}$ maps the upper
half-plane conformally onto the rectangle with vertices $-K,K,K+iK',-K+iK'$,
and its inverse sn extends by reflection to a doubly periodic meromorphic
function with periods $4K$ and $2iK'$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A rectangular lattice $\Lambda=\mathbb Z\alpha+\mathbb Z\beta$ with $\alpha>0$ real and $\beta=ib$, $b>0$, the half-periods $c_1=\alpha/2$, $c_2=\beta/2$, $c_3=(\alpha+\beta)/2$, the values $e_j=\wp(c_j)$, the rectangle $S=\{s\alpha/2+t\beta/2:0\le s,t\le1\}$, and a parameter $0<k<1$.

[F1] $\Lambda=\mathbb Z\alpha+\mathbb Z\beta$ is a full complex lattice with oriented basis, $T_\Lambda=\mathbb C/\Lambda$ its torus, and $\wp=\wp_\Lambda$ is the Weierstrass function $z^{-2}+\sum_{\omega\ne0}((z-\omega)^{-2}-\omega^{-2})$ of the lattice, with derivative $\wp'$; $\wp$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb C\setminus\Lambda$, even, $\Lambda$-periodic, and at every $\lambda\in\Lambda$ has a double pole with principal part $(z-\lambda)^{-2}$ and no other poles ([[def-complex-lattice-and-complex-torus]], [[def-weierstrass-elliptic-p-function]], [[thm-weierstrass-p-normal-convergence-and-periodicity]]).

[F2] $\wp'$ is odd and $\Lambda$-periodic with poles of order three exactly at the lattice points; $\wp(z)=\wp(w)$ holds if and only if $w\equiv\pm z$ modulo $\Lambda$; and the zeros of $\wp'$ are exactly the $\Lambda$-translates of $c_1,c_2,c_3$, each of order one ([[thm-weierstrass-p-normal-convergence-and-periodicity]], [[lem-weierstrass-p-degree-two-and-half-periods]]).

[F3] $(\wp')^2=4\wp^3-g_2\wp-g_3=4(\wp-e_1)(\wp-e_2)(\wp-e_3)$ with $g_2=60G_4$, $g_3=140G_6$, and $e_1,e_2,e_3$ are the three distinct roots of the cubic ([[thm-weierstrass-p-differential-equation]], [[thm-weierstrass-lattice-discriminant-is-nonzero]]); differentiating the identity gives $2\wp'\wp''=(12\wp^2-g_2)\wp'$, hence $\wp''=6\wp^2-g_2/2$ first where $\wp'\ne0$ and then at its isolated zeros by continuity.

[F4] Complex conjugation is a continuous real-field automorphism, so it commutes with sums, products, quotients and limits of convergent nets of complex numbers ([[lem-complex-conjugation-and-modulus-laws]]); $z$ is real exactly when $z=\overline z$, and $\operatorname{Re}z=(z+\overline z)/2$, $\operatorname{Im}z=(z-\overline z)/(2i)$ ([[def-complex-conjugate-real-imaginary-part-and-modulus]], [[thm-complex-numbers-are-the-real-coordinate-plane]]).

[F5] Every $z\in\mathbb C$ has a unique representation $z=s\alpha+t\beta$ with $s,t\in\mathbb R$; subtracting integer parts ([[lem-integer-part]]) gives representatives in the full-period rectangle $\{s\alpha+t\beta:0\le s,t\le1\}$ ([[def-complex-lattice-and-complex-torus]], [[thm-complex-numbers-are-the-real-coordinate-plane]]).

[F6] For $f$ meromorphic on an open $\Omega$, an admissible cycle $\Gamma$, and $f$ not identically zero on any component and nonzero on its trace, the argument principle gives $\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma f'/f=Z(f,\Gamma)-P(f,\Gamma)$ ([[thm-argument-principle-null-homologous-cycle]]). For $w$ avoided on the trace and $f-w$ not identically zero on any component, $\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma f'/(f-w)=N_w(f,\Gamma)-P(f,\Gamma)$; when $f$ is holomorphic, $P=0$ ([[cor-argument-principle-counts-preimages]]). The winding number is $n(\gamma,p)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\gamma d\zeta/(\zeta-p)$ ([[def-winding-number-closed-complex-contour]]). Positively oriented boundaries of rectangles and of truncated half-discs have index $1$ inside and $0$ outside ([[lem-index-of-graph-bounded-region-boundary]]). Endpoint-fixed homotopic rectifiable paths have equal integrals of a holomorphic function ([[thm-homotopy-invariance-of-holomorphic-line-integrals]]); applying this to $(\zeta-p)^{-1}$ proves winding invariance under homotopies avoiding $p$. If a loop's basepoint moves, insert the basepoint path and its reversal to obtain a fixed-basepoint homotopy; their integrals cancel. Uniformly close closed contours avoiding $p$ are linearly homotopic while still avoiding $p$, so have the same winding number.

[F7] A function holomorphic on a half-disc, continuous on its closure and real on its diameter extends holomorphically by complex conjugation across that diameter ([[thm-harmonic-and-holomorphic-schwarz-reflection-principles]]). Translating, rotating and rescaling the domain gives the same assertion at a straight side. At a boundary pole, apply this holomorphic result to a holomorphic reciprocal vanishing on the boundary, then invert the extension.

[F8] The upper half-plane $H$ is a complex domain; its complement in $\widehat{\mathbb C}$ is $\{\operatorname{Im}z\le0\}\cup\{\infty\}$, the closure in the sphere of the convex set $\{\operatorname{Im}z\le0\}$. That convex set is contractible, hence path-connected, hence connected ([[cor-convex-subsets-of-rn-are-contractible]], [[cor-contractible-spaces-are-path-connected]], [[thm-path-connected-implies-connected]]), and the closure of a connected set is connected, so $\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus H$ is connected ([[thm-closure-of-a-connected-set]], [[def-complex-domain]]). A complex domain whose complement in $\widehat{\mathbb C}$ is connected has every cycle null-homologous in it, i.e. is homologically simply connected ([[lem-connected-spherical-complement-implies-null-homology]], [[def-homologically-simply-connected-complex-domain]]); on such a domain every holomorphic function has a primitive, and every nowhere-zero holomorphic function has a holomorphic square root ([[thm-homological-simple-connectivity-equivalences]], [[cor-holomorphic-roots-homologically-simply-connected-domains]]).

[F9] If $g$ is continuous on an interval and nowhere zero, then $g$ has constant sign there ([[thm-intermediate-value]]); a positive continuous integrand on an interval gives a strictly increasing integral, and improper integrals at a finite endpoint and at infinity converge or diverge as evaluated there ([[def-improper-integral-at-a-finite-endpoint]], [[def-improper-integral-at-infinity]]); the change-of-variables formula holds for such integrals with the absolute derivative ([[cor-one-dimensional-change-of-variables-with-absolute-derivative]]).

[F10] An injective holomorphic map on a complex domain is biholomorphic onto its open image, with nowhere-zero derivative ([[cor-injective-holomorphic-derivative-nonzero]]).

[F11] Holomorphic functions have local Taylor expansions and obey the chain and product rules ([[thm-taylor-expansion-holomorphic-function]], [[thm-chain-rule-for-complex-derivatives]], [[thm-algebra-of-complex-derivatives]]). A holomorphic function with nonzero derivative has a holomorphic local inverse ([[thm-holomorphic-inverse-function-theorem]]). If $g(a)\ne0$ is holomorphic near $a$, the implicit function theorem applied to $v^2-g(z)$ supplies a nonvanishing holomorphic square root of $g$ near $a$ ([[thm-holomorphic-implicit-function-theorem]]).

## Verification

1.1 (Conjugation symmetry of $\wp$ and $\wp'$.) Since $\overline\alpha=\alpha\in\Lambda$ and $\overline\beta=-\beta\in\Lambda$, the lattice is conjugation-invariant. Conjugating the defining net of $\wp$ termwise for $z\notin\Lambda$ and using continuity of conjugation and the local uniform convergence of the net ([F1, F4]) gives $\wp(\overline z)=\overline{\wp(z)}$; differentiating this identity gives $\wp'(\overline z)=\overline{\wp'(z)}$. In particular $\wp$ is real on the real axis, and for $z=x+\beta/2$ one has $\overline z=z-\beta\equiv z$ modulo $\Lambda$, so $\wp(\overline z)=\wp(z)$ and $\wp(z)=\overline{\wp(z)}$: $\wp$ is real on the two lines $\operatorname{Im}z=0$ and $\operatorname{Im}z=b/2$. [F1, F4, F5]

1.2 (The half-period rectangle: no poles and no critical points inside.) Put $S=\{s\tfrac\alpha2+t\tfrac\beta2:0\le s,t\le1\}$; its interior $S^\circ$ is $\{0<s,t<1\}$ in these coordinates. An interior point $z=s\alpha/2+t\beta/2$ is not in $\Lambda$: if $z=m\alpha+n\beta$ with $m,n\in\mathbb Z$, then $s=2m$ and $t=2n$ by uniqueness of coordinates ([F5]), but $0<s,t<1$ excludes integers. It is not a zero of $\wp'$: such a zero would satisfy $z\equiv c_j$ modulo $\Lambda$ ([F2]) for one of $c_1=\alpha/2$, $c_2=\beta/2$, $c_3=(\alpha+\beta)/2$, i.e. $(s,t)$ would differ from $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,1)$ by even integers, forcing $s\in 2\mathbb Z+1$ or $t\in 2\mathbb Z+1$, again impossible for $0<s,t<1$. Consequently $\wp$ is holomorphic on $S^\circ$, $\wp'\ne0$ there, and for $z,w\in S^\circ$ the equality $\wp(z)=\wp(w)$ forces $w\equiv\pm z$ by [F2]: a lattice shift $w=z+\lambda$ forces $s'-s,t'-t\in2\mathbb Z$ with $|s'-s|<1$, hence $s=s'$, $t=t'$; and a sign choice $w=-z+\lambda$ forces $s+s'\in2\mathbb Z$ with $0<s+s'<2$, hence $s+s'=2$ (impossible, as $s,s'<1$) or $s+s'=0$ (impossible). Thus $\wp$ is injective on $S^\circ$. [F1, F2, F5]

1.3 (The Jacobi integrand: a normalized square root on $H$.) Let $H=\{z:\operatorname{Im}z>0\}$ and $P(\zeta)=(1-\zeta^2)(1-k^2\zeta^2)$. Its zeros are $\pm1,\pm1/k$, all real, so $P$ is holomorphic and nowhere zero on the simply connected domain $H$; by [F8] there is a holomorphic square root $q$ of $P$ on $H$. For each real $x$ with $P(x)\ne0$, [F11] supplies a local nonvanishing holomorphic square root $v$ of $P$ on a disc about $x$. The quotient $q/v$ on the connected upper half-disc has square $1$, so is a constant sign; thus $q$ extends holomorphically across $x$. On $(-1,1)$ the extended function $q^2=P$ is positive, so $q$ is continuous and nowhere zero there with $q(x)=\pm\sqrt{P(x)}$: the sign is constant by [F9], and after replacing $q$ by $-q$ if necessary we may and do assume $q(x)>0$ for $-1<x<1$. Then $1/q$ is holomorphic on $H$ and, by [F8], has a primitive $I_k(z)=\int_0^z d\zeta/q(\zeta)$ whose extension at $0$ is normalized by $I_k(0)=0$; it satisfies $I_k'(z)=1/q(z)\ne0$ on $H$. [F8, F9, F11]

2.1 (The exact real locus.) For $z\in\mathbb C\setminus\Lambda$: $\wp(z)\in\mathbb R$ iff $\wp(z)=\overline{\wp(z)}=\wp(\overline z)$ iff $\overline z\equiv\pm z$ modulo $\Lambda$ by the fibre criterion ([F2]); and $\overline z\equiv z$ means $2i\operatorname{Im}z\in\Lambda$, i.e. $\operatorname{Im}z\in\tfrac b2\mathbb Z$, while $\overline z\equiv-z$ means $2\operatorname{Re}z\in\Lambda$, i.e. $\operatorname{Re}z\in\tfrac\alpha2\mathbb Z$ ([F4, F5]). Thus on $\mathbb C\setminus\Lambda$ the real-value locus is exactly the union of the horizontal lines $\operatorname{Im}z\in\tfrac b2\mathbb Z$ and the vertical lines $\operatorname{Re}z\in\tfrac\alpha2\mathbb Z$; the excluded lattice points on those lines are poles by [F1]. [F1, F2, F4, F5, step 1.1]

2.2 (Monotonicity along the four edges and the order $e_2<e_3<e_1$.) The boundary $\partial S$ consists of the images of the four segments $0\to c_1\to c_3\to c_2\to0$. On the open segment from $0$ to $c_1$ (a real interval), $\wp$ is real (step 1.1), has no pole and no critical point, so its derivative is continuous and nowhere zero, hence of constant sign ([F9]); since $\wp(x)\to+\infty$ as $x\to0^+$ by the principal part in [F1] and $\wp(c_1)=e_1$, this sign is negative and $\wp$ decreases strictly from $+\infty$ to $e_1$. Likewise along the open segment from $c_1$ to $c_3$ and from $c_3$ to $c_2$ and from $c_2$ to $0$, all lying on the real locus lines, $\wp$ takes real values with nowhere-zero derivative and is thus strictly monotone; the segment from $c_2$ to $0$ is the imaginary interval because $c_2=ib/2$, and $\wp(iy)\to-\infty$ as $y\to0^+$ by [F1], so there $\wp$ decreases to $-\infty$. Because $\wp'(c_j)=0$ ([F2]), Taylor expansion at $c_j$ using [F3] gives $\wp(c_j+w)=e_j+\tfrac12\wp''(c_j)w^2+O(|w|^3)$ with $\wp''(c_j)=6e_j^2-g_2/2$ and, comparing the expansions of both sides of $(\wp')^2=4(\wp-e_1)(\wp-e_2)(\wp-e_3)$, $\wp''(c_j)=2\prod_{i\ne j}(e_j-e_i)$. At $c_1$ the incident edge towards $0$ has values $>e_1$ and the incident edge towards $c_3$ has values $<e_1$ (both signs by the second-order expansion, whose quadratic coefficient for the first edge is $+\wp''(c_1)/2$ and for the second $-\wp''(c_1)/2$), so $e_3<e_1$. At $c_2$ the incident edge towards $0$ has values $<e_2$ and the incident edge towards $c_3$ has values $>e_2$, so $e_3>e_2$. Hence $e_2<e_3<e_1$. [F1, F2, F3, F9, step 1.1]

2.3 (Boundary values of $q$ and $I_k$.) Step 1.3 extends $q$ across every real point where $P\ne0$. At a simple root $a$, write $P(z)=(z-a)g_a(z)$ with $g_a(a)=P'(a)\ne0$ and use [F11] to choose a holomorphic unit $v_a$ with $v_a^2=g_a$. On the upper half-disc, $q(z)=\pm v_a(z)\sqrt{z-a}$, since the quotient has square $1$ and is constant on that connected set. As one passes from the interval to the left of $a$ to the interval to its right through the upper half-plane, $\sqrt{z-a}$ changes from $i\sqrt{|x-a|}$ to $\sqrt{|x-a|}$; the unit $v_a$ retains its sign continuously. Starting with $q>0$ on $(-1,1)$, this gives $q=-i\sqrt{|P|}$ on $(1,1/k)$, $q=+i\sqrt{|P|}$ on $(-1/k,-1)$, and $q=-\sqrt{P}$ on both tails. Here $P'(1)=-2(1-k^2)$, $P'(-1)=2(1-k^2)$, $P'(1/k)=2(1-k^2)/k$, and $P'(-1/k)=-2(1-k^2)/k$. Moreover $|1/q(z)|\le C_a|z-a|^{-1/2}$ near $a$ in $H$. Integrating on radial segments and circular arcs gives a finite boundary limit of $I_k$ with $|I_k(z)-I_k(a)|\le C'_a|z-a|^{1/2}$; thus the primitive is continuous at each of the four branch points. Hence, using $I_k'(x+i0)=1/q(x+i0)$ and $I_k(0)=0$: $I_k$ is strictly increasing on $(-1,1)$ with $I_k(\pm1)=\pm K$; $I_k(x)=K+i\int_1^x ds/\sqrt{(s^2-1)(1-k^2s^2)}$ for $1\le x\le 1/k$, so $I_k(1/k)=K+iK'(k)$ where $K'(k)=\int_1^{1/k}ds/\sqrt{(s^2-1)(1-k^2s^2)}$; $I_k(x)=-K+i\int_x^{-1}ds/\sqrt{(s^2-1)(1-k^2s^2)}$ for $-1/k\le x\le-1$, so $I_k(-1/k)=-K+iK'(k)$; and on the tails $I_k(x)=I_k(1/k)-\int_{1/k}^x ds/\sqrt{(s^2-1)(k^2s^2-1)}$ and $I_k(x)=I_k(-1/k)+\int_x^{-1/k}ds/\sqrt{(s^2-1)(k^2s^2-1)}$, with $\int_{1/k}^{\infty}ds/\sqrt{(s^2-1)(k^2s^2-1)}=K$ and $\int_{-\infty}^{-1/k}ds/\sqrt{(s^2-1)(k^2s^2-1)}=K$, so both tails tend to $iK'(k)$. Here $\int_1^{1/k}ds/\sqrt{(s^2-1)(1-k^2s^2)}=K(\sqrt{1-k^2})=K'$ under the substitution $s=(1-k'^2u^2)^{-1/2}$ with $k'=\sqrt{1-k^2}$, and $\int_{1/k}^{\infty}ds/\sqrt{(s^2-1)(k^2s^2-1)}=K$ under $s=1/(ku)$, both by the change-of-variables formula ([F9]). Finally, for $|z|=R>1/k$ in $H$ one has $|P(z)|\ge(R^2-1)(k^2R^2-1)$, so the integral of $1/q$ along the semicircle of radius $R$ is $O(1/R)$; since $I_k(R)\to iK'(k)$ along the real axis, $I_k(z)\to iK'(k)$ uniformly as $|z|\to\infty$ in $H$. [F4, F8, F9, F11, step 1.3]

3.1 (The image lies in one half-plane.) $S^\circ$ is convex, hence connected, and $\wp(S^\circ)$ is connected; since $\wp'\ne0$ on $S^\circ$, $\wp$ is an open map there and $\wp(S^\circ)$ is open ([F10]); and $\wp(S^\circ)\cap\mathbb R=\varnothing$ by step 2.1, because no interior point lies on any of the lines $\operatorname{Re}z\in\tfrac\alpha2\mathbb Z$, $\operatorname{Im}z\in\tfrac b2\mathbb Z$. An open connected subset of $\mathbb C\setminus(\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\})$ is contained in the upper or in the lower half-plane. [F4, step 2.1, step 1.2]

3.2 (The boundary maps onto $\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\}$.) By step 2.2 the four open edges have images $(e_1,\infty)$, the interval between $e_1$ and $e_3$, the interval between $e_3$ and $e_2$, and $(-\infty,e_2)$, and with $e_2<e_3<e_1$ these intervals are $(e_1,\infty)$, $(e_3,e_1)$, $(e_2,e_3)$ and $(-\infty,e_2)$, which together with the endpoint values and $\wp(0)=\infty$ cover $\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\}$ exactly once on the boundary circle. [F1, step 2.2]

3.3 ($I_k$ maps $H$ biholomorphically onto the rectangle.) Let $R_0=(-K,K)\times(0,K')$ and $R>1/k$. The real-segment path $\gamma_R:=I_k([-R,R])$ starts at $iK'-T_R$ and ends at $iK'+T_R$, where $T_R=\int_R^\infty ds/\sqrt{(s^2-1)(k^2s^2-1)}>0$ tends to zero by step 2.3. It follows the boundary of $R_0$ counterclockwise except for the short top segment joining those endpoints. The image $\delta_R$ of the upper semicircle runs from $iK'+T_R$ back to $iK'-T_R$ and lies in a disc of radius $O(1/R)$ about $iK'$ by step 2.3. For a fixed $w$ away from $\partial R_0$, choose $R$ large enough that both $\delta_R$ and the missing top segment lie in a disc about $iK'$ disjoint from $w$. A straight-line homotopy in that disc deforms $\delta_R$ to the missing segment, so the **closed full image contour** $\gamma_R*\delta_R=I_k(\partial D_R)$ has winding number $1$ about $w\in R_0$ and $0$ about $w\notin\overline{R_0}$.

To apply the argument principle without crossing the four branch points on the real boundary, use $D_{R,\epsilon}:=\{z\in H:|z|<R,\ \operatorname{Im}z>\epsilon\}$. The function $I_k$ is holomorphic with $I_k'=1/q\ne0$ on a neighbourhood of its closure. As $\epsilon\downarrow0$, the image of its closed boundary tends uniformly to $\gamma_R*\delta_R$, since step 2.3 gives continuous boundary values, including the integrable square-root endpoints. For $w$ off $\partial R_0$, winding number is stable for small $\epsilon$; the argument principle [F6], with zero pole count because $I_k$ is holomorphic and with index $1$ on the truncated half-disc, therefore gives exactly one preimage in $D_{R,\epsilon}$ when $w\in R_0$, and none when $w\notin\overline{R_0}$. Letting $\epsilon\downarrow0$ and then $R\to\infty$ proves the same counts on $H$: any preimage lies in some such truncated half-disc, and step 2.3 gives $I_k(z)\to iK'$ at infinity. Thus every $w\in R_0$ has exactly one preimage, and no $w\notin\overline{R_0}$ has one. An image point on $\partial R_0$ would, by $I_k'\ne0$, have an open image neighbourhood containing a point outside $\overline{R_0}$, impossible. Hence $I_k(H)=R_0$, and the injective holomorphic map $I_k:H\to R_0$ is biholomorphic by [F10]. [F6, F10, step 1.3, step 2.3, algebra]

4.1 (The image of $S$ is exactly one half-plane and $\wp|_S$ is conformal.) Every boundary point of $\wp(S^\circ)$ is a limit $\wp(z_n)$ with $z_n\in S^\circ$; by compactness of $\overline S$ pass to a subsequence with $z_n\to z_0\in\overline S$ and $\wp(z_0)=w$ with the value $\wp(0)=\infty$ allowed. If $z_0\in S^\circ$ then $w\in\wp(S^\circ)$, which is impossible for a boundary point because $\wp(S^\circ)$ is open; hence $z_0\in\partial S$ and, by step 3.2, $w\in\wp(\partial S)\subseteq\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\}$. Thus $\partial\wp(S^\circ)\subseteq\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\}$, while by step 3.1 the set $\wp(S^\circ)$ is a nonempty open connected subset of one half-plane $U_\pm$, and $U_\pm\cap(\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\})=\varnothing$. If $w\in U_\pm\setminus\wp(S^\circ)$, join $w$ to a point $q\in\wp(S^\circ)$ by the segment $\gamma$ inside the convex set $U_\pm$ and let $t_0=\inf\{t:\gamma(t)\in\wp(S^\circ)\}$; then $\gamma(t_0)\in\partial\wp(S^\circ)\cap U_\pm$, contradicting $\partial\wp(S^\circ)\subseteq\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\}$. Hence $\wp(S^\circ)=U_\pm$, and $\wp:S^\circ\to U_\pm$ is injective with nowhere-zero derivative, hence biholomorphic, and in particular conformal. [F10, step 3.1, step 3.2]

5.1 (Derivative of the inverse branch.) Let $U=U_\pm$ and $f=\wp|_{S^\circ}^{-1}:U\to S^\circ$. Then $f$ is holomorphic, $\wp(f(\zeta))=\zeta$ for $\zeta\in U$, and the chain rule gives $\wp'(f(\zeta))f'(\zeta)=1$, so $f'(\zeta)=1/\wp'(f(\zeta))$. Substituting $\wp(f(\zeta))=\zeta$ in the differential equation [F3] gives $\wp'(f(\zeta))^2=4\zeta^3-g_2\zeta-g_3$, and since $f'$ is nowhere zero its reciprocal $\zeta\mapsto 1/f'(\zeta)=\wp'(f(\zeta))$ is a holomorphic square root of $4\zeta^3-g_2\zeta-g_3$ on $U$; writing $(4\zeta^3-g_2\zeta-g_3)^{-1/2}$ for that reciprocal root, every inverse branch satisfies $f'(\zeta)=(4\zeta^3-g_2\zeta-g_3)^{-1/2}$. [F3, step 4.1]

6.1 (The four period integrals.) On each of the four real intervals between consecutive roots the polynomial $4x^3-g_2x-g_3=4(x-e_1)(x-e_2)(x-e_3)$ has constant sign; $4x^3-g_2x-g_3>0$ on $(e_1,\infty)$ and on $(e_2,e_3)$, while $g_3+g_2x-4x^3>0$ on $(-\infty,e_2)$ and on $(e_3,e_1)$. Each of these intervals is the image under $\wp$ of one open edge of $\partial S$. Since $\wp^\prime$ is nonzero on each open edge, the local inverse theorem [F11] extends the inverse branch across the corresponding real interval, mapping it bijectively onto that edge with $|f'(x)|=1/\sqrt{|4x^3-g_2x-g_3|}$ by step 5.1, so the integral of the positive square root over the interval equals the length of the corresponding displacement: writing the inverse branch as $g$, $\int_a^b dx/\sqrt{|4x^3-g_2x-g_3|}=|\lim_{x\downarrow a}g(x)-\lim_{x\uparrow b}g(x)|$ by the change-of-variables formula and strict monotonicity of $g$ ([F9]). The four displacements are: from $0$ to $c_1$, length $\alpha/2$; from $c_2$ to $c_3$, length $\alpha/2$; from $c_2$ to $0$, length $|\beta|/2=\beta/(2i)$; from $c_1$ to $c_3$, length $\beta/(2i)$. The improper endpoints converge: at a simple root the integrand is $O(|x-e_j|^{-1/2})$, and at infinity it is $O(|x|^{-3/2})$. Hence the four displayed identities hold with the positive real square roots. [F1, F2, F3, F9, F11, step 2.2, step 3.2, step 5.1]

7.1 (The inverse sn and its double periodicity.) At a finite boundary branch point $a$ of $I_k$, use $z=a+t^2$. The expression $q(a+t^2)/t$ is a holomorphic unit by the factorization in step 2.3; hence $J(t):=I_k(a+t^2)$ extends holomorphically to $t=0$ and $J'(0)=2/(q(a+t^2)/t)|_{t=0}\ne0$. By [F11] its local inverse makes $z=a+t(w)^2$ holomorphic at the associated rectangle vertex. On the tails, the branch sign in step 2.3 gives $q(z)=-kz^2(1+O(z^{-2}))$: this follows by applying [F11] to the square root of $1-(1+k^2)/(k^2z^2)+1/(k^2z^4)$ near $1/z=0$. Thus $I_k(z)=iK'+1/(kz)+O(z^{-3})$, and $t\mapsto I_k(1/t)$ extends holomorphically at $0$ with derivative $1/k\ne0$. Its local inverse shows that $1/\mathrm{sn}$ is holomorphic with a simple zero at $iK'$. Let $\mathrm{sn}=I_k^{-1}:R_0\to H$; it is holomorphic ([F10]) and continuous on $\overline{R_0}\setminus\{iK'\}$ with real boundary values: the bottom edge $[-K,K]$ maps into $(-1,1)$ with $\mathrm{sn}(0)=0$, $\mathrm{sn}(\pm K)=\pm1$; the left edge $-K+i(0,K')$ maps into $(-1/k,-1)$ with $\mathrm{sn}(-K+iK')=-1/k$ (at the boundary branch value $-1/k$); the right edge $K+i(0,K')$ maps into $(1,1/k)$ with $\mathrm{sn}(K+iK')=1/k$; and the top edge $(-K,K)+iK'$ maps into $\mathbb R$ with $|\mathrm{sn}|>1/k$, with a simple pole at $iK'$ (from step 2.3, $I_k(z)=iK'+1/(kz)+O(z^{-2})$ for large $z$, so near the omitted value $iK'$ the inverse behaves like $1/(k(w-iK'))$). Since $\mathrm{sn}$ is holomorphic on $R_0$ and real on each of the four open sides, the Schwarz reflection principle [F7] extends it across each side by reflection there: away from $iK'$ this is the stated holomorphic reflection, and at $iK'$ one applies the same principle to the reciprocal $u=1/\mathrm{sn}$, which is holomorphic near $iK'$ with $u(iK')=0$ and real boundary values, so $u$ reflects and $\mathrm{sn}=1/u$ reflects meromorphically; the reflected copies tile the plane, because the four reflections $\sigma_b(z)=\overline z$, $\sigma_t(z)=\overline z+2iK'$, $\sigma_l(z)=-2K-\overline z$, $\sigma_r(z)=2K-\overline z$ have compositions $\sigma_b\sigma_t:z\mapsto z-2iK'$ and $\sigma_r\sigma_l:z\mapsto z+4K$, and their reflected rectangle copies tile the plane. Across an open edge the extensions agree by reflection; at a vertex they agree with the holomorphic squared inverse just constructed, and at a reflected copy of $iK'$ they agree with its meromorphic pole extension. Thus the pieces glue on every edge and vertex, producing a single meromorphic function $\mathrm{sn}$ on $\mathbb C$ satisfies $\mathrm{sn}(\sigma_jz)=\overline{\mathrm{sn}(z)}$ for each reflection $\sigma_j$, so $\mathrm{sn}(z-2iK')=\overline{\mathrm{sn}(\sigma_tz)}=\mathrm{sn}(z)$ and $\mathrm{sn}(z+4K)=\overline{\mathrm{sn}(\sigma_lz)}=\mathrm{sn}(z)$: $\mathrm{sn}$ is doubly periodic with periods $4K$ and $2iK'$ (and period $2iK'$ implies period $-2iK'$). [F7, F10, F11, step 2.3, step 3.3, algebra] ∎

## Remarks

The sign analysis is the only delicate point. The normalized square root $q$ of $(1-\zeta^2)(1-k^2\zeta^2)$ is positive on $(-1,1)$, and the local factorizations at $\pm1$ and $\pm1/k$ force the boundary values $-i\sqrt{|P|}$ on $(1,1/k)$, $+i\sqrt{|P|}$ on $(-1/k,-1)$, and $-\sqrt{P}$ on both tails; the two tails then approach the same point $iK'$, which is what closes the image of the real axis into the boundary of the rectangle. The same computation for $\wp$ gives $e_2<e_3<e_1$ directly from the second-order expansions at $c_1$ and $c_2$; no numerical evaluation of any elliptic integral is used, only the two standard substitutions reducing $K'(k)$ to $K(\sqrt{1-k^2})$ and the tail integral to $K$ itself.
