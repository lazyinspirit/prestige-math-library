## Example

Let $[0,1]$ carry the subspace topology from the usual topology of $\mathbb{R}$
([[def-interval]], [[def-subspace-topology-top]],
[[lem-real-line-is-a-metric-space]]) and let

$$Q \;:=\; [0,1]^{\mathbb{N}} \;=\; \prod_{k \in \mathbb{N}} [0,1]$$

carry the product topology ([[def-product-topology]]); $Q$ is the **Hilbert
cube**. Define

$$d(x,y) \;:=\; \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{|x_k - y_k|}{2^{\,k+1}} \qquad (x,y \in Q).$$

Then:

1. **$d$ is defined**, with $0 \le d(x,y) \le 1$: the series has nonnegative terms
   bounded by $2^{-(k+1)}$, and $\sum_{k} 2^{-(k+1)} = 1$
   ([[thm-geometric-series]], [[thm-direct-comparison-test]]).
2. **$d$ is a metric on $Q$** ([[def-metric-space]]).
3. **$d$ induces the product topology**, so $Q$ is metrizable
   ([[def-metrizable-space]]).

Claim 3 is the only real work, and it is what the index weights are for: the
factor $2^{-(k+1)}$ makes the tail of the sum small no matter what the
coordinates do, so a constraint on finitely many coordinates already forces $d$
to be small, and conversely $d$ small forces each *individual* coordinate to be
close.

By claim 1 of [[thm-product-of-subspaces-and-closures]] the topology on $Q$ is
also the subspace topology it inherits from $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}$, so the two
readings of "$[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}$" agree.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** $Q = \prod_{k \in \mathbb{N}}[0,1]$ with the product topology, points $x, y, z \in Q$, the function $d$ above, and a real $r > 0$. Powers $2^{-k}$ are integer powers ([[def-integer-power]]) and $2$ denotes $\iota(2)$ ([[def-canonical-natural]]).

[A1] A basis for the product topology on $Q$ is the family of boxes $\prod_k O_k$ with every $O_k$ open in $[0,1]$ and $O_k = [0,1]$ off a list $k_0,\dots,k_{n-1}$; the product topology is generated by the sets $\pi_j^{-1}[O]$ with $O$ open in $[0,1]$ ([[def-product-topology]]).

[A2] $O \subseteq [0,1]$ is open in $[0,1]$ exactly when $O = V \cap [0,1]$ for some $V$ open in $\mathbb{R}$; in particular $\{\, t \in [0,1] : |t - s| < \varepsilon \,\} = (s-\varepsilon,\ s+\varepsilon) \cap [0,1]$ is open in $[0,1]$ for every $s$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$, and every open $O \ni s$ of $[0,1]$ contains such a set ([[def-subspace-topology-top]], [[lem-real-line-is-a-metric-space]], [[def-interval]]).

[L1] $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} r^k = 1/(1-r)$ for $|r| < 1$ ([[thm-geometric-series]]); a nonnegative series converges iff its partial sums are bounded, and then each partial sum is at most the sum ([[thm-nonnegative-series-bounded-partial-sums]]); a nonnegative series dominated termwise by a convergent one converges ([[thm-direct-comparison-test]]); series are additive and homogeneous ([[lem-series-linearity]], [[def-series]]).

[L2] Finite sums are monotone in their terms, $\sum_{k<n}\lambda = n\lambda$, and a finite sum of nonnegative terms that vanishes has all its terms zero ([[lem-finite-sum-laws]], claims 2 and 4); weak inequalities pass to limits ([[lem-limit-preserves-order]]).

[L3] $|s| \ge 0$, $|s| = 0$ iff $s = 0$, $|s| = |-s|$ ([[lem-of-abs-value]]), and $|s-u| \le |s-t| + |t-u|$ ([[lem-of-triangle-inequality]]).

[L4] $2^{m}2^{n} = 2^{m+n}$ and $2^{-m} = (2^m)^{-1}$, with $2^k > 0$ ([[lem-power-laws]], [[def-integer-power]], [[lem-of-inverse-positive]]).

[L5] If $\sum a_k$ converges then $a_k \to 0$ ([[lem-nth-term-test]]); below any positive real lies a positive rational ([[lem-rat-embeds-dense]]), so a convergence tested at rational tolerances delivers every real tolerance.

[L6] In a metric space the balls $B_d(u,\rho)$ form a basis of the metric topology, and $U$ is $d$-open exactly when every $u \in U$ has some $\rho > 0$ with $B_d(u,\rho) \subseteq U$ ([[def-metric-topology]], [[def-metric-ball]]).

## Verification

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 $\sum_{k} 2^{-(k+1)} = 1$, being $\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_k 2^{-k} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 2$ by [L1] and [L4]; and more generally $\sum_{k \ge N} 2^{-(k+1)} = 2^{-N}$ for every $N \in \mathbb{N}$, by shifting the index. [L1, L4]

1.2 $2^{-k} \to 0$, since $\sum_k 2^{-k}$ converges by [L1]; so for every real $\eta > 0$ there is $N \in \mathbb{N}$ with $2^{-N} < \eta$. [L1, L5]

1.3 $d(x,y) = d(y,x)$, term by term, by [L3]. This is (M2). [L3]

2.1 For $x, y \in Q$ and every $k$: $0 \le |x_k - y_k| \le 1$, since both coordinates lie in $[0,1]$; hence $0 \le |x_k-y_k| 2^{-(k+1)} \le 2^{-(k+1)}$. By [L1] and step 1.1 the series defining $d(x,y)$ converges and $0 \le d(x,y) \le 1$. This is claim 1. [step 1.1, A2, L1, L2, L3, L4]

3.1 For every $j \in \mathbb{N}$ and $x,y \in Q$: $|x_j - y_j| 2^{-(j+1)} \le d(x,y)$, the left side being a single term of a nonnegative convergent series and hence at most one of its partial sums, which is at most the sum by [L1]. [step 2.1, L1, L2]

3.2 $d(x,z) \le d(x,y) + d(y,z)$: for every $k$ one has $|x_k-z_k| \le |x_k-y_k| + |y_k-z_k|$ by [L3], so each partial sum of the left series is at most the corresponding partial sum of the sum of the two right series by [L2], and the inequality passes to the limits by [L2] and [L1]. This is (M3), so claim 2 holds. [step 2.1, L1, L2, L3]

3.3 Every $d$-ball contains a basic product-open neighbourhood of its centre. Given $u \in Q$ and $\rho > 0$, take $N$ with $2^{-N} < \rho/2$ by step 1.2 and put $W := \{\, y \in Q : |y_k - u_k| < \rho/2 \text{ for every } k < N \,\}$, which is a basic product-open set containing $u$ by [A1] and [A2]. For $y \in W$, splitting the series at $N$ gives $d(u,y) \le (\rho/2)\sum_{k<N}2^{-(k+1)} + \sum_{k \ge N} 2^{-(k+1)} \le \rho/2 + 2^{-N} < \rho$, by steps 1.1 and 2.1 with [L1] and [L2]. So $u \in W \subseteq B_d(u,\rho)$. [step 1.1, step 2.1, step 1.2, A1, A2, L1, L2]

4.1 $d(x,x) = 0$, every term vanishing by [L3]. Conversely if $d(x,y) = 0$ then by step 3.1 every $|x_j-y_j|2^{-(j+1)} \le 0$, so $|x_j - y_j| = 0$ and $x_j = y_j$ for every $j$ by [L3] and [L4]; hence $x = y$. This is (M1). [step 3.1, L3, L4]

4.2 Every subbasic product-open set is $d$-open. Let $O$ be open in $[0,1]$, let $x \in \pi_j^{-1}[O]$ and take $\varepsilon > 0$ with $\{\, t \in [0,1] : |t - x_j| < \varepsilon \,\} \subseteq O$, available by [A2]. If $d(x,y) < \varepsilon 2^{-(j+1)}$ then $|x_j-y_j| 2^{-(j+1)} < \varepsilon 2^{-(j+1)}$ by step 3.1, so $|x_j - y_j| < \varepsilon$ by [L4], so $y_j \in O$; hence $B_d(x, \varepsilon 2^{-(j+1)}) \subseteq \pi_j^{-1}[O]$. [step 3.1, A1, A2, L4, L6]

4.3 $\mathcal{T}_d$ is contained in the product topology: by [L6] it suffices that every ball $B_d(x,r)$ be product-open, and for $z \in B_d(x,r)$ the triangle inequality of step 3.2 gives $B_d(z, r - d(x,z)) \subseteq B_d(x,r)$ with $r - d(x,z) > 0$, while step 3.3 supplies a basic product-open $W$ with $z \in W \subseteq B_d(z, r - d(x,z))$. [step 3.2, step 3.3, L6]

5.1 The product topology is contained in $\mathcal{T}_d$: by step 4.2 every subbasic product-open set is $d$-open, and $\mathcal{T}_d$ is a topology containing them, hence contains the topology they generate, which is the product topology by [A1]. [step 4.2, A1, L6]

6.1 By steps 5.1 and 4.3 the metric topology of $d$ is the product topology on $Q$, so $Q$ is metrizable; this is claim 3, and with steps 2.1 and 3.2 all three claims are proved. [step 2.1, step 3.2, step 5.1, step 4.3] ∎

## Remarks

- **The weights do two opposite jobs at once.** Making the $k$-th weight small
  ensures that the coordinates beyond a chosen index contribute at most $2^{-N}$
  in total, which is what step 3.2 needs; keeping every weight strictly positive
  ensures that a single coordinate cannot be far apart without $d$ noticing,
  which is what step 4.2 needs. A weight sequence that failed either condition
  would fail to metrise the product topology.

- **Nothing here generalises for free.** The argument uses that the index set is
  $\mathbb{N}$, through the convergent series of weights, and that each factor is
  bounded, through $|x_k - y_k| \le 1$. Neither restriction is removable by this
  method, and no general theorem about metrizability of products is claimed on
  these pages ([[rem-constructions-this-page-stops-short-of]]).

- **The Hilbert cube is a product of subspaces, and that is unambiguous.** Claim 1
  of [[thm-product-of-subspaces-and-closures]] identifies the product of the
  subspaces $[0,1] \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with the subspace $\prod_k [0,1]$ of
  $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}$, so the metric above may equally be read as a metric
  on that subspace.
