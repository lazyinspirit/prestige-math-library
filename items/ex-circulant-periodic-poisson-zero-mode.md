## Statement

For each $N\ge3$, let $C_N=C(c)$ be the periodic cycle Laplacian circulant generated by $c_0=2$, $c_1=-1$, $c_{N-1}=-1$, and $c_j=0$ otherwise. With $\omega=e^{-2\pi i/N}$, its Fourier eigenvalues are $\lambda_k=2-\omega^k-\omega^{-k}$. Exactly $\lambda_0$ vanishes, so $C_Nx=f$ is solvable exactly when $\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}f_j=0$; on the zero-mean subspace the solution is unique. For $N=4$ and $f=(1,-1,1,-1)^T$, the solution is $x=f/4$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The circulant convention, canonical DFT, and complex exponential used in the Statement.

[F1] The circulant generated by $c$ has entries $c_{(i-j)\bmod N}$, and the canonical DFT has negative-sign entries $N^{-1/2}e^{-2\pi i k j/N}$. [[def-toeplitz-circulant-and-displacement-notation]] [[def-unitary-discrete-fourier-transform-on-z-mod-n]]

[F2] Circulants are diagonalized by the canonical DFT with eigenvalues $\sum_j c_j e^{-2\pi i k j/N}$; compatibility is equivalent to vanishing of the transformed right-hand side at each zero eigenvalue. [[prop-circulants-are-unitarily-diagonalized-by-the-canonical-dft]]

[F3] The complex exponential satisfies $\exp(z+w)=\exp(z)\exp(w)$, $|\exp(iy)|=1$ for real $y$, and $\exp z=1$ exactly when $z\in2\pi i\mathbb Z$. [[cor-complex-exponential-cartesian-form-modulus-and-eulers-identity]] [[thm-complex-exponential-addition-and-real-extension]] [[thm-kernel-and-fibres-of-complex-exponential]]

[F4] The complex numbers form a field, so a square of a complex number vanishes only when the number vanishes. [[thm-complex-numbers-form-a-field]]

## Proof

1.1 By [F1]–[F2], the $k$th eigenvalue of this generator is $$\lambda_k=2-\omega^k-\omega^{k(N-1)}.$$ Since $\omega^N=\exp(-2\pi i)=1$ by [F3], $\omega^{k(N-1)}=\omega^{-k}$, giving the stated symbol. [given, F1, F2, F3, algebra]

2.1 Put $z=\omega^k$. It is nonzero by [F3], and $$\lambda_k=0\iff z+z^{-1}=2\iff (z-1)^2=0\iff z=1,$$ where the middle equivalence follows after multiplying by $z$, and the last uses [F4]. By the exponential kernel characterization in [F3], $\omega^k=1$ exactly when $-2\pi i k/N\in2\pi i\mathbb Z$, equivalently $k/N$ is an integer. For $0\le k<N$ this means $k=0$. Thus only the constant Fourier mode is a zero mode. [given, F3, F4, step 1.1, algebra]

3.1 At $k=0$, $(\mathcal F_N f)_0=N^{-1/2}\sum_j f_j$. Hence [F2] gives solvability precisely when $\sum_j f_j=0$. All nonzero modes then determine $\mathcal F_Nx$ uniquely, while the zero-mode coordinate is free; imposing $\sum_jx_j=0$ fixes that coordinate to zero, proving uniqueness on the zero-mean subspace. [F1, F2, step 2.1, algebra]

3.2 When $N=4$, $\omega=\exp(-\pi i/2)$, so the addition law gives $\omega^2=\exp(-\pi i)$ and $\exp(-\pi i)\exp(\pi i)=1$. Euler's identity gives $\exp(\pi i)=-1$, hence $\omega^2=-1$. Since $\omega\ne0$, this implies $\omega^{-1}=-\omega$, and the symbol from step 1.1 gives $(\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)=(0,2,4,2)$. The vector $f=(1,-1,1,-1)^T$ has sum zero. Directly, each cyclic neighbor of $f_j$ is $-f_j$, so $(C_4f)_j=2f_j-(-f_j)-(-f_j)=4f_j$. Therefore $x=f/4$ solves $C_4x=f$, and its zero mean gives the unique zero-mean solution. [given, F1, F3, step 1.1, step 2.1, algebra]

4.1 The compatibility condition is exactly orthogonality to the constant null mode, and division is only performed on the nonzero Fourier modes. The concrete $N=4$ vector verifies the normalization directly. [step 3.1, step 3.2, algebra] ∎
