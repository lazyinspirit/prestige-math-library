## Statement

Let $M=S^3\times[0,1]$. Let $H:D^3\times[0,1]\to M$ be a proper embedding which extends to an embedding of $D^3_{1+\eta}\times[0,1]$ for some $\eta>0$, relatively open at the ambient boundary, with the extended embedding retaining the following endpoint product form. Suppose on actual end collars it has the product form $H(x,t)=(h_i(x),t)$, where $h_i$ are smooth ball charts. Put $T=H(D^3\times[0,1])$, $B_i=h_i(D^3)$, $E_i=\overline{S^3\setminus B_i}$ and $C=\overline{M\setminus T}$. Suppose endpoint complementary-ball charts $e_i:E_i\to D^3$ are supplied with $e_i(h_i(x))=x$ for $x\in S^2$.

There is a homeomorphism $F:C\to D^3\times[0,1]$ satisfying $F(H(x,t))=(x,t)$ for every $x\in S^2$ and $t\in[0,1]$, and satisfying $F(y,t)=(e_i(y),t)$ on smaller actual endpoint product collars. Consequently the tube and its entire complement can be simultaneously carried to the two standard ball products in $S^3\times[0,1]$, with precisely these side and endpoint identifications.

## Facts & Assumptions

[F1] Invariance of domain: an injective continuous map between open subsets of Euclidean spaces of the same dimension is open. This applies in manifold charts. [[thm-invariance-of-domain]]

[F2] A topologically embedded $(n-1)$-sphere in $S^n$ separates it into two complementary domains having that sphere as their common boundary. [[thm-jordan-brouwer-separation]]

## Proof

**Given:** The actual radial-open tube, its actual smooth endpoint product germs, and the compatible complementary-ball charts in the statement.

We first supply the bicollared sphere theorem in dimension $n\ge2$, then apply it with $n=4$. A compact set $K$ is called cellular if $K=\bigcap_jQ_j$ for embedded closed $n$-balls with $Q_{j+1}\subset\operatorname{int}Q_j$. A nontrivial fiber means a fiber containing more than one point. Only finite-dimensional compactness, elementary radial ball homeomorphisms, and the two stated separation/openness facts are used in the cellular argument.

1.1 **Collapsing a cellular set inside a ball.** If $K\subset\operatorname{int}Q$ is cellular, a continuous surjection $q:Q\to Q$ fixed on $\partial Q$ exists whose only nontrivial fiber is K (when K is a singleton, use a homeomorphism instead). Choose the defining balls inside $\operatorname{int}Q$. Construct homeomorphisms $u_j:Q\to Q$, fixed on $\partial Q$, so that $\operatorname{diam}u_j(Q_j)<2^{-j}$, and $u_{j+1}=u_j$ off $\operatorname{int}Q_j$. To make the next choice, compress the compact subset $u_j(Q_{j+1})$ of the interior of the ball $u_j(Q_j)$ into a ball of the desired small diameter by a radial homeomorphism of that ball fixed on its boundary, using any ball coordinate on it. The supports of all later changes lie in $u_j(Q_j)$; consequently $\sup|u_k-u_j|\le\operatorname{diam}u_j(Q_j)$ for every $k\ge j$. This proves uniform convergence to q. Surjectivity follows by compactness, since every u_j is onto. Every point outside K is eventually fixed by all subsequent changes; two points not both in K have distinct images by injectivity of the appropriate u_j and the fact that later images of K remain inside $u_j(Q_j)$. The diameters show $q(K)$ is one point. This proves the claimed cellular collapse. [construct]

1.2 **Finite-fiber ball image.** Suppose $f:Q\to S^n$ has finitely many nontrivial fibers, all contained in $\operatorname{int}Q$. Then $f(Q)$ is the closure of one complementary domain of $f(\partial Q)$. Indeed $f|_{\partial Q}$ is an embedding and $f(\operatorname{int}Q)$ avoids $f(\partial Q)$, hence lies in one domain D by connectedness. Let P be the finite set of exceptional image values in D. At any value of $f(Q)\setminus P$ lying in D, f is locally a homeomorphism by invariance of domain: away from the finitely many compact exceptional fibers its restriction is an injective map on an open neighborhood. Such a value exists, since otherwise the connected image $f(\operatorname{int}Q)$ would be a point, contradicting continuity and the injective boundary map. Thus $f(Q)\cap(D\setminus P)$ is nonempty and both open and closed in $D\setminus P$. For $n\ge2$ that punctured domain is connected, so it is all of $D\setminus P$. Closedness gives $D\subset f(Q)$, and the reverse containment in $\overline D$ was already proved. This establishes the finite-fiber image claim. [F1, F2, construct]

1.3 **A single exceptional fiber is cellular.** Suppose $f:Q\to S^n$ has only one nontrivial fiber $K\subset\operatorname{int}Q$. Put $a=f(K)$. By step 1.2, $f(Q)=\overline D$. Given an open neighborhood U of K inside $\operatorname{int}Q$, $f(U)$ is a neighborhood of a in D: its complement in $f(Q)$ is the compact image of $Q\setminus U$, which misses a. There is a homeomorphism $v:S^n\to S^n$ carrying $\overline D$ into $f(U)$ and equal to the identity on a smaller neighborhood V of a. To construct it, choose p outside $\overline D$, use $S^n\setminus\{p\}\cong\mathbb R^n$, and radially compress a large Euclidean ball containing $\overline D$ into a small ball about a contained in $f(U)$, fixing an even smaller ball about a; extend the radial function increasingly to infinity. Define w on Q by $w(x)=x$ for $x\in K$ and $w(x)=f^{-1}(v(f(x)))$ for $x\notin K$. This expression uses the unique inverse away from a. On $f^{-1}(V)$ it is the identity, so it is continuous across K. It is injective; compactness makes it a homeomorphism onto its image. Its image is an n-ball contained in U, and K lies in its interior because w is the identity on a neighborhood of K. Repeating within successively smaller such neighborhoods constructs nested balls with intersection K. This proves cellularity. [F1, step 1.2, construct]

1.4 **Two exceptional fibers are cellular.** Suppose $f:S^n\to S^n$ is onto and has just two nontrivial fibers A,B, with images a,b. Choose a small round closed ball disjoint from $A\cup B$, and let Q be the complementary closed ball. Then $A\cup B\subset\operatorname{int}Q$. By step 1.2, $f(Q)=\overline D$, where $a,b\in D$. Choose an open neighborhood U of a with $\overline U\subset D\setminus\{b}$. As in step 1.3, choose v carrying $\overline D$ into U and fixing a small neighborhood V of a. Define $w:Q\to Q$ by $w(x)=x$ on A and $w(x)=f^{-1}(v(f(x)))$ off A. Since $v(f(Q))$ avoids b, the inverse is unique except at a; away from A the image avoids a as well. Near A the map is the identity. The only nontrivial fiber of w is B: B maps to the unique point over $v(b)$; all other points are distinct, including the separate points of A. Step 1.3 applied to w proves B cellular; interchange A and B to prove A cellular. This is the corrected reference in Brown's printed Theorem 4. [step 1.2, step 1.3, construct]

1.5 **Recovering a ball from a cellular collapse.** Suppose X is the closure of a complementary domain of a sphere, and $f:X\to R$ is onto a closed n-ball, with only nontrivial fiber $K\subset\operatorname{int}X$ cellular there. Choose a defining ball $Q\subset\operatorname{int}X$ containing K. Step 1.1 supplies $q:Q\to Q$ collapsing just K and fixed on $\partial Q$; extend q by the identity on $X\setminus\operatorname{int}Q$. The maps f and q have precisely the same fibers, so f factors through q as a continuous bijection $X\to R$. Compactness makes this induced map a homeomorphism. Thus X is a ball. [step 1.1, construct]

1.6 **Bicollared sphere theorem.** Let $j:S^{n-1}\times[-1,1]\to S^n$ be an embedding. Let A,B be the two closed exterior sets beyond its two boundary spheres. Collapse A and B to the north and south poles; on the collar use its actual coordinates and the usual latitude quotient $S^{n-1}\times[-1,1]\to S^n$. This defines a continuous surjection $S^n\to S^n$ having only the two nontrivial fibers A,B. Step 1.4 shows both cellular. The closures $X_+,X_-$ of the complementary domains of $j(S^{n-1}\times\{0\})$ contain A,B in their respective interiors; their defining cellular balls can therefore be chosen inside those interiors. The quotient map restricted to either X is onto a hemisphere, a closed n-ball, with just that one exceptional cellular fiber. Step 1.5 shows each X is a ball. This is the required generalized TOP Schoenflies theorem. [F2, step 1.4, step 1.5, construct]

2.1 **A concrete boundary sphere and its bicollar.** Cap both ends of M with standard 4-balls to obtain the standard $S^4$. The boundary of C in this capped ambient space is $\Sigma=(E_0\times\{0\})\cup H(S^2\times I)\cup(E_1\times\{1\})$. The e_i and the side coordinates identify Σ with $\partial(D^3\times I)$, by maps that agree pointwise on each seam. Thus Σ is a topologically embedded $S^3$, with no inference from the core alone. For clarity, the bicollar can be constructed without invoking a global TOP collaring supplier. Transport the product smooth atlas through the open radial tube H onto a neighborhood of the side. Retain the original smooth atlas on neighborhoods of the interiors of the two end complementary balls in the capped ambient space. Arrange all overlaps of these two kinds of neighborhoods to lie inside the genuine supplied endpoint product germs. There $H(x,t)=(h_i(x),t)$, with h_i the actual smooth ball chart, so the transition maps are smooth. No smooth compatibility is asserted outside these overlaps. This gives an auxiliary smooth atlas on a neighborhood of Σ, not on the original annulus or the whole ambient manifold. In that atlas Σ is smooth except for the standard end corners. Near the bottom seam its model is the positive u-axis joined to the positive t-axis in the $(u=r-1,t)$-plane, crossed with $S^2$, and likewise at the top. Round those planar corners in the product neighborhoods. A planar homeomorphism supported in the same neighborhoods carries the unrounded boundary to the rounded smooth boundary. The latter has a smooth collar: take a transverse vector field in its local smooth charts, patch with a smooth partition of unity using its consistently chosen outward side, and use its flow for a uniform short time by compactness. Pull this collar back through the corner homeomorphism. This constructs the required bicollar from the actual tube extension and endpoint overlaps alone. Σ bounds two domains in $S^4$. One domain has closure C. To justify this identification, $\partial C=\Sigma$ is connected; every component of C must have nonempty boundary, since a compact boundaryless component would be open and closed in $S^4$ by invariance of domain. Hence C is connected. Its interior is connected as well, by pushing paths off its boundary in the just-constructed collar. Thus it is one complementary domain of Σ. Brown's theorem in step 1.6 supplies an actual homeomorphism $q:C\to D^4$.  [F1, F2, step 1.6, construct]

2.2 **The entire marked boundary map.** Define $b:\partial C\to\partial(D^3\times I)$ by $b(y,i)=(e_i(y),i)$ and $b(H(x,t))=(x,t)$. The consistency condition on e_i proves agreement on the seams. Each piece is a homeomorphism; the finite closed-piece pasting lemma and compactness prove b is a homeomorphism of the entire boundary. Choose any fixed homeomorphism $r:D^3\times I\to D^4$. Then $k=(r|_{\partial(D^3\times I)})\circ b\circ(q|_{\partial C})^{-1}$ is an actual self-homeomorphism of $S^3$. Its radial Alexander extension is $K(0)=0$ and $K(\rho z)=\rho k(z)$ for $0<\rho\le1$, $z\in S^3$. The map $r^{-1}\circ K\circ q:C\to D^3\times I$ extends **exactly** b.  [step 2.1, construct]

2.3 **Preserving actual collar germs, not merely end faces.** Choose $\delta>0$ inside both exact input product collars. Repeat steps 2.1–2.2 for $M_\delta=S^3\times[\delta,1-\delta]$, its restricted tube, and $C_\delta$. Its end complementary balls remain precisely $E_0,E_1$. On its boundary use the same side rule $(x,t)\mapsto(x,t)$, the same e_i at $t=\delta,1-\delta$, and target $D^3\times[\delta,1-\delta]$. The result $F_\delta$ extends that entire boundary map. Define $F=F_\delta$ on $C_\delta$ and $F(y,t)=(e_i(y),t)$ on the two exterior collars. These maps agree on the complete shared end faces and on the side seams; pasting gives the claimed homeomorphism, with continuous inverse obtained by the same pasting. It is exactly product on those smaller collars. No collar-preserving property is attributed to an arbitrary Alexander extension. [step 2.1, step 2.2, construct]

2.4 **Ambient assembly.** Glue F on C to $H^{-1}$ on T, using a second copy of $D^3$ for the tube ball. They agree on $S^2\times I$. Their union is a homeomorphism $M\to(D^3\cup_{S^2}D^3)\times I=S^3\times I$. It preserves all the prescribed smaller product collars through the specified endpoint pair-ball and complementary-ball charts. In particular the complement parametrizations used for connected-sum gluing are actual parametrizations of the entire complements, compatible on the whole gluing side and at both ends. [step 2.3, construct] ∎

## Remarks

The conclusion assumes an actual open proper tube. It does not construct such a tube around a locally flat annular strip from bare arc straightening. Brown’s printed proof of Theorem 4 ends by citing Theorem 2 for cellularity of a unique exceptional fiber; the actual argument supplied above uses his Theorem 3, proved here as step 1.3. The smaller-collar construction in step 2.3 ensures that no collar-preserving property is attributed to an arbitrary Alexander extension.
