## Example

Assume the Axiom of Choice and supplied projective-resolution data for group
homology. The central quotient
$\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_5)\to\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb F_5)
\cong A_5$ is the binary-icosahedral universal central extension.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The stated choice and resolution hypotheses. Weibel, §6.9,
Example 6.9.1, explicitly identifies
$\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb F_5)\cong A_5$. The proof below calculates
the multiplier and the universal property; it does not use Weibel's
assertion of universality as a proof premise.

[L1] If $P$ is a Sylow subgroup of a finite group $G$, the transfer
identity gives $i_*\operatorname{Tr}=[G:P]$ on $M(G)$
([[lem-corestriction-after-bar-transfer-is-index-multiplication-on-homology]]).
Choice and the supplied-resolution convention give this identity in the
canonical homology groups.

## Verification

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 $A_5$ has order $60$. Its nonidentity conjugacy classes have sizes $20$ (three-cycles), $15$ (products of two disjoint transpositions), and $12,12$ (the two classes of five-cycles). No proper union of these classes with the identity has order dividing $60$, so $A_5$ is simple. It is nonabelian, hence its commutator subgroup is all of $A_5$: $A_5$ is perfect. Its Sylow-$2$ subgroup has order $4$ and cannot be cyclic, since a permutation of order $4$ in $S_5$ is odd; it is $V=C_2\times C_2$. Its Sylow-$3$ and Sylow-$5$ subgroups are cyclic. [given, algebra]

1.2 The quotient $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_5)\to \operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb F_5)\cong A_5$ has central kernel $\{\pm I\}\cong C_2$. The group $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_5)$ is perfect. Indeed, elementary row reduction shows it is generated by the elementary matrices $e_{12}(a),e_{21}(a)$. For $d=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)$, conjugation multiplies the upper parameter by $2^2=4$ and the lower parameter by $2^{-2}=4$. Thus $[d,e_{ij}(a)]=e_{ij}(3a)$ for $i\ne j$; since $3$ is a unit in $\mathbb F_5$, every elementary generator is a commutator. [given, algebra]

2.1 The multiplier $M(A_5)$ is finite and annihilated by $60$. For each prime $p\mid60$, [L1] says that its $p$-primary part is the image of $M(P)$: multiplication by the index $[A_5:P]$, prime to $p$, is an automorphism on the $p$-primary part. The cyclic-group calculation gives $M(P)=0$ for $p=3,5$. For $p=2$, the abelian-multiplier theorem gives $M(V)\cong\bigwedge^2 V\cong C_2$. Consequently $M(A_5)$ is either $0$ or $C_2$. [L1, step 1.1, algebra]

3.1 Choose a free presentation of the perfect group $A_5$. Under Choice, [[thm-free-presentation-construction-has-the-universal-property]] gives its universal central extension $u:U\to A_5$, and the kernel corollary identifies $\ker u$ with $M(A_5)$. If this multiplier were zero, universality would give a homomorphic section $A_5\to\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_5)$ of the central quotient. The latter group would then be $C_2\times A_5$, contradicting its perfectness from step 1.2. Hence $M(A_5)\cong C_2$ by step 2.1. [step 1.1, step 2.1, step 1.2, algebra]

4.1 Universality gives a map $U\to\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_5)$ over $A_5$. Its image, together with the central kernel $C_2$, generates $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_5)$. Because that group is perfect, its commutator subgroup lies in the image, so the map is surjective. Both $U$ and $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_5)$ have order $2\cdot60=120$, so it is an isomorphism over $A_5$. This proves the asserted universal central extension. [step 1.2, step 3.1, algebra] ∎
