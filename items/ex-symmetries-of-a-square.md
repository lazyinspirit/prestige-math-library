## Example

Let $X = \{1,2,3,4\}$ and regard its four elements as the vertices of a square
read in cyclic order, so that the **edges** are the four pairs

$$E \;=\; \bigl\{\, \{1,2\},\ \{2,3\},\ \{3,4\},\ \{4,1\} \,\bigr\}$$

and the two remaining pairs $\{1,3\}$, $\{2,4\}$ are the diagonals. Call a
permutation $f$ of $X$ a **vertex symmetry of the square** when, for all
$x \ne y$ in $X$, $\{x,y\} \in E$ if and only if $\{f(x),f(y)\} \in E$.

Put $r = (1\,2\,3\,4)$ and $s = (1\,3)$ in $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$
([[def-symmetric-group]]) and

$$D \;=\; \{\, \mathrm{id},\ r,\ r^{2},\ r^{3},\ s,\ rs,\ r^{2}s,\ r^{3}s \,\},$$

where juxtaposition is composition and powers are those of [[def-group-power]].
Then:

1. $r^{4} = \mathrm{id}$, $s^{2} = \mathrm{id}$ and $sr = r^{-1}s$;
2. $D$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ ([[def-subgroup]]) whose eight
   listed elements are pairwise distinct, so $|D| = 8$
   ([[def-order-in-a-group]]), and $D = \langle \{r,s\} \rangle$
   ([[def-generated-subgroup]]);
3. $D$ is not abelian: $rs \ne sr$;
4. $D$ is exactly the set of vertex symmetries of the square.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** $X = \{1,2,3,4\}$; $r$ the permutation sending $1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 1$; $s$ the permutation exchanging $1$ and $3$ and fixing $2$ and $4$; $E$ the four edge pairs listed above ([[def-symmetric-group]]).

[L1] $1, 2, 3, 4$ are pairwise distinct natural numbers ([[def-natural-numbers]], [[lem-nat-transitive-irreflexive]]).

[L2] $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ is a group under composition with identity $\mathrm{id}$ ([[lem-symmetric-group-is-a-group]], [[def-group]]); two permutations are equal exactly when they agree at every point ([[def-injection-surjection-bijection]]).

[L3] Exponent laws in a group: $f^{j+k} = f^{j}f^{k}$, $f^{-k} = (f^{k})^{-1}$ and $(f^{j})^{k} = f^{jk}$ ([[lem-group-power-laws]], [[def-group-power]]).

[L4] Cancellation in a group ([[lem-group-cancellation]]).

[L5] One-step test for subgroups ([[lem-subgroup-criterion]], [[def-subgroup]]); $\langle S \rangle$ is the smallest subgroup containing $S$ ([[def-generated-subgroup]]).

[L6] If $\operatorname{ord}(f) = m$ then $f^{0}, \dots, f^{m-1}$ are pairwise distinct and $\langle f \rangle$ has exactly $m$ elements ([[lem-order-characterisation]], [[def-order-in-a-group]]); $|A| = m$ means $A \approx m$ for the unique such natural ([[def-countable]], [[def-equinumerous]]).

## Verification

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 Powers of $r$, computed pointwise: $r^{2}$ sends $1 \mapsto 3$, $2 \mapsto 4$, $3 \mapsto 1$, $4 \mapsto 2$; $r^{3}$ sends $1 \mapsto 4$, $2 \mapsto 1$, $3 \mapsto 2$, $4 \mapsto 3$; and $r^{4}$ sends every point back to itself, so $r^{4} = \mathrm{id}$. None of $r$, $r^{2}$, $r^{3}$ is $\mathrm{id}$, each moving $1$. Hence $\operatorname{ord}(r) = 4$ and $r^{-1} = r^{3}$. [L2, L3, L6, given]

1.2 $s^{2} = \mathrm{id}$, since $s$ exchanges $1$ and $3$ and fixes $2$ and $4$, so applying it twice fixes every point; hence $s^{-1} = s$ and $s \ne \mathrm{id}$. [L2, L3, given]

2.1 $sr = r^{3}s$. Both sides are computed pointwise: $s \circ r$ sends $1 \mapsto r(1) = 2 \mapsto s(2) = 2$, $2 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 1$, $3 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 4$, $4 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 3$; while $r^{3} \circ s$ sends $1 \mapsto s(1) = 3 \mapsto r^{3}(3) = 2$, $2 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 1$, $3 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 4$, $4 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 3$. The two agree at every point. With step 1.1 this gives $sr = r^{-1}s$, which with claim 1's other two equations completes claim 1. [step 1.1, L2, given]

2.2 $s \notin \{\mathrm{id}, r, r^{2}, r^{3}\}$: $s$ fixes $2$, while $r$, $r^{2}$ and $r^{3}$ send $2$ to $3$, $4$ and $1$ respectively, and $s \ne \mathrm{id}$ by step 1.2. [step 1.1, step 1.2, L2]

3.1 By induction from step 2.1, $s r^{k} = r^{-k} s$ for every $k \in \mathbb{Z}$: the case $k=0$ is trivial, and $s r^{k+1} = (s r^{k}) r = r^{-k} s r = r^{-k} r^{-1} s = r^{-(k+1)} s$, while applying $s$ on both sides of $s r^{k} = r^{-k}s$ and using $s^{2} = \mathrm{id}$ gives the statement for $-k$. [step 1.2, step 2.1, L3]

3.2 The eight listed elements are pairwise distinct. The four powers $r^{0}, r^{1}, r^{2}, r^{3}$ are pairwise distinct because $\operatorname{ord}(r) = 4$. If $r^{i}s = r^{j}s$ then $r^{i} = r^{j}$ by cancelling $s$ on the right, so $i = j$ for $i, j$ among $0,1,2,3$. And $r^{i}s = r^{j}$ would give $s = r^{j-i}$, contradicting step 2.2. [step 1.1, step 2.2, L4, L6]

4.1 $D$ is closed under composition. A product of two listed elements has the form $(r^{i}s^{\varepsilon})(r^{j}s^{\delta})$ with $\varepsilon, \delta \in \{0,1\}$. If $\varepsilon = 0$ it equals $r^{i+j}s^{\delta}$; if $\varepsilon = 1$ then, moving $s$ past $r^{j}$ by step 3.1, it equals $r^{i}r^{-j}s\,s^{\delta} = r^{i-j}s^{1+\delta}$. In either case, reducing the exponent of $r$ using $r^{4} = \mathrm{id}$ and the exponent of $s$ using $s^{2} = \mathrm{id}$ gives one of the eight listed elements. [step 1.1, step 1.2, step 3.1, L3]

4.2 $D$ is closed under inverses: $(r^{i})^{-1} = r^{-i}$, again one of the four powers after reduction; and $(r^{i}s)^{-1} = s^{-1}(r^{i})^{-1} = s r^{-i} = r^{i}s$ by step 3.1 and $s^{-1} = s$, so each of the four elements $r^{i}s$ is its own inverse. [step 1.1, step 1.2, step 3.1, L3]

4.3 $D$ is not abelian: $sr = r^{3}s$ by step 2.1, while $rs = r^{1}s$; if these were equal then $r^{3} = r$ by cancelling $s$ on the right, contradicting the distinctness of the powers of $r$. This is claim 3. [step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.2, L4]

5.1 $D$ is a subgroup: it contains $\mathrm{id}$, is closed under composition by step 4.1 and under inverses by step 4.2, so $x y^{-1} \in D$ for $x, y \in D$ and the one-step test applies. Its eight elements are distinct by step 3.2, so the map $8 \to D$ listing them is a bijection and $|D| = 8$. [step 3.2, step 4.1, step 4.2, L5, L6]

6.1 $D = \langle \{r,s\} \rangle$: $D$ is a subgroup containing $r$ and $s$, so $\langle \{r,s\} \rangle \subseteq D$; conversely any subgroup containing $r$ and $s$ contains every $r^{i}s^{\varepsilon}$, hence contains $D$, so $D \subseteq \langle \{r,s\}\rangle$. This with step 5.1 is claim 2. [step 5.1, L5]

7.1 Every element of $D$ is a vertex symmetry. The map $r$ carries the four edges $\{1,2\}, \{2,3\}, \{3,4\}, \{4,1\}$ to $\{2,3\}, \{3,4\}, \{4,1\}, \{1,2\}$, so it maps $E$ onto $E$; being a bijection of $X$, it therefore also carries each of the two non-edges $\{1,3\}$, $\{2,4\}$ to a non-edge, and the "if and only if" holds. The map $s$ carries those four edges to $\{3,2\}, \{2,1\}, \{1,4\}, \{4,3\}$, again onto $E$, so the same applies. The vertex symmetries form a subgroup, since the defining condition is preserved by composition and, being an equivalence, by inverses; hence it contains $\langle \{r,s\}\rangle = D$. [step 6.1, L2, L5, given]

8.1 Conversely let $f$ be a vertex symmetry. The neighbours of a point $x$, meaning the $y$ with $\{x,y\} \in E$, are exactly $r(x)$ and $r^{3}(x)$, and these two are distinct because $r^{2}(x) \ne x$ by step 1.1. There is a unique $i \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ with $r^{i}(1) = f(1)$, since those four values are $1, 2, 3, 4$; put $f_1 = r^{-i}f$, again a vertex symmetry, with $f_1(1) = 1$. [step 1.1, step 5.1, step 7.1, L2, given]

9.1 Since $\{1,2\} \in E$, the pair $\{f_1(1), f_1(2)\} = \{1, f_1(2)\}$ is an edge, so $f_1(2)$ is a neighbour of $1$, that is $f_1(2) \in \{2,4\}$. Since $\{2,3\} \in E$, the point $f_1(3)$ is a neighbour of $f_1(2)$, and it differs from $f_1(1) = 1$ because $f_1$ is injective; the neighbours of $2$ are $1$ and $3$ and the neighbours of $4$ are $3$ and $1$, so in both cases $f_1(3) = 3$. Then $f_1(4)$ is the one element of $X$ not already taken. [step 8.1, L2, given]

10.1 So either $f_1$ is $1 \mapsto 1$, $2 \mapsto 2$, $3 \mapsto 3$, $4 \mapsto 4$, that is $f_1 = \mathrm{id}$ and $f = r^{i}$; or $f_1$ is $1 \mapsto 1$, $2 \mapsto 4$, $3 \mapsto 3$, $4 \mapsto 2$. In the second case $f_1 = r^{2}s$, since $r^{2} \circ s$ sends $1 \mapsto s(1) = 3 \mapsto r^{2}(3) = 1$, $2 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 4$, $3 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 3$ and $4 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 2$; hence $f = r^{i}r^{2}s = r^{i+2}s \in D$. [step 1.1, step 9.1, L2, L3]

11.1 By steps 7.1, 9.1 and 10.1 the vertex symmetries of the square are exactly the elements of $D$, which is claim 4; claims 1, 2 and 3 are steps 2.1, 6.1 and 4.3. [step 2.1, step 6.1, step 4.3, step 7.1, step 9.1, step 10.1] ∎

## Remarks

- **The square is a combinatorial object here, not a geometric one.** The identification of these eight permutations with the rigid motions of a square in the Euclidean plane is not available at this point in the reading order: $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ with its metric comes much later. What is used instead is the edge relation $E$, and claim 4 says the group is exactly the symmetry group of that relation, which is what "vertex permutations of a square" means here.

- **Every element is a rotation or a reflection**, in the sense that $D$ splits as the four powers of $r$ and the four elements $r^{i}s$; step 4.2 shows each of the latter is its own inverse, matching the geometric picture in which a reflection applied twice is the identity.

- The relation $sr = r^{-1}s$ of claim 1 is the whole reason $D$ is closed: it is what lets any word in $r$ and $s$ be pushed into the normal form $r^{i}s^{\varepsilon}$, which is step 4.1. Without it the eight elements would not obviously be all of $\langle \{r,s\}\rangle$.
