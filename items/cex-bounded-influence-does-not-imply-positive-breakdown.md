## Statement refuted

At a probability law $F$, finite gross-error sensitivity forces the empirical plug-in statistic $T(P_n)$ to have replacement breakdown bounded below by a positive constant as $n\to\infty$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Countable Choice, the Borel probability law $P(A):=\lambda(A\cap[-1,1])/2$ on $\mathbb R$, and the functional and statistics defined in the Counterexample below. Here $\lambda$ is one-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

[F1] [[def-borel-sigma-algebra]]: $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$ is the sigma-algebra generated by the open subsets of $\mathbb R$.

[F2] [[def-probability-measure]]: a probability measure is a countably additive measure of total mass one.

[F3] [[def-lebesgue-measure-and-the-lebesgue-sigma-algebra]]: $\lambda$ is the Lebesgue measure set function on the Lebesgue measurable subsets of $\mathbb R$.

[F4] [[thm-lebesgue-measure-is-a-complete-measure]]: under Countable Choice, Lebesgue measurable sets form a sigma-algebra and $\lambda$ is a complete measure on it.

[F5] [[thm-lebesgue-measure-of-a-box-of-every-kind]]: under Countable Choice, every interval $[a,b]$ is Borel/Lebesgue measurable with $\lambda([a,b])=b-a$, including degenerate intervals.

[A1] [[def-countable-choice]]: Countable Choice is the hypothesis of [F4], [F5], and [F14], supplying the measure, interval-volume, and Borel-measurability properties of $\lambda$ used to construct $P$.

[F6] [[def-lower-median-functional]]: $m(G)$ is the lower median of a law in its domain.

[F7] [[lem-finite-empirical-measure-is-a-probability-law]]: $P_n=n^{-1}\sum_i\delta_{x_i}$ is a probability measure for every finite sample.

[F8] [[def-statistical-functional-and-influence-function]]: the gross-error influence function is the right derivative along a point-contamination path.

[F9] [[def-gross-error-path]]: $P_{\epsilon,z}=(1-\epsilon)P+\epsilon\delta_z$ is the point-contamination path.

[F10] [[def-gross-error-sensitivity]]: sensitivity is the supremum of absolute influence values over the directions where they exist.

[F11] [[def-finite-sample-replacement-breakdown]]: the breakdown fraction is the first replacement count at which the maximum displacement is unbounded, divided by $n$.

[F12] [[thm-median-influence-function]]: at a continuous positive density at the median, the median's right influence is $-1/(2f(m))$ below $m$, $+1/(2f(m))$ above $m$, and $0$ at $m$.

[F13] [[lem-lower-median-of-odd-empirical-sample-is-middle-order-statistic]]: for an odd empirical sample, its lower median is the middle order statistic.

[F14] Under Countable Choice, every Borel subset of $\mathbb R$ is Lebesgue measurable ([[thm-borel-sets-are-lebesgue-measurable]]).

## Counterexample

Let $\mathcal P_c$ be the Borel probability laws on $\mathbb R$ for which $G([a,b])=1$ for some finite $a\le b$. Then $F_G(t)=0$ for $t<a$ and $F_G(t)=1$ for $t\ge b$, so [F6] defines $m(G)$. For $G\in\mathcal P_c$, call $z$ an atom location when $G(\{z\})>0$. Define

$$T(G):=\begin{cases}\sup\{z:G(\{z\})>0\},&\text{if there are two distinct atom locations }z_1,z_2\text{ with }|z_i-m(G)|>2,\\m(G),&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

The supremum is finite in the first case because every atom location lies in an interval carrying $G$. For a sample $x\in\mathbb R^n$, let $P_n$ be its empirical law from [F7] and set $T_n(x):=T(P_n)$.

1.1 By [F14], every Borel set is Lebesgue measurable; since [F5] makes $[-1,1]$ Borel and Lebesgue measurable with measure $2$, each $A\cap[-1,1]$ is measurable for Borel $A$. For pairwise disjoint Borel sets $A_j$, these intersections are disjoint, so countable additivity of $\lambda$ gives countable additivity of $P(A)=\lambda(A\cap[-1,1])/2$. Also $P(\varnothing)=0$ because $\lambda(\varnothing)=0$, and $P(\mathbb R)=1$ by [F5]. Thus [F1] and [F2] make $P$ a Borel probability law. For $-1\le t\le1$, [F5] gives $F_P(t)=P((-\infty,t])=(t+1)/2$; for $t<-1$ the intersection $(-\infty,t]\cap[-1,1]$ is empty. It follows that $m(P)=0$, the density near zero is $f=1/2$, and $P$ has no atoms, the latter by the degenerate-interval case of [F5]. [A1, F1, F2, F3, F4, F5, F6, F14, algebra]

1.2 Fix any point $z\in\mathbb R$. By [F9], $P_{\epsilon,z}$ is carried by a bounded interval containing $[-1,1]$ and $z$, and its only possible atom location is $z$, because [F5] gives $P(\{z\})=0$. The trigger in $T$ requires two distinct atom locations, so it is false and $T(P_{\epsilon,z})=m(P_{\epsilon,z})$ for all sufficiently small $\epsilon\ge0$. Therefore [F8] and [F12] give $\operatorname{IF}(z;T,P)=\operatorname{IF}(z;m,P)$, equal to $-1$ for $z<0$, $+1$ for $z>0$, and $0$ for $z=0$. [F5, F6, F8, F9, F12, given, algebra]

1.3 Fix odd $n=2r+1\ge5$ and a clean sample $x\in[-1,1]^n$. Its empirical median is the middle order statistic by [F13] and lies in $[-1,1]$, so every atom location of $P_n$ is within distance $2$ of that median. The trigger is false and $T_n(x)$ is the sample median. [F6, F7, F13, given, algebra]

1.4 After at most one replacement, at least $n-1=2r$ observations remain in $[-1,1]$. At least $r+1$ resulting observations are at most $1$ and at least $r+1$ are at least $-1$, so the middle order statistic, hence the lower empirical median by [F13], remains in $[-1,1]$. All unchanged atom locations are within distance $2$ of it, and only the single replacement can add a distinct outlying atom. Thus the trigger is still false and $T_n$ remains the sample median in $[-1,1]$ for every one-coordinate replacement; its maximum displacement is bounded. [F6, F7, F13, given, algebra]

2.1 The absolute values in step 1.2 have supremum $1$ over $z\in\mathbb R$, so [F10] gives $\gamma^*(T,P)=1<\infty$. [F10, step 1.2, algebra]

2.2 Replace two coordinates by distinct values $M$ and $2M$ with $M>3$. The remaining $2r-1$ observations lie in $[-1,1]$, so the middle order statistic (position $r+1$) is still one of them and lies in $[-1,1]$; by [F13] this is the lower empirical median. Both new atom locations are more than $2$ above that median, so the trigger is true and $T_n=2M$. Letting $M\to+\infty$ makes the maximum displacement unbounded. Since step 1.4 ruled out one replacement and [F11] defines the first unbounded count, the breakdown fraction is exactly $2/n$. [F6, F7, F11, F13, step 1.4, given, algebra]

3.1 Along odd $n\to\infty$, $2/n\to0$, while step 2.1 gives finite gross-error sensitivity at the fixed law $P$. Hence this functional has bounded influence and an empirical plug-in statistic whose replacement breakdown is not bounded away from zero, refuting the Statement. [step 2.1, step 2.2, algebra] ∎
