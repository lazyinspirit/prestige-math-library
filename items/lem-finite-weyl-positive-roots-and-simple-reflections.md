## Statement

For the preceding finite reduced crystallographic root system, regular vectors $t$ exist, $W$ is finite, and the simple positive roots are a basis of $E$. Every root has integral coordinates in this basis of one sign. Each $s_i$ permutes $\Phi^+\setminus\{\alpha_i\}$ and sends $\alpha_i$ to $-\alpha_i$. The simple reflections generate $W$, and every root is the image of a simple root under their group. The simple coroots similarly form an integral basis of the coroot group, and $P$ is the lattice generated by their dual basis; $W$ preserves $Q,Q^\vee,P$. All assertions are choice-free, including rank zero.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The finite Euclidean root system and its conventions.

[F1] All root, reflection, positivity and lattice definitions are those of [[def-finite-weyl-root-system-lattice-and-chamber-conventions]].

## Proof

1.1 Choose any finite basis $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ of $E$. For each nonzero root $\alpha$, the polynomial $\sum_{j=1}^r(e_j,\alpha)z^{j-1}$ is nonzero, since nondegeneracy and spanning prevent all its coefficients vanishing. A nonzero degree-$d$ real polynomial has at most $d$ roots: division by $z-a$ at a zero and induction prove this assertion. Finitely many such polynomials therefore have only finitely many forbidden real values. Choose one other value $z$ and take $t=\sum_j z^{j-1}e_j$. In rank zero take $t=0$. Also $W$ acts on the finite spanning set $\Phi$ and an operator fixing every root is the identity; this injects $W$ into a finite permutation group. Hence $W$ is finite. [F1, given, algebra]

1.2 If independent roots $\alpha,\beta$ have $(\alpha,\beta)<0$, the positive integers $m=-2(\alpha,\beta)/(\alpha,\alpha)$ and $n=-2(\alpha,\beta)/(\beta,\beta)$ obey $mn<4$, by the strict Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. The latter follows directly by minimizing $(\alpha-u\beta,\alpha-u\beta)>0$ over real $u$. Thus at least one of $m,n$ equals one. The corresponding root reflection produces $\alpha+\beta$, proving it is a root. If distinct simple positive roots had positive inner product, apply this result to one and the negative of the other. Their difference would be a root; whichever sign it has expresses one of the simple roots as a sum of two positive roots, a contradiction. Distinct simple roots therefore have nonpositive inner products. [F1, given, algebra]

2.1 Every positive root is a sum of simple roots with nonnegative integer coefficients. If not simple, split it into two positive roots. Both have smaller $t$-value, and induction on the position in the finite ordered list of positive $t$-values terminates the splitting. Thus the simple roots span $E$. They are independent: in a nontrivial real relation separate the positive and negative coefficients to obtain $v=\sum_{i\in I}a_i\alpha_i=\sum_{j\in J}b_j\alpha_j$ with disjoint index sets and strictly positive coefficients. Neither side can be empty, since pairing with $t$ would then give a contradiction; it also shows $v\ne0$. Step 1.2 gives $(v,v)=\sum_{i,j}a_i b_j(\alpha_i,\alpha_j)\le0$, contradicting positive definiteness. Hence they are a basis. Negating gives the negative-root coordinates as well. [step 1.2, F1, algebra]

3.1 For $\beta\in\Phi^+\setminus\{\alpha_i\}$, its simple expansion has a positive coefficient at some index other than $i$: otherwise reducedness would make $\beta=\alpha_i$. Reflection $s_i$ changes only the coefficient at $i$. Its image is a root and still has that other positive coefficient, so step 2.1 makes every coefficient nonnegative. Thus $s_i\beta$ is positive. The involution $s_i$ then permutes all these positive roots and reverses $\alpha_i$. [step 2.1, F1, algebra]

3.2 The coroot set $\Phi^\vee$ is a finite reduced crystallographic root system: $s_\alpha\beta^\vee=(s_\alpha\beta)^\vee$, its double dual is $\Phi$, and its two integer pairings interchange those of $\Phi$. Its positive roots are positive scalar multiples of the positive roots of $\Phi$, and every positive coroot has nonnegative real coordinates in the basis $\alpha_i^\vee$. Apply step 2.1's simple-basis conclusion to this dual system. The cone spanned by its positive roots is exactly the cone generated by the $\alpha_i^\vee$, since those coroots themselves belong to it. The extreme rays of a cone spanned by a basis are exactly its basis rays: a vector with two positive coordinates splits into two nonproportional cone vectors, whereas a single-coordinate vector cannot. The dual simple roots must therefore lie on exactly these rays. Reducedness gives that they are precisely the $\alpha_i^\vee$. Their integer positive expansions give $Q^\vee=\bigoplus_i\mathbb Z\alpha_i^\vee$. [step 2.1, F1, algebra]

4.1 Suppose a positive root $\beta=\sum_j n_j\alpha_j$ is not simple. Since $(\beta,\beta)=\sum_j n_j(\beta,\alpha_j)>0$, some $(\beta,\alpha_i)>0$. Its positive integral coroot pairing means $s_i\beta=\beta-m\alpha_i$ with an integer $m>0$. By step 3.1 this root stays positive and has smaller integer height $\sum_j n_j$. Repeating finitely many times reaches a simple root. Therefore every positive root is in the group orbit of a simple root; a negative root is obtained by first negating that simple root with its reflection. For any orthogonal $u$, direct substitution gives $s_{u\alpha}=u s_\alpha u^{-1}$. Consequently every root reflection lies in the group generated by the $s_i$, proving that this group is $W$. [step 2.1, step 3.1, F1, algebra]

5.1 Let $\omega_i$ be the basis dual to $\alpha_i^\vee$ under the nondegenerate form. Step 3.2 implies $P=\bigoplus_i\mathbb Z\omega_i$. Reflection invariance of the root and coroot sets gives invariance of $Q,Q^\vee$. If $\lambda\in P$ and $w\in W$, then $(w\lambda,\alpha^\vee)=(\lambda,w^{-1}\alpha^\vee)\in\mathbb Z$; hence $W$ preserves $P$. Every basis and root list used was finite. In rank zero all lists are empty, their groups are zero and $W$ is trivial, so the same conclusions hold. [step 1.1, step 3.2, F1, algebra] ∎
