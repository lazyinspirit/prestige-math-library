## Example

Every field $K$ ([[def-field]]) is a Noetherian ring, and so is $\mathbb Z$.
Consequently $K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ and $\mathbb Z[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ are Noetherian
for every $n\in\mathbb N$
([[cor-finite-variable-polynomial-ring-noetherian]]).

What is asserted is that every ideal of each of these rings is finitely
generated. No description of the ideals of $K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ or
$\mathbb Z[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is claimed, and for $n\ge2$ none is available from
this argument.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A field $K$, the ring $\mathbb Z$ of integers, and $n\in\mathbb N$.

[L1] A **field** is a set $F$ with two operations and distinguished elements $0\ne1$ such that $(F,+)$ is an abelian group, multiplication is associative and commutative on all of $F$ with $x\cdot1=x$, every $x\ne0$ has a multiplicative inverse, and multiplication distributes over addition ([[def-field]]).

[L2] Every subgroup $H\le(\mathbb Z,+)$ equals $n\mathbb Z=\langle n\rangle$ for exactly one natural number $n$; in particular every subgroup of $(\mathbb Z,+)$ is cyclic ([[lem-subgroups-of-z-are-cyclic]]).

[L3] For $S\subseteq R$, $(S)$ is the intersection of all two-sided ideals containing $S$; $(\{a\})$ is written $(a)$ and is called principal ([[def-generated-and-principal-ideals]]).

[L4] An ideal of a ring is in particular an additive subgroup of it ([[def-left-right-and-two-sided-ideal]]).

[L5] In a commutative ring, $(S)$ consists of finite sums $\sum r_is_i$, and $(a)=Ra$ ([[thm-generated-ideal-description-in-a-commutative-ring]]).

[L6] For a commutative ring, being Noetherian is equivalent to every ideal being finitely generated ([[thm-noetherian-ring-ideal-characterisations]]).

[L7] If $R$ is a Noetherian commutative ring then $R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is Noetherian for every $n\in\mathbb N$ ([[cor-finite-variable-polynomial-ring-noetherian]]).

## Verification

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 Let $I$ be an ideal of a field $K$. If $I=\{0\}$ then $I=(0)$. Otherwise $I$ contains some $a\ne0$, which has an inverse in $K$, so $1=a^{-1}a\in I$ and hence $r=r\cdot1\in I$ for every $r\in K$, giving $I=K=(1)$. Either way $I$ is generated by one element, so every ideal of $K$ is finitely generated and $K$ is Noetherian. [L1, L3, L5, L6, given]

1.2 Let $I$ be an ideal of $\mathbb Z$. It is an additive subgroup of $(\mathbb Z,+)$, so it equals $m\mathbb Z$ for some natural number $m$; and $m\mathbb Z=\mathbb Z m=(m)$. So every ideal of $\mathbb Z$ is principal, hence finitely generated, and $\mathbb Z$ is Noetherian. [L2, L3, L4, L5, L6, given]

2.1 Since $K$ and $\mathbb Z$ are Noetherian commutative rings, $K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ and $\mathbb Z[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ are Noetherian for every $n\in\mathbb N$, the case $n=0$ returning the base rings themselves. [L7, step 1.1, step 1.2] ∎

## Remarks

- **A field is Noetherian for a reason that says nothing about size.** The argument in step 1.1 uses only invertibility of nonzero elements, so it applies to $\mathbb Q$, to $\mathbb R$ and to a field with infinite transcendence degree over its prime subfield alike.

- **The conclusion is finite generation, not principality.** For $n\ge2$ the ideal generated by $x_1$ and $x_2$ in $K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is not principal, and the Noetherian condition does not claim otherwise.
