## Statement

Work in ZF. Let $A$ be a normal finite-type integral domain over a field $k$, let $F=\operatorname{Frac}(A)$, and let $T\subseteq A\setminus\{0\}$ be any multiplicative set. Inside $F$,
$$A_T=\bigcap_{\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak q=1,\;\mathfrak q\cap T=\varnothing}A_{\mathfrak q}.$$
For a field the empty intersection is $F$. Consequently, on a normal integral scheme essentially of finite type over a field, a Cartier divisor is effective if and only if all its height-one orders are nonnegative. It is zero if all its height-one orders are zero. These are actual local regularity/effectivity tests, without an arbitrary-ring normality or $(S_2)$ theorem.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The field, normal finite-type domain, fraction field and multiplicative set in the Statement.

[F1] Noether normalization makes a nonzero finite-type algebra finite over a polynomial subalgebra. [[cor-noether-normalisation-module-finiteness]]

[F2] A finitely generated torsion-free module over a PID is finite free. [[cor-finitely-generated-torsion-free-modules-over-a-pid-are-free]]

[F3] Finite-variable polynomial rings over a field are UFDs, and their height-one primes are generated by irreducible elements. [[lem-finite-variable-polynomial-rings-over-fields-are-ufds]]

[F4] The Chinese remainder theorem holds for a finite family of comaximal ideals. [[thm-chinese-remainder-theorem-for-comaximal-ideals]]

## Proof

1.1 By [F1], choose a polynomial subalgebra $R=k[z_1,\ldots,z_d]\subseteq A$ over which $A$ is finite, and put $L=\operatorname{Frac}(R)$. Then $F/L$ is finite. Moreover $A$ is exactly the integral closure of $R$ in $F$: every element of $A$ is integral over $R$, and an element integral over $R$ is integral over $A$ and hence belongs to the normal ring $A$. If $d=0$, $A$ is a finite-dimensional domain over a field and therefore is itself the field $F$, so the assertion is immediate. Assume $d>0$ below. [F1, given, algebra]

2.1 At a height-one prime $\mathfrak p=(h)$ of $R$, the ring $R_{\mathfrak p}$ is a DVR: factor out the power of the irreducible $h$ in a rational function. The ring $S=A\otimes_RR_{\mathfrak p}$ is finite free over it by [F2]. Its generic localization is the field $F$, and every maximal ideal lies over $(h)$; a field integral over the image of $R_{\mathfrak p}$ cannot have that image a nonfield. The maximal ideals form a finite set, since $S/hS$ is finite-dimensional over $\kappa(\mathfrak p)$ and [F4] bounds their number by that dimension. Each corresponding prime $\mathfrak q$ of $A$ has height one: no strict prime chain lies over $\mathfrak p$, since $S/hS$ is finite-dimensional. A smaller nonzero prime would therefore contract below $\mathfrak p$, hence to zero, and localizing at the nonzero elements of $R$ would give a nonzero prime of the field $F$. Its local ring is a DVR by the finite ideal argument for a normal one-dimensional Noetherian local domain. Explicitly, choose $0\ne b\in\mathfrak m$, the least $r$ with $\mathfrak m^r\subseteq(b)$ and $x\in\mathfrak m^{r-1}\setminus(b)$. For $z=x/b$, the inclusion $z\mathfrak m\subseteq\mathfrak m$ would make $z$ integral by the determinant trick; otherwise some $y\in\mathfrak m$ has $zy$ a unit and $\mathfrak m=yS_{\mathfrak q}$. The finitely generated intersection of its powers is zero by the same determinant trick, giving the valuation. Normality commutes with the localizations here by clearing a finite monic equation. [F2, F3, F4, step 1.1, algebra]

3.1 An element $x\in F$ belongs to $S$ if it belongs to all these finitely many $S_{\mathfrak q}$. To prove this directly, let $J=\{a\in S:ax\in S\}$. If $x\notin S$, the nonzero module $S/J$ is finite over the local ring $R_{\mathfrak p}$, and its reduction modulo $h$ is nonzero by finite Nakayama. A nonzero quotient of least positive vector-space dimension of this finite-dimensional residue algebra is a field. It lifts to a maximal ideal $\mathfrak q$ of $S$ containing $J$. But membership of $x$ in $S_{\mathfrak q}$ would give a denominator in $J\setminus\mathfrak q$, a contradiction. This proves the test without selecting maximal ideals in arbitrary rings. [step 2.1, algebra]

4.1 Suppose first $x$ is regular at every height-one prime of $A$. Steps 2.1–3.1 show that multiplication by $x$ preserves $A\otimes_RR_{\mathfrak p}$ for every height-one $\mathfrak p$ of $R$. In a finite free basis its characteristic polynomial therefore has coefficients in $R_{\mathfrak p}$. This polynomial is independent of that basis and is the characteristic polynomial of multiplication on $F/L$. A fraction in $L$ lying in all these polynomial DVRs belongs to $R$, by [F3] and cancellation of its finite irreducible denominator factors. The characteristic polynomial is thus monic over $R$; the finite adjugate identity gives its Cayley–Hamilton equation on $x$. Hence $x$ is integral over $R$ and lies in $A$ by step 1.1. This proves the unlocalized assertion. [F3, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.1, algebra]

5.1 Now assume $x$ is regular at every height-one prime avoiding $T$. The characteristic polynomial of multiplication by $x$ has only finitely many irreducible denominator factors in $R$. At a polynomial height-one prime not among them, $x$ is integral over $R_{\mathfrak p}$ and thus belongs to every normal $A_{\mathfrak q}$ above it. Above each of the finitely many remaining primes there are only finitely many DVRs from step 2.1. Any one where $x$ has negative valuation must meet $T$, by the hypothesis and its height-one property. Choose one element of that intersection for each such DVR, multiply these finitely many elements, and raise their product $t\in T$ to a sufficiently large finite power to remove every negative valuation on this list. Since $t\in A$, it introduces no negative valuation elsewhere. Steps 3.1–4.1 then applied to $t^Nx$ show it belongs to $A$: its multiplication polynomial is integral at every polynomial height-one prime. Thus $x\in A_T$. Conversely every element of $A_T$ is regular at the stated primes. This proves the displayed intersection formula for arbitrary $T$, using only a finite list of its elements. [F3, step 2.1, step 3.1, step 4.1, algebra]

6.1 On a normal height-one local ring, the finite ideal argument in step 2.1 gives a DVR, so a local equation $f$ of a Cartier divisor has nonnegative order exactly when $f$ belongs to that DVR. Apply step 5.1 on each essentially finite-type affine chart: nonnegative orders imply that every local equation is regular, and its nonzero value in the fraction field makes it a nonzero divisor. This is precisely effectivity. If every order is zero, both $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are regular and $f$ is a unit, so the divisor is zero. The reverse assertions follow in the local DVRs. The equations already glue by units, and no simultaneous choice of local parameters or infinite prime list was used. [step 2.1, step 4.1, step 5.1, algebra] ∎
