## Statement

Let $K$ and $L$ be number fields inside a common algebraic closure, with
compositum $KL$, let $[KL:\mathbb Q]=[K:\mathbb Q]\,[L:\mathbb Q]$, let
$\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m$ and $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n$ be integral bases of
$\mathcal O_K$ and $\mathcal O_L$, and suppose $\gcd(d_K,d_L)=1$. Then

$$\mathcal O_{KL}=\mathcal O_K\mathcal O_L,$$

the products $\alpha_i\beta_j$ form an integral basis of $\mathcal O_{KL}$, and

$$d_{KL}=d_K^{[L:\mathbb Q]}\,d_L^{[K:\mathbb Q]}.$$

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Number fields $K,L$ inside a fixed algebraic closure of $\mathbb Q$, with $m:=[K:\mathbb Q]$, $n:=[L:\mathbb Q]$, compositum $KL$, $[KL:\mathbb Q]=mn$, integral bases $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m$ of $\mathcal O_K$ and $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n$ of $\mathcal O_L$, and $\gcd(d_K,d_L)=1$.

[F1] $\mathcal O_K$ and $\mathcal O_L$ are free $\mathbb Z$-modules of ranks $m$ and $n$, freely generated by the given integral bases ([[thm-ring-of-integers-free-of-rank-degree]], [[def-integral-basis-and-power-integral-basis]]); $\mathcal O_{KL}$ is free of rank $mn$.

[F2] Degrees multiply in a tower and equal the dimension of the top field over the bottom one ([[thm-tower-law-for-finite-field-extensions]], [[def-extension-degree-and-finite-extension]]): $[KL:L]=m$ and $[KL:K]=n$ because $[KL:\mathbb Q]=[KL:L]\,[L:\mathbb Q]$ and $[L:\mathbb Q]=n$.

[F3] The algebraic integers form a subring of $\mathbb C$ ([[cor-integral-elements-form-a-subring]], [[def-integral-element-and-algebraic-integer]]); if $\gamma$ is a root of a monic polynomial in $\mathbb Z[t]$ then so is $u(\gamma)$ for every field homomorphism $u$ fixing $\mathbb Z$, because $u$ commutes with the polynomial expression.

[F4] $\mathcal O_L$ is the integral closure of $\mathbb Z$ in $L$ ([[def-ring-of-integers-of-a-number-field]], [[def-integral-closure-and-integrally-closed-domain]]), and it is integrally closed in $L$ ([[thm-integral-closure-is-integrally-closed]]). Hence $L\cap\overline{\mathbb Z}=\mathcal O_L$, an element of $\mathcal O_L$ has integer coordinates in the integral basis $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n$, and a rational algebraic integer is an integer ([[cor-rational-algebraic-integers-are-integers]]).

[F5] For a number field $F$ of degree $r$ a $\mathbb Q$-basis $x_1,\dots,x_r$ has discriminant $\operatorname{disc}(x_1,\dots,x_r)=\det(\sigma_i(x_j))^2\ne0$, the determinant being taken over the $r$ distinct embeddings $F\to\mathbb C$ ([[thm-discriminant-as-an-embedding-determinant]]); for an integral basis this number is the well-defined nonzero integer $d_F$ ([[def-discriminant-of-a-number-field-basis-and-order]], [[thm-number-field-discriminant-is-well-defined-and-nonzero]]). In particular $d_K=D^2$ for $D=\det(\sigma_k(\alpha_i))$ and $d_K,d_L\ne0$.

[F6] If $(u_1,\dots,u_r)$ is an $F$-basis of $K$ and $(v_1,\dots,v_s)$ is a $K$-basis of $L$, then the products $u_iv_j$ form an $F$-basis of $L$ ([[lem-product-basis-for-a-tower-of-finite-extensions]]).

[F7] If an $m$-dimensional vector space has a spanning list of $m$ vectors, then that list is a basis: a dependence lets one vector be solved for in terms of the others, leaving an $(m-1)$-element spanning set, which cannot span a space containing an independent $m$-element basis by the finite-bound corollary. A basis is an independent spanning set ([[cor-independent-set-is-no-larger-than-a-finite-spanning-set]], [[def-linear-basis]]).

[F8] If a positive integer $r$ and a finite list of integers $a_{ij}$ have no common prime divisor, then repeated applications of integer Bézout give integers $c,b_{ij}$ with $cr+\sum_{i,j}b_{ij}a_{ij}=1$. Also, $\gcd(d_K,d_L)=1$ gives integers $u,v$ with $u\,d_K+v\,d_L=1$ ([[thm-bezout-identity]]).

[F9] $\mathbb Q$ has characteristic zero and is perfect ([[cor-fields-of-characteristic-zero-and-finite-fields-are-perfect]]), so the finite extension $K/\mathbb Q$ is simple: $K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ for some $\alpha$, with minimal polynomial $f\in\mathbb Q[t]$ of degree $m$ and $K\cong\mathbb Q[t]/(f)$ ([[cor-finite-extension-of-a-perfect-field-is-simple]], [[thm-evaluation-kernel-and-minimal-polynomial]]).

[F10] $\det(S\circ T)=\det(S)\det(T)$ for endomorphisms of a finite-dimensional space ([[cor-determinant-multiplicativity-from-the-top-exterior-power]]), and simultaneous reordering of the rows and columns of a square matrix does not change its determinant, being a similarity by a permutation matrix ([[cor-determinant-is-invariant-under-similarity]]). The determinant of a matrix is given by the Leibniz sum $\sum_{\pi}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\prod_i a_{\pi(i),i}$ ([[def-determinant-of-a-square-matrix]]).

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 By [F1] the given bases are $\mathbb Q$-bases of $K$ and $L$, and [F2] gives $[KL:L]=m$ and $[KL:K]=n$. [F1, F2, given]

1.2 Every embedding $\sigma:K\to\mathbb C$ extends to an embedding $\widetilde\sigma:KL\to\mathbb C$ with $\widetilde\sigma|_L=\mathrm{id}_L$. Indeed, by [F9] write $K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ with minimal polynomial $f$ of degree $m$; then $KL=L(\alpha)$, and $[L(\alpha):L]=m=\deg f$ shows that $f$ has no factor of intermediate degree over $L$, so $f$ is irreducible over $L$ and $KL\cong L[t]/(f)$ as $L$-algebras. The evaluation homomorphism $L[t]\to\mathbb C$, $t\mapsto\sigma(\alpha)$, kills $f$ because $\sigma$ fixes $\mathbb Q$ and $f(\alpha)=0$; it therefore induces the required $\widetilde\sigma$ under the isomorphism $L[t]/(f)\cong KL$. [F2, F9, given]

2.1 Put $V:=\sum_{i=1}^m L\alpha_i\subseteq KL$. Since $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m$ are a $\mathbb Q$-basis of $K$, each product $\alpha_i\alpha_j$ is a $\mathbb Q$-linear combination of them; the same structure constants lie in $L$, so $V$ is closed under multiplication. Also $1\in V$, and $V$ contains both $L$ and $K$. It is finite-dimensional over $L$. For each nonzero $x\in V$, multiplication by $x$ is an injective $L$-linear map $V\to V$, because $V$ lies in the field $KL$; finite dimensionality makes the map surjective, so some $y\in V$ satisfies $xy=1$. Thus $V$ is a field containing $K$ and $L$, hence $V=KL$. By [F2] it has $L$-dimension $m$. Its $m$ elements $\alpha_i$ span $V$; if they were dependent, a nonzero coefficient in a dependence relation could be inverted in $L$ to express one $\alpha_i$ in terms of the others, so the remaining $m-1$ vectors would still span $V$. But an $m$-element basis of $V$ is an independent subset of a space with that $(m-1)$-element spanning set, contradicting [F7]. Thus the $\alpha_i$ are independent and spanning, hence an $L$-basis by [F7]. The symmetric argument with $W:=\sum_{j=1}^n K\beta_j$ shows that $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n$ form a $K$-basis of $KL$ by the same finite argument. [F7, F2, step 1.1, given]

3.1 Applying [F6] to the tower $\mathbb Q\subseteq L\subseteq KL$ with the $\mathbb Q$-basis $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n$ of $L$ and the $L$-basis $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m$ of $KL$, the products $\alpha_i\beta_j$ form a $\mathbb Q$-basis of $KL$; in particular they are $\mathbb Q$-linearly independent and there are $mn$ of them. [F6, step 2.1]

3.2 Let $\gamma\in\mathcal O_{KL}$. By step 2.1 there are unique $x_1,\dots,x_m\in L$ with $\gamma=\sum_ix_i\alpha_i$. Each $x_i$ lies in $L=\operatorname{Frac}(\mathcal O_L)$, so there are integers $a_{ij}$ and a positive integer $r$ with $x_i=\sum_j(a_{ij}/r)\beta_j$; dividing out common factors, we may assume that no prime divides $r$ and all $a_{ij}$ simultaneously. [step 2.1, F4, given]

4.1 Let $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_m$ be the distinct embeddings of $K$ into $\mathbb C$ and let $\widetilde\sigma_k$ extend $\sigma_k$ as in step 1.2. Applying $\widetilde\sigma_k$ to $\gamma=\sum_ix_i\alpha_i$ and using $\widetilde\sigma_k|_L=\mathrm{id}$ together with $x_i\in L$ gives the linear system $\sum_i\sigma_k(\alpha_i)x_i=\widetilde\sigma_k(\gamma)$, $k=1,\dots,m$. Its coefficient matrix $M:=(\sigma_k(\alpha_i))$ has determinant $D$ with $D^2=d_K\ne0$ by [F5]; the entries $\sigma_k(\alpha_i)$ of $M$ are algebraic integers, and so are the entries $\widetilde\sigma_k(\gamma)$, by [F3] applied to the integral elements $\alpha_i\in\mathcal O_K$ and $\gamma\in\mathcal O_{KL}$. [F3, F5, step 3.2, step 1.2]

5.1 Cramer's rule applied to the system of step 4.1 gives $D\,x_i=D_i$, where $D_i$ is the determinant of a matrix with algebraic-integer entries; hence $D$ and every $D_i$ are algebraic integers by [F3], and $\Delta x_i=D\cdot D_i$ is an algebraic integer, where $\Delta:=D^2=d_K$ is a nonzero integer. [F3, F5, F10, step 4.1]

6.1 By step 3.2, $\Delta x_i=\sum_j(\Delta a_{ij}/r)\beta_j$ lies in $L$, so $\Delta x_i\in L\cap\overline{\mathbb Z}=\mathcal O_L$ by step 5.1 and [F4]. As $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n$ is an integral basis, the rational numbers $\Delta a_{ij}/r$ are integers; equivalently $r\mid \Delta a_{ij}=d_Ka_{ij}$ for all $i,j$. [F4, step 3.2, step 5.1]

7.1 The denominator was reduced in step 3.2, so no prime divides $r$ and every $a_{ij}$; therefore their common gcd is $1$. By [F8], choose integers $c,b_{ij}$ with $cr+\sum_{i,j}b_{ij}a_{ij}=1$. Multiplying by $d_K$ and using $r\mid d_Ka_{ij}$ from step 6.1 shows that every term on the left is divisible by $r$, hence $r\mid d_K$. [F8, step 3.2, step 6.1]

8.1 Regroup the same expansion from step 3.2 as $\gamma=\sum_j y_j\beta_j$, where $y_j=\sum_i(a_{ij}/r)\alpha_i\in K$. The symmetric trace-matrix argument, now using the $K$-basis $\alpha_i$ and the $L$-basis $\beta_j$ from step 2.1, gives $r\mid d_La_{ij}$ for every same coefficient $a_{ij}$. The same reduced-denominator identity of [F8] therefore gives $r\mid d_L$. Together with step 7.1 and $\gcd(d_K,d_L)=1$, this implies $r=1$. Thus $\gamma=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\alpha_i\beta_j\in\mathcal O_K\mathcal O_L$ for every $\gamma\in\mathcal O_{KL}$, proving $\mathcal O_{KL}\subseteq\mathcal O_K\mathcal O_L$. [F8, step 3.2, step 2.1, step 6.1, step 7.1, given]

9.1 Conversely $\mathcal O_K\mathcal O_L\subseteq\mathcal O_{KL}$: products and sums of integral elements are integral by [F3], and they lie in $KL$. With step 8.1 this gives $\mathcal O_{KL}=\mathcal O_K\mathcal O_L$, and since the products $\alpha_i\beta_j$ are $\mathbb Q$-linearly independent by step 3.1 and span $\mathcal O_K\mathcal O_L$ over $\mathbb Z$ by construction, they form an integral basis of $\mathcal O_{KL}$. [F3, step 3.1, step 8.1]

10.1 The restrictions of the $mn$ distinct embeddings of $KL$ into $\mathbb C$ to $K$ and to $L$ define a map $\operatorname{Emb}(KL)\to\operatorname{Emb}(K)\times\operatorname{Emb}(L)$; it is injective, because an embedding of $KL$ is determined by its restrictions to $K$ and to $L$, which together generate $KL$ over $\mathbb Q$. Both sides have $mn$ elements by [F5], so the map is a bijection. Order rows by pairs $(k,l)$ and columns by pairs $(i,j)$, with the first index slow in each order. The embedding matrix of the integral basis $\alpha_i\beta_j$ of step 9.1 then has entries $\sigma_k(\alpha_i)\tau_l(\beta_j)$. [F5, step 9.1]

11.1 The embedding matrix of step 10.1 is $K_1K_2$: summing over the intermediate pair $(a,b)$ gives $\sum_{a,b}\sigma_k(\alpha_a)\delta_{lb}\delta_{ai}\tau_b(\beta_j)=\sigma_k(\alpha_i)\tau_l(\beta_j)$. Here $(K_1)_{(k,l),(i,j)}=\sigma_k(\alpha_i)\delta_{lj}$ and $(K_2)_{(k,l),(i,j)}=\delta_{ki}\tau_l(\beta_j)$. By [F10], $\det(K_1K_2)=\det(K_1)\det(K_2)$. In the first-index-slow ordering of step 10.1, $K_2$ is block diagonal with $m$ blocks $N=(\tau_l(\beta_j))$, so $\det(K_2)=\det(N)^m$. For $K_1$, reorder both its rows and its columns by the same perfect shuffle from $(k,l)$ to $(l,k)$ and from $(i,j)$ to $(j,i)$. These are the same permutation of $mn$ positions, so their determinant signs multiply to $1$; in the resulting ordering $K_1$ is block diagonal with $n$ blocks $M=(\sigma_k(\alpha_i))$. Hence $\det(K_1)=\det(M)^n$, using [F10] and the Leibniz formula for block diagonal matrices. [F10, step 10.1]

12.1 By [F5], $d_{KL}=\det(\sigma_k(\alpha_i)\tau_l(\beta_j))^2=(\det M)^{2n}(\det N)^{2m}=d_K^{n}d_L^{m}=d_K^{[L:\mathbb Q]}d_L^{[K:\mathbb Q]}$, using $\det(M)^2=d_K$ and $\det(N)^2=d_L$ from [F5] and $m=[K:\mathbb Q]$, $n=[L:\mathbb Q]$. This completes the proof. [F5, step 11.1] ∎
