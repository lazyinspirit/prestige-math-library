## Example

Assume AC. Fix a finite horizon $H\ge1$ and a number $0<\varepsilon<1$. Consider one state $s$, actions $a,b$, rewards $0,1$, and a transition back to $s$ after either action; action $a$ gives reward $0$ and action $b$ gives reward $1$. Let $\gamma=1$, let the target policy choose $b$ with probability $1$, and let the behavior policy choose $b$ with probability $\varepsilon$ and $a$ with probability $1-\varepsilon$. Full trajectory coverage holds because the only target action has positive behavior probability. The target return is $H$. For one behavior trajectory, the ordinary trajectory importance-sampling score is $H\varepsilon^{-H}$ if all $H$ actions are $b$, an event of probability $\varepsilon^H$, and is $0$ otherwise. Thus the score has mean $H$, second moment $H^2\varepsilon^{-H}$, and variance $H^2(\varepsilon^{-H}-1)$, which grows exponentially in $H$ for fixed $\varepsilon$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** AC, the one-state finite MDP, horizon $H$, $\gamma=1$, target policy $b$, behavior probability $\varepsilon$ for $b$, and one trajectory sampled under behavior.

[F1] [[def-offline-reinforcement-learning-dataset]]: the return is the finite discounted reward sum and the logged trajectory law is generated by the behavior policy and the stated joint reward/next-state mass.

[F2] [[alg-trajectory-importance-sampling-ope]]: the ordinary score is $W_{0:H-1}G_H$, with $W_{0:H-1}$ the product of the $H$ action-propensity ratios.

## Verification

1.1 Under the target policy all $H$ actions are $b$, every reward is $1$, and $\gamma=1$, so $G_H=H$. Under behavior, the event that all $H$ actions are $b$ has probability $\varepsilon^H$; on that event every factor is $1/\varepsilon$, hence $W_{0:H-1}=\varepsilon^{-H}$. If any action is $a$, its target probability is zero, the product weight is zero, and the OIS score is zero. [given, F1, F2, algebra]

2.1 The score $X=W_{0:H-1}G_H$ therefore equals $H\varepsilon^{-H}$ with probability $\varepsilon^H$ and $0$ otherwise. Thus $E_\mu X=\varepsilon^H H\varepsilon^{-H}=H$ and $E_\mu X^2=\varepsilon^H H^2\varepsilon^{-2H}=H^2\varepsilon^{-H}$. Subtracting $(E_\mu X)^2=H^2$ gives $\operatorname{Var}_\mu(X)=H^2(\varepsilon^{-H}-1)$. Since $\varepsilon^{-H}$ is exponential in $H$, the variance grows exponentially although every finite-horizon score is finite. [given, F1, F2, step 1.1, algebra] ∎
