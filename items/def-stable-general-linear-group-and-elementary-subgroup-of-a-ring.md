## Definition

Throughout, $R$ is an associative unital ring, not assumed commutative
([[def-ring]]). For $n\ge0$ let $R^n$ be the set of column vectors
$v=(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ with $v_i\in R$, made into a **right $R$-module** by
entrywise addition and the right action
$$(v\cdot r)_i:=v_ir\qquad(r\in R)$$
([[def-left-and-right-modules]]); for $n=0$ this is the zero module, whose
unique element is the empty column. A map $f:R^n\to R^m$ is right $R$-linear
when $f(v+v')=f(v)+f(v')$ and $f(v\cdot r)=f(v)\cdot r$.

**Matrices.** Every right-linear $f:R^n\to R^m$ has a unique matrix
$A\in M_{m\times n}(R)$, written $A=(A_{ij})$, such that
$$(Av)_i=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}v_j ,$$
the sum being the finite sum in the additive group of $R$; uniqueness uses that
the standard basis vectors $e_1,\dots,e_n$ of $R^n$ generate it and that columns
of $A$ are the coordinate vectors of $f(e_j)$. If $g:R^m\to R^p$ has matrix
$B$, then $g\circ f:R^n\to R^p$ has matrix $BA$, with
$(BA)_{ik}=\sum_jB_{ij}A_{jk}$; this is the displayed order and it uses only
associativity and distributivity.

**General linear group.** Let $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ be the group of $n\times n$
matrices over $R$ possessing a two-sided inverse, with multiplication of
matrices as the group operation and $I_n$ as the identity; by the previous
paragraph $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ is exactly the group of right-linear automorphisms
of $R^n$. The **stabilization** $A\mapsto\operatorname{diag}(A,1)$ identifies
$\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ with a subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_{n+1}(R)$, and
$$\mathrm{GL}(R):=\bigcup_{n\ge0}\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$$
is the stable general linear group, in which every element is represented by
some $n\times n$ matrix. A matrix $A$ belongs to $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ when there
is a matrix $B$ satisfying *both* $AB=I_n$ and $BA=I_n$; either equation alone
need not imply the other over an arbitrary unital ring. No determinant,
commutativity, or rank function is used anywhere in this definition.

For a concrete one-sided inverse, take $R=\operatorname{End}_k(V)$ where $V$
has basis $e_0,e_1,\ldots$. Let $S(e_i)=e_{i+1}$ and let $L(e_0)=0$,
$L(e_{i+1})=e_i$. Then $LS=1_V$, while $SL(e_0)=0\ne e_0$, so the
$1\times1$ matrices $A=L$ and $B=S$ satisfy $AB=I_1$ but not $BA=I_1$.

**Elementary matrices.** For $n\ge1$, $1\le i,j\le n$ with $i\ne j$ and $r\in R$,
let $E_{ij}$ be the matrix with entry $1$ in position $(i,j)$ and $0$
elsewhere, and let
$$e_{ij}(r):=I_n+rE_{ij}$$
be the corresponding **elementary matrix**. It is invertible with two-sided
inverse $e_{ij}(-r)$, because $E_{ij}^2=0$ and hence
$e_{ij}(r)e_{ij}(-r)=I_n$. Let $\mathrm{E}_n(R)$ be the
subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ generated by all elementary matrices in
$\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ for $n\ge1$, and set $\mathrm{E}_0(R)=\{I_0\}$. Let
$$\mathrm{E}(R):=\bigcup_{n\ge0}\mathrm{E}_n(R)$$
be the **stable elementary subgroup**, the subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}(R)$
generated by the images of all elementary matrices. Elementary matrices are
stable in the same way: $e_{ij}(r)$ in $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ is the image of the
elementary matrix with the same indices in $\mathrm{GL}_{n+1}(R)$.
