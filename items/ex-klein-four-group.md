## Example

Let $X = \{1,2,3,4\}$, four pairwise distinct natural numbers, and work in
$\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ ([[def-symmetric-group]],
[[lem-symmetric-group-is-a-group]]). Put

$$a = (1\,2)(3\,4), \qquad b = (1\,3)(2\,4), \qquad c = (1\,4)(2\,3),$$

each being the composite of the two disjoint transpositions shown, and set
$V = \{\mathrm{id}, a, b, c\}$. Then:

1. $V$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ ([[def-subgroup]]) with four
   distinct elements, so $|V| = 4$ ([[def-order-in-a-group]]);
2. $V$ is abelian, with multiplication table generated by
   $a^{2} = b^{2} = c^{2} = \mathrm{id}$, $ab = ba = c$, $ac = ca = b$ and
   $bc = cb = a$;
3. every element of $V$ other than $\mathrm{id}$ has order $2$;
4. $V$ is **not** cyclic ([[def-generated-subgroup]]).

$V$ is called the **Klein four-group**.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** $X = \{1,2,3,4\}$ and the permutations $a, b, c$ of $X$ acting as follows: $a$ sends $1 \mapsto 2$, $2 \mapsto 1$, $3 \mapsto 4$, $4 \mapsto 3$; $b$ sends $1 \mapsto 3$, $3 \mapsto 1$, $2 \mapsto 4$, $4 \mapsto 2$; $c$ sends $1 \mapsto 4$, $4 \mapsto 1$, $2 \mapsto 3$, $3 \mapsto 2$ ([[def-symmetric-group]]).

[L1] $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$ are pairwise distinct natural numbers, since each is a member of every later one and no natural number is a member of itself ([[def-natural-numbers]], [[lem-nat-transitive-irreflexive]]).

[L2] $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ is a group under composition, with identity $\mathrm{id}$ ([[lem-symmetric-group-is-a-group]], [[def-group]]).

[L3] Two permutations agree exactly when they agree at every point of $X$ ([[def-injection-surjection-bijection]]).

[L4] One-step test: a nonempty subset $H$ of a group with $x y^{-1} \in H$ for all $x, y \in H$ is a subgroup ([[lem-subgroup-criterion]], [[def-subgroup]]).

[L5] Powers and order: $f^{0} = \mathrm{id}$, $f^{1} = f$, $f^{2} = f \circ f$, and $\operatorname{ord}(f)$ is the least $k \ge 1$ with $f^{k} = \mathrm{id}$ ([[def-group-power]], [[def-order-in-a-group]]).

[L6] If $\operatorname{ord}(g) = m$ is finite then $|\langle g \rangle| = m$ ([[lem-order-characterisation]]); $\langle g \rangle$ is the smallest subgroup containing $g$ ([[def-generated-subgroup]]); $|A| = m$ means $A \approx m$, and that natural is unique ([[def-countable]], [[def-equinumerous]], [[def-order-in-a-group]]).

## Verification

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 The four elements are pairwise distinct: at the point $1$ they take the values $\mathrm{id}(1) = 1$, $a(1) = 2$, $b(1) = 3$ and $c(1) = 4$, which are pairwise distinct. [L1, L3, given]

1.2 Each of $a$, $b$, $c$ is its own inverse: $a \circ a$ sends $1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 1$, $2 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 2$, $3 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 3$ and $4 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 4$, so $a \circ a = \mathrm{id}$; the same computation with the corresponding pairs gives $b \circ b = \mathrm{id}$ and $c \circ c = \mathrm{id}$. [L2, L3, given]

1.3 $a \circ b = c$: it sends $1 \mapsto b(1) = 3 \mapsto a(3) = 4$, $2 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 3$, $3 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 2$, $4 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 1$, and $c$ sends $1 \mapsto 4$, $2 \mapsto 3$, $3 \mapsto 2$, $4 \mapsto 1$. And $b \circ a = c$: it sends $1 \mapsto a(1) = 2 \mapsto b(2) = 4$, $2 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 3$, $3 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 2$, $4 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 1$. [L3, given]

1.4 $a \circ c = b$ and $c \circ a = b$: the first sends $1 \mapsto c(1) = 4 \mapsto a(4) = 3$, $2 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 4$, $3 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 1$, $4 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 2$; the second sends $1 \mapsto a(1) = 2 \mapsto c(2) = 3$, $2 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 4$, $3 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 1$, $4 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 2$; and $b$ sends $1 \mapsto 3$, $2 \mapsto 4$, $3 \mapsto 1$, $4 \mapsto 2$. [L3, given]

1.5 $b \circ c = a$ and $c \circ b = a$: the first sends $1 \mapsto c(1) = 4 \mapsto b(4) = 2$, $2 \mapsto 3 \mapsto 1$, $3 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 4$, $4 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 3$; the second sends $1 \mapsto b(1) = 3 \mapsto c(3) = 2$, $2 \mapsto 4 \mapsto 1$, $3 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 4$, $4 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 3$; and $a$ sends $1 \mapsto 2$, $2 \mapsto 1$, $3 \mapsto 4$, $4 \mapsto 3$. [L3, given]

2.1 $V$ is nonempty and closed under $x \mapsto x^{-1}$: $\mathrm{id}^{-1} = \mathrm{id}$ and each of $a, b, c$ is its own inverse by step 1.2. [step 1.2, L2]

2.2 $V$ is closed under composition: composing $\mathrm{id}$ with anything returns that element, each of $a, b, c$ composed with itself gives $\mathrm{id}$ by step 1.2, and the six mixed products are computed in steps 1.3, 1.4 and 1.5, each landing in $V$. [step 1.2, step 1.3, step 1.4, step 1.5, L2]

2.3 $V$ is abelian: the products computed in steps 1.3, 1.4 and 1.5 agree in either order, $\mathrm{id}$ commutes with everything, and each element commutes with itself. With step 1.2 this is the table of claim 2. [step 1.2, step 1.3, step 1.4, step 1.5, L2]

2.4 Each of $a, b, c$ has order $2$: it is not $\mathrm{id}$ by step 1.1, so $\operatorname{ord} \ne 1$, and its square is $\mathrm{id}$ by step 1.2; hence $2$ is the least $k \ge 1$ with the $k$-th power equal to $\mathrm{id}$. This is claim 3, $\operatorname{ord}(\mathrm{id}) = 1$ being immediate. [step 1.1, step 1.2, L5]

3.1 Hence for $x, y \in V$ one has $y^{-1} \in V$ and $x y^{-1} \in V$, so $V$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ by the one-step test. [step 2.1, step 2.2, L4]

4.1 $V$ has exactly four elements: the map $4 \to V$ sending $0, 1, 2, 3$ to $\mathrm{id}, a, b, c$ is a bijection by step 1.1, so $V \approx 4$ and $|V| = 4$. This with step 3.1 is claim 1. [step 1.1, step 3.1, L6]

5.1 $V$ is not cyclic: if $V = \langle g \rangle$ for some $g \in V$, then $g$ has finite order and $|\langle g \rangle| = \operatorname{ord}(g)$, so $\operatorname{ord}(g) = |V| = 4$; but every element of $V$ has order $1$ or $2$ by step 2.4, and $4 \ne 1$ and $4 \ne 2$. This is claim 4. [step 4.1, step 2.4, L6] ∎

## Remarks

- **An abelian group need not be cyclic.** $V$ is the smallest witness of that, and it separates the two conditions that [[lem-cyclic-subgroup-is-the-set-of-powers]] relates in one direction only: cyclic implies abelian, never the converse.

- **The four-group is realised here inside a symmetric group rather than as a product.** The external direct product of two groups is introduced on a later page, so $V$ is exhibited as a set of four explicit permutations and checked by hand; nothing above uses any construction the library has not built.

- Every non-identity element having order $2$ is exactly what rules out a generator: a cyclic group of order $4$ must contain an element of order $4$ ([[lem-order-characterisation]]), and $(\mathbb{Z}/4, +)$ is such a group ([[ex-integers-mod-n-additive-group]]). So there are at least two groups of order $4$ that are not the same group. Classifying the groups of a given order needs machinery from a later page and is not attempted here.
