## Definition

Let $A=\mathbb Z[v^{\pm1}]$ and $\Lambda=A[z]=\mathbb Z[v^{\pm1},z]$
([[def-the-laurent-polynomial-ring]],
[[def-polynomial-ring-over-a-commutative-ring]]), and put
$z_-:=v^{-1}(z+1-v)\in\Lambda$. Let $R$ be the commutative ring presented by
$$\boxed{\,R:=\Lambda\bigl[z^{-1},u^{\pm1},s\bigr]\big/\bigl(s^2-v,\ vzu^2-(z+1-v)\bigr)\,,}$$
i.e. the quotient of the localisation of $\Lambda[s,u]$ at the multiplicative
subset generated by $z$ and $u$ by the two displayed relations
([[def-multiplicative-subset-and-localisation]], [[def-quotient-ring]],
[[thm-universal-property-of-localisation]]). Write again $v,z,s,u$ for the
images in $R$.

**Elementary properties.** $R$ is a commutative ring with unit; $v$, $z$ and
$u$ are units by construction, and $s\cdot(sv^{-1})=s^2v^{-1}=1$, so $s$ is a
unit too, with $s^{-1}=sv^{-1}$. The relations read $s^2=v$ and
$u^2=z_-/z$, equivalently $vzu^2=z+1-v$. The elements
$$l:=us,\qquad m:=s-s^{-1}=s^{-1}(v-1)$$
belong to $R$, and $l$ is a unit. No invertibility of $m$ is imposed. The identity
$$l^{-1}-l=m\,(uz)^{-1}$$
holds in $R$.

**Universal property.** Let $T$ be a commutative ring with unit. Giving a unital
ring homomorphism $R\to T$ is exactly the same as giving units
$v_0,z_0,u_0,s_0\in T^\times$ satisfying
$$s_0^2=v_0,\qquad v_0z_0u_0^2=z_0+1-v_0 ;$$
the homomorphism then sends $v\mapsto v_0$, $z\mapsto z_0$, $u\mapsto u_0$,
$s\mapsto s_0$. The existence direction is the universal property of polynomial
rings, localisations and quotients; uniqueness holds because these images determine the images of their inverses, and these generators together with the prescribed inverses generate $R$.

**Caveats.** $R$ is introduced only to hold the normalisation of
[[def-homflypt-polynomial-from-the-hecke-markov-trace]]; no claim is made that
$R$ is a domain or a UFD, and the two square roots $s$ of $v$ and $u$ of
$z_-/z$ are formal. The elements $v,z$ are inverted because $v$ is a unit of
$\Lambda$ and the trace identity $\alpha=(uz)^{-1}$ inverts $z$; $u$ is inverted
because the exponent $e(\beta)$ of a braid may be negative and because
$\alpha=(uz)^{-1}$ inverts $u$. Inverting $u$ is part of the definition: the
relation $u^2=z_-/z$ alone does not force $u$ to be a unit.
The element $m=s-s^{-1}$ need not be a unit: the universal specialization
$v=z=u=s=1$ sends it to $0\in\mathbb Z$. Division by $m$ is therefore only
licensed after passing to the localization $R[m^{-1}]$; the quotient ring $R/(m)$ is defined without inverting $m$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The rings $A=\mathbb Z[v^{\pm1}]$, $\Lambda=A[z]$, the elements $z_-=v^{-1}(z+1-v)$, and the multiplicative subsets generated by $z$ and $u$. No choice principle is used.

[F1] $\Lambda$ is commutative with unit and $v$ is a unit with powers $v^\pm$; $z$ is a nonzero non-unit and $\Lambda$ is a domain ([[def-the-laurent-polynomial-ring]], [[lem-units-and-powers-of-the-laurent-polynomial-ring]], [[def-polynomial-ring-over-a-commutative-ring]]).

[F2] Localisation inverts a multiplicative subset and has the universal property: a unital ring homomorphism from the localisation is exactly a unital ring homomorphism from the original ring sending the subset to units ([[def-multiplicative-subset-and-localisation]], [[thm-universal-property-of-localisation]], [[thm-universal-property-of-a-polynomial-ring]]).

[F3] A quotient ring is the universal ring receiving the original ring with the prescribed elements killed ([[def-quotient-ring]], [[thm-quotient-ring-universal-property]]); a unital ring homomorphism is a map preserving addition, multiplication and $1$ ([[def-ring-homomorphism]]).

## Proof

1.1 **Well-formedness.** The localisation $\Lambda[z^{-1},u^{\pm1},s]$ of the polynomial ring $\Lambda[s,u]$ at the multiplicative subset generated by $z$ and $u$ is a commutative ring with unit by [F2], and by [F1] the images $v,z,u$ are units (for $v$ because $v$ is already a unit of $\Lambda$). Forming the quotient by the ideal generated by the two relations gives a commutative ring $R$ with unit by [F3], and the displayed relations hold in it by construction. Since $s^2=v$ and $v$ is a unit, $s$ is a unit with $s^{-1}=sv^{-1}$, so $s^{-1}$ exists and $m=s-s^{-1}=s^{-1}(s^2-1)=s^{-1}(v-1)$ as displayed. [F1, F2, F3, algebra]

1.2 **The identity.** Using $s^2=v$ and $vzu^2=z+1-v$, so that $u^2v=(z+1-v)z^{-1}$ and $1-u^2v=(v-1)z^{-1}$, one computes $l^{-1}-l=(us)^{-1}-us=(1-u^2s^2)(us)^{-1}=(1-u^2v)(us)^{-1}=(v-1)z^{-1}u^{-1}s^{-1}=s^{-1}(v-1)(uz)^{-1}=m(uz)^{-1}$, which is the displayed identity. [algebra]

2.1 **Universal property.** By [F2] a unital homomorphism from $\Lambda[z^{-1},u^{\pm1},s]$ to $T$ is exactly a unital homomorphism $\Lambda[s,u]\to T$ sending $z$ and $u$ to units, i.e. a choice of images $v_0$ (a unit, since $v$ is a unit of $\Lambda$ and homomorphisms send units to units), $z_0,u_0\in T^\times$ and $s_0\in T$; by [F3] it factors through the quotient $R$ exactly when the two relations hold at the images, i.e. $s_0^2=v_0$ and $v_0z_0u_0^2=z_0+1-v_0$; uniqueness holds because the images of $v,z,u,s$ determine those of the inverted elements, and these elements and inverses generate $R$. [F2, F3, step 1.1] ∎
