## Statement

Assume countable choice. For every integer $r\geq0$, let $R_r=\mathbb Z[x_1^{\pm1},\ldots,x_r^{\pm1}]$, identified with the integral group ring of $\mathbb Z^r$. Then $K_0(R_r)=\mathbb Z[R_r]$ and $K_1(R_r)\cong C_2\oplus\mathbb Z^r$, with generators represented by the units $-1,x_1,\ldots,x_r$. Consequently the reduced projective class group $\widetilde K_0(\mathbb Z[\mathbb Z^r])$ and the Whitehead group $\mathrm{Wh}(\mathbb Z^r)$ vanish.

Here $K_0(A)$ is the Grothendieck group of finitely generated projective $A$-modules, $K_1(A)=\mathrm{GL}(A)/E(A)$ uses stabilization by identity matrices, $\widetilde K_0(A)=K_0(A)/\mathbb Z[A]$, and $\mathrm{Wh}(G)$ is the quotient of $K_1(\mathbb Z[G])$ by the classes of units $\pm g$ for $g\in G$.

## Facts & Assumptions

[A1] Countable choice is assumed in the sense of [[def-countable-choice]]. It is used to lift the images of a countable free basis through a surjection. All rings and modules in the resolution arguments below are countable; no arbitrary-module global-dimension assertion or dependent choice is used.

## Proof

**Given:** A nonnegative integer $r$ and assumption [A1]. For a noetherian ring $A$, write $G_0(A)$ for the Grothendieck group of finitely generated $A$-modules. For an exact module category $\mathcal C$, its Grothendieck group has generators $[M]$ and relations $[M]=[M']+[M'']$ for its short exact sequences. The proof develops the needed polynomial, nilpotent and localization arguments locally.

1.1 Every ring obtained from $\mathbb Z$ by finitely many polynomial and Laurent extensions has a fixed enumeration by finite coefficient and exponent codes. A module with a specified finite or countable generating list over such a ring has an enumeration, allowing repetitions and zero. A submodule inherits an enumeration by listing the enumerated elements that belong to it and putting zero at other indices; quotients inherit the images of the list. These constructions use classical excluded middle and no choice. A countably generated free module is projective under [A1]: through a given surjection choose a preimage of each of its countably many basis images. These fibers may be arbitrary sets, and countable choice suffices. A summand is projective; a countably generated projective module is a summand of a countably generated free module by splitting its countable free surjection. [given, A1, construct]

1.2 A subgroup $H$ of the countably free abelian group $F=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb N}\mathbb Ze_n$ is countably free without arbitrary well-ordering. Set $H_n=H\cap\bigoplus_{i<n}\mathbb Ze_i$. Projection onto coordinate $n$ identifies $H_{n+1}/H_n$ with an ideal $d_n\mathbb Z$. If $d_n>0$, take the vector $v_n$ of least natural-number code in $H_{n+1}$ having coordinate $d_n$; then $H_{n+1}=H_n\oplus\mathbb Zv_n$. If $d_n=0$, there is no added vector. Since every element has finite support, the vectors added form a basis of $H$. A countable free surjection onto a countably generated abelian group therefore has free kernel and gives a projective resolution of length at most one. [step 1.1, construct, algebra]

1.3 Inductively, suppose every countably generated module over a particular enumerated ring $A$ has a resolution of length at most $d$ by countably generated projectives. If $M$ is countably generated over $A[t]$, then the elements $t^jm_i$ from its specified generators give a countable generating list over $A$. There is an exact sequence $0\to A[t]\otimes_A M\xrightarrow{\delta}A[t]\otimes_A M\xrightarrow{\epsilon}M\to0$, where $\delta(t^j\otimes m)=t^{j+1}\otimes m-t^j\otimes tm$ and $\epsilon(t^j\otimes m)=t^jm$. The greatest nonzero coefficient proves injectivity of $\delta$; reduction of polynomial tensors to constants proves exactness in the middle. Induce an $A$-projective resolution of $M$ to the free $A$-module $A[t]$, obtaining length-$d$ projective resolutions of both induced terms. Lift $\delta$ to a chain map and take its cone. This is a length-at-most-$d+1$ resolution of $M$. Each lift is justified by step 1.1, and only finitely many lifts and resolutions are chosen for each fixed finite number of variables. [step 1.1, step 1.2, construct, algebra]

1.4 For a specified countable multiplicative set $S\subset A$, a module generated over $S^{-1}A$ by $m_i$ is generated over $A$ by $m_i/s$. Localize its bounded countable $A$-projective resolution. Localization is exact and preserves the summand decompositions witnessing projectivity, so the same length bound holds over $S^{-1}A$. Steps 1.2–1.3 consequently give the requisite finite bound for countably generated modules over every mixed polynomial and Laurent ring occurring below. These rings are noetherian: in the polynomial step the ideals of leading coefficients stabilize, finite polynomials realizing their generators reduce every higher degree, and the remaining lower-degree coefficient submodule is finitely generated; in the localization step clear denominators in generators of the contracted ideal. Thus finite modules have finite resolutions by finitely generated projectives. Explicitly, take finite free surjections and their finite kernels up to the countable resolution bound. Compare this partial free resolution with a bounded countable projective resolution: the pullback of two projective epimorphisms onto a module splits over both projectives and gives Schanuel's isomorphism $H\oplus P'\cong H'\oplus P$. Iterating it shows the last finite kernel is a summand of a projective module and hence projective. All comparisons are finite and use step 1.1. [step 1.1, step 1.3, construct, algebra]

1.5 We will also use the resulting kernel bound. If $P\twoheadrightarrow M$ is a projective epimorphism of countable modules and $M$ has a projective resolution of length $e>0$, its kernel $H$ has one of length at most $e-1$. Indeed compare $P\twoheadrightarrow M$ with the first map $Q_0\twoheadrightarrow M$ of such a resolution, whose kernel $L$ has length at most $e-1$. The pullback gives $H\oplus Q_0\cong L\oplus P$. Resolve the right side, lift the inclusion of the projective summand $Q_0$ to degree zero, and use the augmentation projection to split off $Q_0$ as a degree-zero projective subcomplex. The remaining complex resolves $H$ with the same bound. If $e=0$, split $P\twoheadrightarrow M$ directly. This proves the kernel bounds used below without invoking unrestricted Ext or arbitrary-module dimension theorems. [step 1.1, step 1.4, construct, algebra]

1.6 We need an Euler-resolution fact for full additive module subcategories $\mathcal P\subset\mathcal C$. Suppose both are closed under kernels of epimorphisms between their objects and every object of $\mathcal C$ has a finite $\mathcal P$-resolution. Then $K_0(\mathcal P)\to K_0(\mathcal C)$ is an isomorphism. Here is the comparison proof. Given $f:C'\to C$ and a finite resolution $P_*\to C$, construct a finite resolution of $C'$ mapping to it by induction on the length of $P_*$. At length zero any resolution of $C'$ maps to $P_0=C$. Otherwise let $Z=\ker(P_0\to C)$, take the pullback $B=P_0\times_C C'$, choose a $\mathcal P$-epimorphism $P'_0\twoheadrightarrow B$, and set $Y=\ker(P'_0\to C')$. Apply the induction to $Y\to Z$ and the truncated resolution, then splice. All kernels remain in $\mathcal C$. A finite acyclic $\mathcal P$-complex has zero Euler sum because its cycles lie in $\mathcal P$, inducting from its degree-zero term, and its cycle short exact sequences telescope. Comparing two resolutions by the construction applied to $C\to C\oplus C$ shows their Euler sums agree, since the projection maps are quasi-isomorphisms with acyclic cones. The cone of a lifted $C'\to C$ in a short exact sequence resolves $C''$, so the Euler sums are additive. Thus $[C]\mapsto\sum_i(-1)^i[P_i]$ is the inverse homomorphism. Applying this fact to projectives and finite modules over the rings in step 1.4 gives $K_0(A)\cong G_0(A)$. [step 1.4, step 1.5, construct, algebra]

2.1 For a commutative countable noetherian ring $A$ and a multiplicative set $S$, let $\mathcal T_S$ be the finite $S$-torsion modules. The sequence $K_0(\mathcal T_S)\to G_0(A)\to G_0(S^{-1}A)\to0$ is exact. Finite localized presentations descend by clearing finitely many denominators, proving surjectivity. An isomorphism of descended modules lifts to a map after multiplication by a denominator; its kernel and cokernel are torsion, so it identifies the same classes modulo torsion. A short exact sequence over the localization similarly descends to a finite complex with torsion homology; its kernel and image sequences establish additivity modulo torsion. These observations construct the inverse from $G_0(S^{-1}A)$ to the displayed cokernel. Every finite torsion module is killed by a single element of $S$. For $A[t]$ and powers of $t$, filter such a module by $t^iM$. Its successive factors are finite $A$-modules $N$, whose classes vanish in $G_0(A[t])$ by $0\to N[t]\xrightarrow{t}N[t]\to N\to0$. Hence $G_0(A[t])\cong G_0(A[t,t^{-1}])$. [step 1.1, construct, algebra]

2.2 The polynomial extension $i:G_0(A)\to G_0(A[t])$ is injective. A left inverse is $[M]\mapsto[M/tM]-[\ker(t:M\to M)]$. Both modules on the right are finite over $A$; the kernel-cokernel sequence for multiplication by $t$ proves this expression is additive. On $N[t]$ it gives $[N]$. To prove surjectivity, take an inclusion-maximal ideal $J$ among the quotients that fail surjectivity, if any, and replace $A$ by $A/J$. This maximality needs no dependent choice here. Each ideal has a least finite generating tuple in the enumeration of finite ring lists; thus ideals have countable codes. If a counterexample set had no maximal element, repeatedly selecting its least-coded strict enlargement would define by ordinary recursion an increasing chain. Its union ideal is finitely generated and hence contained in one member, contradicting strict increase. Every nonzero quotient of the replaced ring therefore satisfies surjectivity. [step 1.1, step 1.4, construct, algebra]

2.3 The replaced ring in step 2.2 is reduced. Otherwise its nonzero nilradical $I$ is nilpotent: it is finitely generated by nilpotent elements, and a sufficiently long product must repeat a generator too often. Filtering finite modules over $A$ and $A[t]$ by powers of $I$ shows their classes are sums of classes from $A/I$ and $(A/I)[t]$. Surjectivity for this proper quotient would imply it for $A$, a contradiction. For reduced noetherian $A$ let $\mathfrak p_1,\ldots,\mathfrak p_s$ be its finitely many minimal primes. Its total quotient ring is $Q=\prod_i\operatorname{Frac}(A/\mathfrak p_i)$. To see the decomposition explicitly, choose $a_i\in\bigcap_{j\ne i}\mathfrak p_j\setminus\mathfrak p_i$ by finite prime avoidance. The sum $a=\sum_i a_i$ avoids all minimal primes and is a nonzerodivisor, since $A$ embeds in the product of the domains $A/\mathfrak p_i$. The fractions $a_i/a$ select its factors. A denominator nonzero in factor $i$ is made invertible there by replacing its representative $c$ with $a_ic+\sum_{j\ne i}a_j$, which avoids every minimal prime. This produces every field fraction in that factor and proves the assertion about $Q$. These are finite choices. [step 2.2, construct, algebra]

2.4 Localize $A$ and $A[t]$ at the nonzerodivisors of $A$. For a field $F$, the Euclidean ring $F[t]$ has $G_0(F[t])=\mathbb Z$: finite modules split into a free part and cyclic torsion parts, and each $F[t]/(f)$ has zero class by its two-term free resolution. Hence $G_0(Q)\to G_0(Q[t])$ is an isomorphism. Given a class in $G_0(A[t])$, its localized class therefore lifts from $G_0(A)$ by step 2.1. Subtract that lift. The remainder comes from a torsion module killed by some nonzerodivisor $s$. If $s$ is a unit there is no remainder; otherwise $(s)\ne0$, and surjectivity for $A/sA$ from maximality in step 2.2 expresses this torsion contribution as one coming from $G_0(A)$. This contradicts failure of surjectivity. Together with step 2.2 and Laurent localization in step 2.1, this proves polynomial and Laurent invariance of $G_0$ for these countable commutative noetherian rings. Finite integer modules have zero torsion classes and free rank detects their remaining class, so $G_0(\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z$. Thus step 1.6 yields $K_0(R_r)=\mathbb Z[R_r]$, and the same conclusion holds after adjoining polynomial variables. [step 1.6, step 2.1, step 2.2, step 2.3, algebra]

3.1 For a ring $A$ of step 1.4, let $\operatorname{Nil}_{\mathcal P}(A)$ consist of finite projective modules with a nilpotent endomorphism, and let $\operatorname{Nil}_{\mathcal M}(A)$ allow all finite modules. Exact sequences respect the endomorphism and are exact on underlying modules. Every $(M,\nu)$ in the second category has a finite resolution by the first. If $\nu^N=0$ and $P\twoheadrightarrow M$ is finite free, put the forward shift $\sigma$ on $P^N$ and send summand $i$ to $\nu^i$ times that surjection, for $0\leq i<N$. This is an equivariant surjection. Its kernel is finite by noetherianity, remains nilpotent, and its underlying projective dimension drops by step 1.5 until projective. Both categories have the kernel closure required in step 1.6. Thus their $K_0$ groups agree. In the module category the filtration by $\nu^iM$ gives $[(M,\nu)]=\sum_i[(\nu^iM/\nu^{i+1}M,0)]=[(M,0)]$. Forgetting the endomorphism is consequently an isomorphism to $G_0(A)$, with inverse the zero endomorphism. It follows that $K_0(\operatorname{Nil}_{\mathcal P}(A))\to K_0(A)$ is an isomorphism as well. [step 1.5, step 1.6, construct, algebra]

3.2 Stable elementary block operations give the usual abelianization description $K_1(A)=\mathrm{GL}(A)_{\mathrm{ab}}$. For completeness, elementary matrices are commutators after adjoining a third index, since $e_{ij}(c)=[e_{ik}(c),e_{kj}(1)]$. The elementary block factorization of $\operatorname{diag}(U,U^{-1})$ is obtained by multiplying $E_{12}(U)E_{21}(-U^{-1})E_{12}(U)$ by $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-I\\I&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$; the final matrix is $E_{12}(-I)E_{21}(I)E_{12}(-I)$. It follows that the stable elementary subgroup is normal and that stabilized blocks multiply and commute in the quotient; conversely the commutators vanish there. In particular a block triangular invertible matrix has the sum of its diagonal classes. For a finite projective $P$ and nilpotent $\nu$, extend $1-s\nu$ on $P[s]$ by the identity on a complement making $P$ free. The $K_1(A[s])$ class is independent of complement by adding both complements and conjugating. A short exact sequence of nilpotent projectives splits on underlying modules, so its automorphism is block triangular. This gives an additive homomorphism $\tau:K_0(\operatorname{Nil}_{\mathcal P}(A))\to K_1(A[s])$. It is zero on every $(P,0)$ and therefore zero by step 3.1. [step 3.1, construct, algebra]

3.3 If $g(s)\in\mathrm{GL}_m(A[s])$ and $g(0)=I$, linearize it by degree reduction. For degree $n\geq2$, write $g=I-sh(s)+Cs^n$, with $\deg h\leq n-2$. Starting from $\operatorname{diag}(g,I)$, add $Cs^{n-1}$ to the upper-right block and subtract $s$ times the second block column from the first. The result is $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}I-sh&Cs^{n-1}\\-sI&I\end{smallmatrix}\right]=I-s\left[\begin{smallmatrix}h&-Cs^{n-2}\\I&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, of degree at most $n-1$. Iterate to a representative $I-s\nu$ with constant $\nu$. Its polynomial inverse $\sum_{i=0}^q B_is^i$ satisfies $B_i=\nu^i$ and $\nu^{q+1}=0$ by coefficient comparison. Thus its class is zero by step 3.2. For general $g$ apply this to $g(0)^{-1}g(s)$. Evaluation and extension therefore give $K_1(A[s])\cong K_1(A)$. Only a polynomial inverse was used, so no Laurent linearization assumption is hidden here. [step 3.2, construct, algebra]

4.1 Put $A=R_r$, $B=A[t]$, $C=A[t,t^{-1}]$. Let $\mathcal H$ be the finite $B$-modules killed by a power of $t$ with $B$-projective dimension at most one. It is equivalent to $\operatorname{Nil}_{\mathcal P}(A)$. In one direction the characteristic sequence $0\to P[t]\xrightarrow{t-\nu}P[t]\to(P,\nu)\to0$ is exact: compare the leading coefficient for injectivity and reduce successive powers of $t$ to $\nu$ for its cokernel. Conversely take $0\to P\to Q\to M\to0$ with finite $B$-projectives and $t^NM=0$. Reduction modulo $t^N$ gives $0\to M\to P/t^NP\to Q/t^NQ\to M\to0$; the first term is $\operatorname{Tor}_1^B(M,B/(t^N))=M$ by the two-term resolution of $B/(t^N)$. The middle terms are $A$-projective, being summands of finite free $A$-modules. The module $M$ is finite over $A$ and has a finite $A$-projective resolution by step 1.4. Repeat the displayed two-term period to an even length at least that resolution length, and pad the finite resolution with zero terms to the same length. Iterated Schanuel comparison identifies its periodic syzygy $M$, plus projective summands, with a projective module. Thus $M$ is $A$-projective. The endomorphism $t$ is nilpotent, proving the equivalence. By steps 3.1 and 2.4, $K_0(\mathcal H)\cong K_0(A)=\mathbb Z$, detected by underlying $A$-rank. [step 1.4, step 2.4, step 3.1, construct, algebra]

4.2 If $\alpha$ is a square $B$-matrix invertible over $C$, it is injective and its cokernel belongs to $\mathcal H$. Indeed a sufficiently large power of $t$ clears its inverse and kills the cokernel, while the matrix itself is a length-one free resolution. For $g=\alpha/t^q\in\mathrm{GL}_m(C)$ with $q\geq0$ clearing denominators define $\partial[g]=[\operatorname{coker}\alpha]-m[B/t^qB]$ in $K_0(\mathcal H)$. For injective square matrices the cokernel sequence of their product gives $[\operatorname{coker}(\alpha\beta)]=[\operatorname{coker}\alpha]+[\operatorname{coker}\beta]$. This proves independence when multiplying numerator and denominator by $t^k$ and hence for any two presentations of $g$; it also proves multiplicativity of the boundary. Upon stabilizing $g$ use numerator $\operatorname{diag}(\alpha,t^q)$, so the added cokernel cancels the added denominator term. The resulting stable homomorphism factors through $K_1(C)$ by step 3.2 and vanishes on $K_1(B)$. It sends $[t]$ to $[A]$, and scalar powers of $t$ split it. [step 3.2, step 4.1, construct, algebra]

4.3 Every $M\in\mathcal H$ has a square free $B$-presentation. Choose $0\to P\to Q\to M\to0$ with finite projectives. Their ranks agree over the fraction field because $M$ is torsion. Since $K_0(B)=\mathbb Z$ by step 2.4, $[P]=[Q]$, so the group completion of the direct-sum monoid of projectives gives $P\oplus T\cong Q\oplus T$ for some projective $T$. Add its identity presentation and then a common complement to make both sides free. This gives an injective square matrix $\alpha$ invertible over $C$. We will show that $a(M)=[\alpha]$ is a well-defined additive class in $D=K_1(C)/\operatorname{im}K_1(B)$. [step 2.4, step 4.1, construct, algebra]

4.4 Suppose $\alpha,\beta$ give isomorphic cokernels, with isomorphism $\gamma:M_\alpha\to M_\beta$. Pad by identity matrices so they act on the same $F=B^m$. Let $Q=F\oplus F$, $L=M_\alpha\oplus M_\beta$, and $\pi:Q\twoheadrightarrow L$ be the sum quotient map. The automorphism $\theta(u,v)=(\gamma^{-1}v,\gamma u)$ of $L$ has lifts $f,g$ to endomorphisms of $Q$ of $\theta,\theta^{-1}$, since $Q$ is free. The automorphism $\Gamma=E_{12}(f)E_{21}(-g)E_{12}(f)\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-I\\I&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ of $Q\oplus Q$ induces $\operatorname{diag}(\theta,\theta^{-1})$ on $L\oplus L$ by the multiplication in step 3.2. Consequently for $e(q,q')=\pi(q)$ we have $e\Gamma=\theta e$. On $F^4$, let $\pi_1$ be the quotient onto $M_\alpha$ from the first component and $\pi_2$ the quotient onto $M_\beta$ from the second component. Then $\pi_2\Gamma=\gamma\pi_1$. Their kernels have free identifications $D_\alpha=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha,I,I,I)$ and $D_\beta=\operatorname{diag}(I,\beta,I,I)$. The induced kernel isomorphism is a $B$-automorphism $\Gamma_1$ of $F^4$ satisfying $\Gamma D_\alpha=D_\beta\Gamma_1$. Thus $[\alpha]-[\beta]=[\Gamma_1]-[\Gamma]$ lies in the image of $K_1(B)$. This proves that $a(M)$ is independent of presentation and cokernel identification. [step 3.2, step 4.3, construct, algebra]

4.5 For $0\to M'\to M\to M''\to0$ in $\mathcal H$, choose square free presentations of its endpoints. Lift the free surjection onto $M''$ through $M$ and combine it with the free surjection onto $M'$. The resulting surjection onto $M$ has kernel an extension of the endpoint free kernels, which splits because the quotient kernel is free. In these identifications its presentation is block triangular with the two endpoint presentation matrices as diagonal blocks. After localization remove the off-diagonal block by an elementary multiplication. Thus $a(M)=a(M')+a(M'')$, and $a$ induces a homomorphism $K_0(\mathcal H)\to D$. The boundary satisfies $\partial a[M]=[M]$. Conversely for $g=\alpha/t^q$, its class in $D$ is $a[\operatorname{coker}\alpha]-qm[t]$. The presentation $t:B\to B$ gives $a[A]=[t]$, and $B/t^qB$ has $q$ filtration factors $A$. Therefore $a\partial[g]=[g]$ as well. These maps are inverse, proving $\ker\partial=\operatorname{im}K_1(B)$. The explicit splitting in step 4.2 gives $K_1(C)=\operatorname{im}K_1(B)\oplus\mathbb Z[t]$. By step 3.3 the first group is the image of $K_1(A)$; it injects, since evaluation $t=1$ is its retraction. We have proved $K_1(R_r[t,t^{-1}])\cong K_1(R_r)\oplus K_0(R_r)$, with the new generator represented by $t$. [step 3.3, step 4.1, step 4.2, step 4.3, step 4.4, construct, algebra]

5.1 Euclidean integer row operations reduce the first column of a unimodular matrix to a primitive coordinate vector; column additions clear its first row, and induction reduces to diagonal entries $\pm1$. A pair of negative signs is elementary by the factorization of $\operatorname{diag}(u,u^{-1})$ in step 3.2 with $u=-1$. The determinant detects the remaining sign. Hence $K_1(\mathbb Z)=C_2$. Inducting in step 4.5 yields $K_1(R_r)=C_2\oplus\mathbb Z^r$ generated by $-1,x_1,\ldots,x_r$; the boundary detects each new exponent and evaluation at the new variable equal to one detects the preceding summand. Step 2.4 already gives $K_0(R_r)=\mathbb Z[R_r]$. [step 2.4, step 3.2, step 4.5, algebra]

5.2 Under $\mathbb Z[\mathbb Z^r]\cong R_r$, quotienting $K_0$ by the free rank-one class therefore gives zero. Quotienting $K_1$ by the classes of $\pm g$ also gives zero, because these units include all the generators in step 5.1. These are exactly $\widetilde K_0(\mathbb Z[\mathbb Z^r])$ and $\mathrm{Wh}(\mathbb Z^r)$ as defined in the statement. Every countable-basis lift used above is justified by [A1], and all other choices were finite or least-coded; hence the conclusion holds under countable choice. [A1, step 5.1, algebra] ∎
