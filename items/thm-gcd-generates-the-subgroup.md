## Statement

Let $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$, and put

$$g\mathbb{Z} := \{\, gk : k \in \mathbb{Z} \,\}, \qquad a\mathbb{Z} + b\mathbb{Z} := \{\, ax + by \;:\; x, y \in \mathbb{Z} \,\} .$$

Then $a\mathbb{Z} + b\mathbb{Z}$ and $a\mathbb{Z} \cap b\mathbb{Z}$ are subgroups
of $(\mathbb{Z},+)$ ([[def-subgroup]]), and

$$a\mathbb{Z} + b\mathbb{Z} \;=\; \gcd(a,b)\,\mathbb{Z}, \qquad a\mathbb{Z} \cap b\mathbb{Z} \;=\; \operatorname{lcm}(a,b)\,\mathbb{Z}$$

([[def-common-divisor-and-gcd]], [[def-lcm]]). Equivalently, in the group
$(\mathbb{Z},+)$,

$$\langle \{a,b\} \rangle \;=\; \langle \gcd(a,b) \rangle, \qquad \langle a \rangle \cap \langle b \rangle \;=\; \langle \operatorname{lcm}(a,b) \rangle$$

([[def-generated-subgroup]]). Since $\gcd(a,b) \ge 0$ and
$\operatorname{lcm}(a,b) \ge 0$, and every subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z},+)$ has
exactly one nonnegative generator ([[lem-subgroups-of-z-are-cyclic]]), these are
identifications of subgroups by their canonical generator, not merely equalities
of two sets that happen to coincide.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Integers $a$ and $b$; $d := \gcd(a,b)$ and $\ell := \operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$.

[L1] $\mathbb{Z}$ is a commutative ring: addition and multiplication are associative and commutative, $x + 0 = x$, $x \cdot 1 = x$, $x \cdot 0 = 0$, multiplication distributes over addition, and every $x$ has an additive inverse; we write $u - v$ for $u + (-v)$ ([[thm-int-comm-ring]], [[def-int-operations]], [[def-integers]]).

[L2] $(\mathbb{Z},+,0)$ is an abelian group; $g\mathbb{Z} = \langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup for every $g$; and every subgroup equals $\iota(n)\mathbb{Z}$ for exactly one $n \in \mathbb{N}$ ([[lem-subgroups-of-z-are-cyclic]], [[def-group]]).

[L3] A subgroup contains the identity and is closed under the operation and under inverses; a nonempty $S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ with $x - y \in S$ for all $x, y \in S$ is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z},+)$ ([[def-subgroup]], [[lem-subgroup-criterion]]).

[L4] $\langle S \rangle$ is the smallest subgroup containing $S$: it contains $S$ and is contained in every subgroup containing $S$ ([[def-generated-subgroup]]).

[L5] An intersection of subgroups is a subgroup ([[lem-intersection-of-subgroups]]).

[L6] $u \mid v$ means $v = uk$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$; equivalently $v \in u\mathbb{Z}$ ([[def-divides-in-z]]).

[L7] $\gcd(a,b) \ge 0$ and is a common divisor of $a$ and $b$; $\gcd(0,0) = 0$ ([[def-common-divisor-and-gcd]]).

[L8] For $(a,b) \ne (0,0)$ there are $x_0, y_0$ with $a x_0 + b y_0 = \gcd(a,b)$ ([[thm-bezout-identity]]).

[L9] $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b) \ge 0$ and is a common multiple of $a$ and $b$ ([[def-lcm]]).

[L10] Every common multiple of $a$ and $b$ is a multiple of $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$ ([[thm-gcd-lcm-product]]).

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 $I := a\mathbb{Z} + b\mathbb{Z}$ is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z},+)$: it contains $0 = a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 0$, so is nonempty, and for $ax + by$ and $ax' + by'$ in $I$ the difference is $a(x - x') + b(y - y') \in I$ by distributivity, so the one-step test applies. [L1, L3]

1.2 $I \subseteq d\mathbb{Z}$. Since $d$ is a common divisor, write $a = d a_1$ and $b = d b_1$; then $ax + by = d(a_1 x + b_1 y) \in d\mathbb{Z}$ for all $x, y$. [L1, L6, L7]

1.3 $d \in I$. If $(a,b) \ne (0,0)$ this is [L8]. If $(a,b) = (0,0)$ then $d = 0 = a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 0 \in I$. The two cases are exhaustive. [L1, L7, L8]

1.4 $a\mathbb{Z} \cap b\mathbb{Z}$ is a subgroup by [L2] and [L5], and by [L6] it is exactly the set of common multiples of $a$ and $b$: $m \in a\mathbb{Z}$ says $a \mid m$, and $m \in b\mathbb{Z}$ says $b \mid m$. [L2, L5, L6]

2.1 $I = \langle \{a,b\} \rangle$. It contains $a = a \cdot 1 + b \cdot 0$ and $b = a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 1$, so $\langle \{a,b\} \rangle \subseteq I$ by [L4]. Conversely any subgroup $H$ containing $a$ and $b$ contains $\langle a \rangle = a\mathbb{Z}$ and $\langle b \rangle = b\mathbb{Z}$ by [L2] and [L4], hence contains $ax + by$ for all $x, y$ by closure under the operation; taking $H = \langle \{a,b\} \rangle$ gives $I \subseteq \langle \{a,b\} \rangle$. [step 1.1, L1, L2, L3, L4]

2.2 Hence $d\mathbb{Z} = \langle d \rangle \subseteq I$, because $I$ is a subgroup containing $d$ and $\langle d \rangle$ is the smallest such. With step 1.2, $I = d\mathbb{Z}$. [step 1.1, step 1.2, step 1.3, L2, L4]

2.3 $\ell\mathbb{Z} \subseteq a\mathbb{Z} \cap b\mathbb{Z}$: $\ell$ is a common multiple by [L9], so $\ell$ lies in the subgroup $a\mathbb{Z} \cap b\mathbb{Z}$, and $\ell\mathbb{Z} = \langle \ell \rangle$ is the smallest subgroup containing $\ell$. [step 1.4, L2, L4, L9]

3.1 $a\mathbb{Z} \cap b\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \ell\mathbb{Z}$: every common multiple $m$ satisfies $\ell \mid m$ by [L10], that is $m \in \ell\mathbb{Z}$. With step 2.3, $a\mathbb{Z} \cap b\mathbb{Z} = \ell\mathbb{Z}$. [step 1.4, step 2.3, L6, L10]

4.1 Both identities are proved, and in group-theoretic form they read $\langle \{a,b\} \rangle = I = d\mathbb{Z} = \langle d \rangle$ by step 2.1 and step 2.2, and $\langle a \rangle \cap \langle b \rangle = a\mathbb{Z} \cap b\mathbb{Z} = \ell\mathbb{Z} = \langle \ell \rangle$ by step 3.1 and [L2]. [step 2.1, step 2.2, step 3.1, L2]

5.1 Finally $d \ge 0$ and $\ell \ge 0$, so each is $\iota(n)$ for a natural $n$, and by [L2] a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z},+)$ has exactly one such generator; hence $d$ and $\ell$ are *the* canonical generators of the two subgroups, and the identities identify the subgroups and not merely the underlying sets. [step 4.1, L2, L7, L9] ∎

## Remarks

- **This is the seam between the arithmetic and the group theory of this page**,
  and it is the concrete shadow of the statement that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal
  ideal domain. Read from left to right, it says that the set of integer
  combinations of $a$ and $b$ is exactly the set of multiples of their greatest
  common divisor — which is Bézout's identity and the divisibility
  characterisation of $\gcd$ packed into one equation between subgroups.

- **The classification is used for one thing only.** Both inclusions above come
  from Bézout and divisibility; [[lem-subgroups-of-z-are-cyclic]] is invoked in
  step 5.1, for the uniqueness of the nonnegative generator, and nowhere else.

- **Boundary pairs.** At $(a,b) = (0,0)$ the theorem reads
  $\{0\} + \{0\} = 0\mathbb{Z} = \{0\}$ and $\{0\} \cap \{0\} = 0\mathbb{Z}$,
  using $\gcd(0,0) = 0$ and $\operatorname{lcm}(0,0) = 0$; at $a = 0$ and
  $b \ne 0$ it reads $\{0\} + b\mathbb{Z} = |b|\mathbb{Z}$ and
  $\{0\} \cap b\mathbb{Z} = \{0\}$. Both are true as stated, and neither needed a
  separate clause.
