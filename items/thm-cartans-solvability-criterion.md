## Statement

Let $\mathfrak g$ be finite-dimensional over a characteristic-zero field.
Then $\mathfrak g$ is solvable if and only if
$K_{\mathfrak g}(\mathfrak g,[\mathfrak g,\mathfrak g])=0$.

More generally, a finite-dimensional linear Lie algebra
$\mathfrak h\subseteq\mathfrak{gl}(V)$ is solvable if
$\operatorname{tr}(xy)=0$ for all $x\in[\mathfrak h,\mathfrak h]$ and
$y\in\mathfrak h$; when $\mathfrak h$ is solvable, these traces do vanish.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Finite-dimensional vector spaces and Lie algebras over a field of characteristic zero.

[L1] Every finite-dimensional representation of a solvable algebra over an algebraically closed characteristic-zero field is simultaneously upper triangularizable ([[cor-simultaneous-upper-triangularization-of-solvable-lie-algebra-representations]]).

[L2] If every adjoint operator of a finite-dimensional algebra is nilpotent, that algebra is nilpotent ([[thm-engels-theorem]]).

[L3] An algebra is solvable exactly when its derived algebra is nilpotent in the linear situation used here ([[thm-lies-criterion-for-solvability-by-the-derived-algebra]]).

[L4] Solvable ideals and solvable quotients give solvable extensions ([[prop-subalgebras-quotients-and-extensions-of-solvable-lie-algebras]]).

[L5] Finite-dimensional traces are cyclic ([[thm-trace-of-ab-equals-trace-of-ba]]).

[L6] Every finite-dimensional endomorphism over an algebraically closed field has Jordan canonical form ([[cor-endomorphisms-over-an-algebraically-closed-field-have-jordan-form]]).

[L7] The Killing form is the trace form of the adjoint representation ([[def-killing-form-of-a-finite-dimensional-lie-algebra]]).

## Proof

**Proof technique:** scalar reduction, Jordan decomposition, and Engel.

1.1 Suppose first that $\mathfrak h$ is solvable. Choose bases of $\mathfrak h$ and $V$, and let $k_0\subseteq k$ be the subfield generated over $\mathbb Q$ by the finitely many structure constants and matrix entries. The resulting $k_0$-form $\mathfrak h_0$ is solvable because its derived series becomes that of $\mathfrak h$ after the faithful scalar extension $k_0\subseteq k$. The finitely generated field $k_0$ embeds in $\mathbb C$. Over $\mathbb C$, [L1] gives a basis in which $\mathfrak h_0$ is upper triangular and its derived algebra is strictly upper triangular. Thus $\operatorname{tr}(yx)=0$ for all $x\in[\mathfrak h_0,\mathfrak h_0]$ and $y\in\mathfrak h_0$. These finitely many bilinear identities hold over $k_0$ and hence after extension to $k$. [L1, L5, algebra]

1.2 We prove the converse first under the stronger hypothesis $\operatorname{tr}(xy)=0$ for every $x,y\in\mathfrak h$. Choose bases and let $k_0$ be the characteristic-zero subfield generated by the finitely many matrix entries of a basis of $\mathfrak h$ and its structure constants. The strong trace hypothesis is determined by the finitely many pairs of basis vectors, so it persists after extending the resulting $k_0$-form to $\mathbb C$. Solvability over $\mathbb C$ descends because every derived term commutes with scalar extension. We may therefore carry out the strong converse over $\mathbb C$. [L5, algebra]

1.3 Over $\mathbb C$, fix $x\in[\mathfrak h,\mathfrak h]$. By [L6], choose a Jordan basis on $V$ and write $x=x_s+x_n$, where $x_s$ is diagonal, $x_n$ is block-nilpotent, and they commute. The commuting left and right multiplication operators on $\operatorname{End}(V)$ show that the semisimple part of $\operatorname{ad}_x=L_x-R_x$ is $\operatorname{ad}_{x_s}$. Write the diagonal entries of $x_s$ as $a_1,\ldots,a_n$, and let $\overline{x_s}$ have diagonal entries $\overline{a_1},\ldots, \overline{a_n}$. On the generalized eigenspaces of $\operatorname{ad}_x$, $\operatorname{ad}_{\overline{x_s}}$ has eigenvalues $\overline{a_i-a_j}$. Finite Hermite interpolation on the finitely many differences $a_i-a_j$, taking zero to zero, expresses $\operatorname{ad}_{\overline{x_s}}$ as a polynomial without constant term in $\operatorname{ad}_x$. Since $\mathfrak h$ is stable under $\operatorname{ad}_x$, it follows that $[\overline{x_s},\mathfrak h]\subseteq\mathfrak h$. [L6, algebra]

2.1 Write $x$ as a finite sum of commutators. By [L5], $\operatorname{tr}(A[B,C])=\operatorname{tr}([A,B]C)$. Step 1.3 and the strong trace hypothesis therefore give $\operatorname{tr}(\overline{x_s}x)=0$. In the Jordan basis this trace is $\sum_i\overline{a_i}a_i$, so every $a_i=0$ and $x$ is nilpotent. If $x^m=0$ on $V$, then the commuting operators $L_x,R_x$ on $\operatorname{End}(V)$ satisfy $(L_x-R_x)^{2m-1}=0$, because every binomial term contains either $L_x^m$ or $R_x^m$. Restriction to the invariant derived algebra shows that each of its adjoint operators is nilpotent. Hence [L2] makes $[\mathfrak h,\mathfrak h]$ nilpotent, and [L3] makes $\mathfrak h$ solvable. [L2, L3, L5, step 1.3, algebra]

3.1 Under the stated weaker hypothesis, apply steps 1.2–2.1 to the linear Lie algebra $[\mathfrak h,\mathfrak h]$: the required strong trace vanishing holds for every pair in it. Its solvability implies solvability of $\mathfrak h$ by the derived-series definition. Together with step 1.1 this proves the linear criterion. [step 1.1, step 1.2, step 1.3, step 2.1, algebra]

4.1 Apply the linear criterion to $\operatorname{ad}(\mathfrak g)$. Its derived algebra is $\operatorname{ad}([\mathfrak g,\mathfrak g])$, and [L7] says that the trace hypothesis is precisely the asserted Killing-form condition. Hence $\operatorname{ad}(\mathfrak g)$ is solvable. The exact sequence $0\to Z(\mathfrak g)\to\mathfrak g\to\operatorname{ad}(\mathfrak g)\to0$ has abelian kernel, so [L4] makes $\mathfrak g$ solvable. The reverse direction is step 1.1 for the adjoint representation. The zero algebra makes both conditions vacuous and is solvable. [L4, L7, step 1.1, step 3.1] ∎
