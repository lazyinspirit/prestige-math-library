## Statement

Assume AC and let $A$ be a finite symmetrizable GCM over $\mathbb C$. Every integrable module in the finite-cone, finite-weight-space category $\mathcal O$ is an algebraic direct sum of simple highest-weight modules $L_A(\lambda)$ with $\lambda\in P^+$. The zero module is the empty direct sum. AC is used to select bases simultaneously in the set-indexed family of finite-dimensional singular weight spaces.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** AC, the symmetrizable GCM, and an integrable $V\in\mathcal O$.

[F1] The Casimir decomposes $V$ as a direct sum of integrable $\mathcal O$ submodules, in each of which primitive weights form an antichain ([[lem-casimir-separates-comparable-dominant-primitive-weights]]).

[F2] Maximal-weight vectors generate integrable highest-weight modules and primitive weights are dominant integral ([[lem-an-integrable-o-module-is-generated-by-dominant-maximal-weights]]).

[F3] Every simple highest-weight module of weight $\lambda$ is the unique simple Verma quotient $L_A(\lambda)$ ([[thm-kac-moody-verma-module-has-a-unique-simple-quotient]]).

[F4] AC permits choices from arbitrary families of nonempty sets ([[def-axiom-of-choice]]).

[F5] Category $\mathcal O$ has finite-dimensional weight spaces and finite upper support sets, and is closed under submodules and quotients ([[def-kac-moody-category-o]]).

[F6] A module generated by a highest vector of weight $\lambda$ is a Verma quotient with support in $\lambda-Q^+$ and top dimension one ([[thm-universal-property-and-pbw-character-of-kac-moody-verma-modules]]).

## Proof

1.1 Work first in one Casimir summand $C$ of F1. If $v\in C_\lambda$ is nonzero and singular, meaning killed by $\mathfrak n^+$, its cyclic module $M$ has support in $\lambda-Q^+$ and top $\mathbb Cv$ by F6. A proper submodule misses that top, since containing $v$ would generate all of $M$. If a proper nonzero submodule existed, F5 gives it a maximal support weight $\mu$: take a maximal element in the finite upper set above any of its weights. Every vector there is singular. Thus $\mu<\lambda$ and both are primitive weights of $C$, contradicting F1. Hence $M$ is simple, and F2 and F3 identify it with $L_A(\lambda)$ for dominant integral $\lambda$. In this simple module the only singular vectors lie in its top line: any other nonzero singular vector would generate it by simplicity, and F6 would force its original top to be at or below a strictly lower weight, impossible. [F1, F2, F3, F5, F6, given]

2.1 For each eigenvalue $a$ and weight $\lambda$, let $C=V^{(a)}$ and $C^0_\lambda=\{v\in C_\lambda:\mathfrak n^+v=0\}$. It is finite dimensional by F5. Its set of finite ordered bases is nonempty, with the empty basis for zero dimension. The pairs $(a,\lambda)$ form a set. Apply F4 to choose a basis $B_{a,\lambda}$ for every such space. This is the specified use of AC. For fixed $a$, each basis vector $b$ generates a simple submodule $M_b$ by 1.1. There is a natural module map from their algebraic external direct sum to $C$, sending each copy of $M_b$ to its given submodule. [F4, F5, step 1.1]

3.1 This map is injective. Otherwise a nonzero kernel vector has finite summand support, so its kernel inside the corresponding finite external direct sum $D$ is nonzero. The module $D$ belongs to $\mathcal O$: its finite-dimensional weight spaces and finite-cone bounds are finite sums of those of the summands. By F5 its kernel has a maximal support weight and a nonzero singular vector $z$ there. Projecting $z$ to each simple summand gives singular vectors; by 1.1 each component is a scalar multiple of that summand's chosen top vector, and only summands with the weight of $z$ can contribute. Its image in $C$ is thus a linear relation among distinct members of $B_{a,\lambda}$. Their independence makes every scalar zero, contradicting $z\ne0$. Hence the sum $S=\sum_b M_b$ is direct. [F5, step 1.1, step 2.1]

4.1 Suppose $C/S\ne0$. By F5 it has a maximal support weight $\mu$ and a nonzero highest vector there. The quotient weight-space description supplies a lift $v\in C_\mu$, which is primitive in $C$ and does not belong to $S$. For every $i$, $e_iv\in S$. If one is nonzero, its finite expression in the direct sum from 3.1 has a nonzero component of weight $\mu+\alpha_i$ in some $M_b$ of highest weight $\lambda$. F6 gives $\lambda\geq\mu+\alpha_i>\mu$. The weights $\lambda$ and $\mu$ are both primitive in $C$, contrary to F1. Thus every $e_i v=0$, hence their generated algebra $\mathfrak n^+$ kills $v$. It lies in $C^0_\mu$, whose chosen basis is contained in $S$, so $v\in S$, again a contradiction. Therefore $C=S$. [F1, F5, F6, step 2.1, step 3.1]

5.1 Reassemble the Casimir direct sum from F1. Steps 1.1–4.1 give a direct sum of dominant simple highest-weight modules in each component, so their combined algebraic sum is the stated decomposition of $V$. Each vector has finitely many Casimir components and finitely many simple components within each. Zero singular spaces contribute empty bases and no summands; the zero module gives the empty sum. One-dimensional singular spaces require just one generator, and zero dominant labels cause no exception. No unproved splitting theorem is used: injectivity and spanning were proved separately. All choices beyond the single family of bases in 2.1 concern finitely many elements or individual witnesses. [F1, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.1, step 4.1] ∎
