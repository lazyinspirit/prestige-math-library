## Example

In the ring $\mathbb Z[\sqrt2,\sqrt3]$, every element is a $\mathbb Z$-linear combination of $1,\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt6$. In particular the ring is finite over $\mathbb Z$, and the sum $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ and product $\sqrt6$ of the two quadratic generators are integral over $\mathbb Z$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The subring $R:=\mathbb Z[\sqrt2,\sqrt3]\subseteq\mathbb R$.

[L1] A subalgebra generated by finitely many integral elements is module-finite ([[lem-algebra-generated-by-finitely-many-integral-elements-is-module-finite]]).

[L2] Over a nonzero base ring, integral elements form a subring ([[cor-integral-elements-form-a-subring]]).

## Verification

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 The elements $\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt3$ satisfy the monic equations $T^2-2=0$ and $T^2-3=0$ over $\mathbb Z$, so they are integral over $\mathbb Z$. Therefore [L1] implies that $R$ is a finitely generated $\mathbb Z$-module. [L1, given]

2.1 Every element of $R$ is a polynomial in $\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt3$. Replacing $(\sqrt2)^2$ by $2$ and $(\sqrt3)^2$ by $3$ reduces every monomial to a $\mathbb Z$-linear combination of $1,\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt6$, so these four elements span $R$ over $\mathbb Z$. [step 1.1, given, algebra]

3.1 Since $\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt3$ are integral over $\mathbb Z$, [L2] implies that their sum and product are integral over $\mathbb Z$. Here the product is exactly $\sqrt6$, and the spanning result of step 2.1 places both elements inside one explicit finite $\mathbb Z$-module. [L2, step 2.1] ∎
