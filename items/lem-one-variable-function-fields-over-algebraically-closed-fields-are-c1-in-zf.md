## Statement

Let k be an algebraically closed field and let K/k be finitely generated of transcendence degree one. For every d≥1 and every homogeneous form F∈K[X₁,…,X_s] of degree d with s>d, there is a nonzero tuple x∈K^s with F(x)=0. No choice principle is assumed. In particular K is a C1 field. This statement does not assert a Brauer/cohomology comparison or an étale cohomological-dimension bound.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** k, K, d, s and F as in the statement.

[F1] Every nonzero finite-type k-algebra is a finite module over a polynomial k-subalgebra. [[cor-noether-normalisation-module-finiteness]]

[F2] An algebraically closed field has no proper finite algebraic extension. [[def-algebraically-closed-field]]

[F3] A field extension generated by finitely many algebraic elements is finite. [[thm-finitely-generated-algebraic-extensions-are-finite]]

## Proof

1.1 Every nonzero finite-type k-algebra A has a k-point, without a choice principle. By [F1], A is finite over R=k[z₁,…,z_t] for algebraically independent z_i. Let m=(z₁,…,z_t). The injection R→A remains injective after localization, so A_m is a nonzero finite R_m-module. The determinant form of Nakayama shows A_m/mA_m≠0: otherwise a unit of R_m would annihilate A_m. Hence A/mA is a nonzero finite-dimensional k-algebra. A proper ideal of maximal k-dimension exists by the finite dimension bound and is maximal. Its quotient is a finite field extension of k, equal to k by [F2]. Its quotient map gives A→k. [F1, F2, given, algebra]

2.1 Let r<N and let f₁,…,f_r be homogeneous positive-degree forms in A=k[x₁,…,x_N]. They have a common nonzero k-zero. Suppose otherwise, and let I=(f₁,…,f_r). For each j, the algebra A[z]/(I,x_jz−1) has no k-point, so is zero by step 1.1. Clearing the finite denominator in A[x_j⁻¹] gives x_j^{N_j}∈I for some N_j≥1. Since I is homogeneous, take the degree-N_j part of a finite representation and write x_j^{N_j}=∑_i f_i g_{ij} with each nonzero g_{ij} homogeneous of degree N_j−deg f_i<N_j. [step 1.1, given, algebra]

2.2 Let B=k[f₁,…,f_r]. The identities of step 2.1 make A a finite B-module: whenever a monomial contains x_j^{N_j}, replace that factor by ∑f_i g_{ij}; the remaining x-degree strictly decreases, so finite induction expresses the monomial using the finitely many x-monomials with exponents <N_j. More precisely, a monomial of total x-degree ≤T is a linear combination of those fixed monomials with coefficients that are polynomials of f-degree ≤T. If M=∏_jN_j, this gives dim_k A_{≤T}≤M binom(T+r,r). But dim_k A_{≤T}=binom(T+N,N), which exceeds that bound for sufficiently large T because N>r. The contradiction proves the nonzero common zero. Zero forms can be omitted; when no forms remain, a standard basis vector is a nonzero zero. [step 2.1, algebra]

3.1 Choose t∈K transcendental over k. Since K/k has transcendence degree one and is finitely generated, every element in a finite generating list is algebraic over k(t). By [F3], K/k(t) is finite. Fix one finite k(t)-basis b₁,…,b_e. These are finite choices for this specified field, not an indexed choice family. [F3, given, choose, algebra]

3.2 For a positive integer N, seek each x_i in the form x_i=∑_{h=1}^e∑_{j=0}^N u_{ihj}t^j b_h, with unknown coefficients u_{ihj}∈k. Expand F(x) in the basis b_h and clear the finitely many denominators of the coefficients of F and of the finitely many products of basis elements. The coefficient of each b_h becomes a polynomial in t whose coefficients are homogeneous degree-d polynomials in the u variables. There is a fixed C, independent of N, for which all these t-degrees are at most dN+C. Thus F(x)=0 is imposed by at most e(dN+C+1) homogeneous polynomial equations in se(N+1) variables. Because s>d, choose N large enough that the number of variables is strictly larger than the number of equations. [step 3.1, given, construct, algebra]

4.1 By step 2.2 the finite homogeneous system in step 3.2 has a nonzero solution in k. It makes F(x)=0. At least one x_i is nonzero: the b_h are a k(t)-basis and a nonzero polynomial in t with coefficients in k cannot vanish because t is transcendental. Thus x is a nonzero K-tuple solving F=0, proving the C1 property. [step 2.2, step 3.2, algebra] ∎

## Scope

This direct coefficient argument proves C1 without selecting an algebraic closure of K or constructing a curve model. It does not identify the Brauer group with Galois cohomology and does not by itself prove a cohomological-dimension or finiteness theorem.
