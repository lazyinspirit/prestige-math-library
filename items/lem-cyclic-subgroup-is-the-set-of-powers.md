## Statement

Let $G$ be a group and $g \in G$, with integer powers as in [[def-group-power]].
Then

$$\langle g \rangle \;=\; \{\, g^{n} \;:\; n \in \mathbb{Z} \,\} ,$$

the cyclic subgroup generated by $g$ ([[def-generated-subgroup]]) being exactly
the set of integer powers of $g$. Consequently every cyclic group is abelian, and
so is every cyclic subgroup of any group.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A group $G$ with identity $e$, an element $g \in G$, and the set $P := \{\, g^{n} : n \in \mathbb{Z} \,\}$ of its integer powers ([[def-group-power]]).

[L1] $\langle g \rangle$ is the smallest subgroup of $G$ containing $g$: it is a subgroup, it contains $g$, and it is contained in every subgroup containing $g$ ([[def-generated-subgroup]]).

[L2] Exponent laws: $g^{m+n} = g^{m}g^{n}$, $g^{-m} = (g^{m})^{-1}$, and $g^{m}g^{n} = g^{n}g^{m}$, for all $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$ ([[lem-group-power-laws]]).

[L3] $g^{0} = e$, $g^{1} = g$, and $g^{\sigma(k)} = g^{k} g$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}$; a natural number $k$ in an exponent means the integer $\iota(k)$ ([[def-group-power]], [[lem-nat-embeds-int]]).

[L4] One-step test: a nonempty $S \subseteq G$ with $x y^{-1} \in S$ for all $x, y \in S$ is a subgroup ([[lem-subgroup-criterion]], [[def-subgroup]]).

[L5] $\mathbb{Z}$ is a commutative ring, so $m + (-n)$ is again an integer and addition is commutative ([[thm-int-comm-ring]], [[def-integers]]); its order is total, and every $x \ge 0$ is $\iota(k)$ for a unique natural $k$ while every $x < 0$ has $-x = \iota(k)$ for a unique natural $k$ ([[thm-int-ordered-ring]], [[def-int-order]], [[lem-nat-embeds-int]]).

[L6] Induction on $\mathbb{N}$ ([[thm-induction-principle]]).

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 $P$ is nonempty, since $g^{0} = e \in P$, and $P \subseteq G$ by construction. [L3, given]

1.2 For $x = g^{m}$ and $y = g^{n}$ in $P$: $x y^{-1} = g^{m}(g^{n})^{-1} = g^{m} g^{-n} = g^{m+(-n)} \in P$, since $m + (-n)$ is an integer. [L2, L5]

1.3 $g = g^{1} \in P$. [L3]

1.4 Let $H$ be any subgroup of $G$ with $g \in H$. Then $g^{k} \in H$ for every natural $k$: the set of such $k$ contains $0$, because $g^{0} = e \in H$, and it is closed under $\sigma$, because $g^{\sigma(k)} = g^{k} g$ is a product of two elements of $H$; induction finishes it. [L3, L4, L6, given]

2.1 By steps 1.1 and 1.2 and the one-step test, $P$ is a subgroup of $G$, and by step 1.3 it contains $g$; hence $\langle g \rangle \subseteq P$, since $\langle g \rangle$ is contained in every subgroup containing $g$. [step 1.1, step 1.2, step 1.3, L1, L4]

2.2 Let $H$ be any subgroup of $G$ with $g \in H$, and let $x \in \mathbb{Z}$. If $x \ge 0$ then $x = \iota(k)$ and $g^{x} = g^{k} \in H$ by step 1.4. If $x < 0$ then $-x = \iota(k)$, so $g^{x} = (g^{k})^{-1}$, which lies in $H$ because $g^{k} \in H$ by step 1.4 and $H$ is closed under inverses. So $P \subseteq H$. [step 1.4, L3, L4, L5]

3.1 Taking $H = \langle g \rangle$ in step 2.2, which is legitimate because $\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup containing $g$, gives $P \subseteq \langle g \rangle$; with step 2.1 this proves $\langle g \rangle = P$. [step 2.1, step 2.2, L1]

4.1 Any two elements of $P$ commute: $g^{m} g^{n} = g^{n} g^{m}$ for all integers $m, n$. Hence $\langle g \rangle$ is abelian, by step 3.1. [step 3.1, L2]

5.1 If $G$ is cyclic, say $G = \langle g \rangle$, then $G$ is abelian by step 4.1; and every cyclic subgroup of any group is abelian for the same reason. [step 3.1, step 4.1, L1] ∎

## Remarks

- The two inclusions are proved by different means, and both are needed: one shows the set of powers is *a* subgroup containing $g$, the other shows *every* subgroup containing $g$ swallows all the powers. Only together do they identify the smallest one.

- Step 1.4 is where induction does the work, and it is stated for natural exponents only; negative exponents are then handled in step 2.2 by closure under inverses, using $g^{x} = (g^{k})^{-1}$ from the definition of integer powers rather than a second induction.

- Nothing here says $\langle g \rangle$ is finite, or that distinct exponents give distinct powers. Both questions are settled by [[lem-order-characterisation]], which shows the powers repeat with period $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ when that is finite and are pairwise distinct when it is not.
