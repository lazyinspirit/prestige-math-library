## Example

Compare the standard affine diagrams $\tilde B_n$ and $\tilde C_n$ ([[def-cg-standard-affine-diagrams]] (2),(3)) and the crystallographic scalings that produce them:

**(i) The coincidence at $n=2$.** $\tilde B_2=\tilde C_2$ is the three-vertex path with edge labels $(4,4)$: the $n=2$ member is defined by the coincidence convention, and both its edges carry label $4$. Both arise from the same finite type: $B_2$ and $C_2$ are the same Coxeter system ([[thm-cg-crystallographic-finite-type-and-lattice-stability]] (1) and the finite coincidence $B_2=C_2=I_2(4)$ of [[thm-cg-finite-coxeter-classification-including-h-and-dihedral]] (4)), and the $B_n$- and $C_n$-scalings with $n=2$ are exchanged by duality ([[thm-cg-crystallographic-finite-type-and-lattice-stability]] (4)).

**(ii) The difference for $n\ge3$.** $\tilde B_n$ has a vertex of degree $3$ (the vertex $v_2$ carrying the extra $v_0$) and exactly one edge labelled $4$, namely $\{v_{n-1},v_n\}$; $\tilde C_n$ is a path and has exactly two edges labelled $4$, at its two ends. Hence for $n\ge3$ the two diagrams are not isomorphic: any graph isomorphism maps each vertex's neighbor set bijectively to the corresponding neighbor set and therefore preserves degrees, but the degree sequences disagree.

**(iii) Both are affine.** By [[lem-cg-affine-type-crystallographic-alcove-diagrams]] (3), the cosine matrices of $\tilde B_n$ and $\tilde C_n$ are positive semidefinite of corank one with positive kernel vectors; concretely, for $\tilde C_n$ the vector $(1,\sqrt2,\sqrt2,\dots,\sqrt2,1)$ (first and last entry $1$) lies in the kernel, and for $\tilde B_n$ with $n\ge3$ the vector $(1,1,2,2,\dots,2,\sqrt2)$ (entries ordered $v_0,v_1,v_2,v_3,\dots,v_n$, i.e. $x_0=x_1=1$, $x_2=\dots=x_{n-1}=2$, $x_n=\sqrt2$) lies in the kernel; in both cases the entries are positive.

**(iv) Why the Coxeter diagram alone does not decide the lattice.** $B_n$ and $C_n$ have the same finite Coxeter diagram but their two crystallographic scalings are dual ([[thm-cg-crystallographic-finite-type-and-lattice-stability]] (4), [[def-cg-crystallographic-scaling-coroot-and-lattice]]); the fundamental alcoves are bounded $n$-simplices (triangles when $n=2$) ([[lem-cg-highest-root-and-fundamental-alcove]] (2)), but the affine Weyl groups $W_a(B_n)=Q^\vee\rtimes W$ and $W_a(C_n)={Q^\vee}'\rtimes W$ ([[def-cg-affine-root-hyperplane-reflection-and-alcove]], [[lem-cg-affine-reflection-identities-and-local-finiteness]] (4)) realize the two different Coxeter diagrams $\tilde B_n$ and $\tilde C_n$ for $n\ge3$ ([[lem-cg-affine-type-crystallographic-alcove-diagrams]] (1),(5), [[thm-cg-affine-alcove-transitivity-presentation-and-length]] (2)), and for $n\ge3$ they are not isomorphic even as abstract groups: $W_a(B_n)$ has a maximal finite subgroup of order $2^{n-1}n!$, whereas $W_a(C_n)$ has none, as proved below. Thus the Coxeter diagram of the finite system does not determine which lattice acts.

**(v) Convention warning.** The labels $4$ here are orders of facet-reflection products, i.e. Coxeter labels ($\cos(\pi/4)={\sqrt2}/{2}$) ([[lem-cg-affine-type-crystallographic-alcove-diagrams]] (1)), not Lie-theoretic bond multiplicities; the extended affine Weyl group $P^\vee\rtimes W$ is a further, different object ([[thm-cg-affine-alcove-transitivity-presentation-and-length]] (4)).

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The two affine families and their finite $B_n,C_n$ root-system scalings.

[F1] The $n=2$ coincidence is a definition, and for $n\ge3$ the $B$ recipe has one branch and one $4$-edge, while the $C$ recipe is a path with two $4$-edges ([[def-cg-standard-affine-diagrams]] (2),(3),(7)).

[F2] The cosine matrices of both standard affine diagrams are positive semidefinite of corank one and have positive kernel vectors ([[lem-cg-affine-type-crystallographic-alcove-diagrams]] (3)).

[F3] The affine facet-product labels $m^\Phi_{ij}=\operatorname{ord}(s_is_j)$ give the standard affine diagrams of the corresponding $B_n,C_n$ root systems, which occur in the list; their $n=2$ convention is shared ([[lem-cg-affine-type-crystallographic-alcove-diagrams]] (1),(4)).

[F4] The finite $B_k,C_k$ systems have the same Coxeter diagram and its label-$4$ path admits the two dual crystallographic scalings ([[thm-cg-crystallographic-finite-type-and-lattice-stability]] (1),(4)).

[F5] By the finite-type and crystallographic theorems, the standard finite Coxeter groups of types $B_k,C_k$, and $D_k$ for $k\ge4$ are isomorphic to Weyl groups of based crystallographic root systems with the standard generators matched to simple-root reflections. The based-root uniqueness theorem identifies these with the coordinate root systems of [F6] when the Cartan matrices agree ([[thm-cg-crystallographic-finite-type-and-lattice-stability]] (2), [[thm-a-based-root-system-is-determined-up-to-isomorphism-by-its-cartan-matrix]]).

[F6] The coordinate root sets and simple roots for $A_n,B_n,C_n,D_n$ are those displayed in [[ex-classical-root-systems-in-euclidean-coordinates]].

[F7] The coroot is $\alpha^\vee=2\alpha/B(\alpha,\alpha)$, and the affine Weyl group has the Euclidean decomposition $W_a=Q^\vee\rtimes W$ ([[def-cg-affine-root-hyperplane-reflection-and-alcove]], [[lem-cg-affine-reflection-identities-and-local-finiteness]] (4)).

[F8] The affine-Gram classification theorem supplies the faithful slice model, its affine relation and intersection formula, strict closed fundamental domains, and finiteness of proper standard parabolics ([[thm-cg-affine-gram-classification-and-euclidean-realization]] (2)–(4)).

[F9] The finite Coxeter classification gives $A_1=B_1$, $B_2=C_2=I_2(4)$ and $D_3=A_3$ ([[thm-cg-finite-coxeter-classification-including-h-and-dihedral]] (4)).

[F10] Support determines standard-parabolic membership and the restricted Coxeter presentation ([[thm-hh-parabolic-minimal-representatives-and-length-additivity]] (1)–(2)).

[F11] The affine facet reflections generate the affine Weyl group, have the exact Coxeter presentation and act simply transitively on alcoves; the comparison clauses distinguish the extended lattice group ([[thm-cg-affine-alcove-transitivity-presentation-and-length]] (1)–(4)).

[F12] $\pi>0$, $\cos(\pi/2)=0$, cosine is strictly decreasing on $[0,\pi]$, $\cos(2x)=2\cos^2x-1$, $\cos(\pi-x)=-\cos x$, and every nonnegative real has a unique nonnegative square root ([[def-pi-via-first-positive-cosine-zero]], [[thm-quarter-turn-values-and-shift-formulas]], [[thm-sine-cosine-signs-monotonicity-and-ranges]], [[thm-double-angle-and-power-reduction-identities]], [[thm-cofunction-supplementary-and-reflection-identities]], [[thm-of-square-roots]]).

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct; all finite coordinate constructions use no choice principle.

1.1 At $n=2$, [F1] specifies the common $(4,4)$ path; [F3] realizes it from each dual root-length assignment. The finite systems coincide by [F9], and their two scalings are exchanged by [F4]. For $n\ge3$, the $B$ graph has a degree-three vertex and one $4$-edge, whereas the $C$ graph has degrees at most two and two $4$-edges. A graph isomorphism would biject each vertex's neighbors and preserve degree, which is impossible for these degree sequences. [F1, F3, F4, F9, algebra]

1.2 By [F12], if $c_4:=\cos(\pi/4)$ and $c_3:=\cos(\pi/3)$, then $c_4=\sqrt2/2$ and $c_3=1/2$: positivity follows from $0<\pi/4,\pi/3<\pi/2$ and strict decrease to $\cos(\pi/2)=0$; for $c_4$ the double-angle identity gives $2c_4^2=1$, and for $c_3$ it gives $2c_3^2-1=\cos(2\pi/3)=-c_3$, so $(2c_3-1)(c_3+1)=0$ and $c_3=1/2$. Put $c=c_4$. For $\tilde C_n$, take endpoint coordinates $1$ and all $n-1$ interior coordinates $\sqrt2$. Each endpoint equation is $1-c\sqrt2=0$; at $n=2$ the central equation is $\sqrt2-2c=0$; for $n\ge3$, a vertex next to an end has equation $\sqrt2-c-\sqrt2/2=0$, and any other interior vertex has equation $\sqrt2-(\sqrt2+\sqrt2)/2=0$. For $\tilde B_n$, $n\ge3$, use $x_0=x_1=1$, $x_2=\cdots=x_{n-1}=2$, $x_n=\sqrt2$. At each branch leaf the equation is $1-x_2/2=0$. At $v_2$, the equation is $2-(1+1)/2-c\sqrt2=0$ when $n=3$, and $2-(1+1+2)/2=0$ when $n\ge4$. Every intervening chain vertex (if any) has equation $2-(2+2)/2=0$; the vertex $v_{n-1}$ for $n\ge4$ has equation $2-1-c\sqrt2=0$, and the final vertex has equation $\sqrt2-2c=0$. Thus the displayed vectors are positive kernel vectors; [F2] independently gives the semidefinite corank-one assertion. At $n=2$ use the $C$ vector $(1,\sqrt2,1)$ for both names. [F1, F2, F12]

1.3 In the coordinate models [F6], for $k\ge2$ the finite Coxeter groups of type $B_k$ or $C_k$ are identified by [F5] with the Weyl groups on the displayed root sets. Their simple reflections interchange neighboring coordinates or change the last coordinate's sign; conjugating by permutations permits each sign change. Every root reflection is a signed permutation, and these generators give all signed permutations, so the group has order $2^k k!$. For $D_k$, $k\ge4$, [F5,F6] identify the finite Coxeter group with the Weyl group of roots $\pm e_i\pm e_j$. Its adjacent swaps and the reflection in $e_{k-1}+e_k$ generate exactly the permutations with an even number of sign changes: composing that reflection with the swap gives a double sign change, and conjugates give all pairs. Every root reflection has even sign parity, so the group has order $2^{k-1}k!$. For $k=3$, the remaining all-$3$ path is $A_3=D_3$ by [F9]; its coordinate roots $e_i-e_j$ on the sum-zero subspace of $\mathbb R^4$ give all transpositions of four coordinates, so its group is $S_4$ of order $24$. A one-vertex $A_1$ factor has the sign reflection group of order $2$, and the rank-zero group is trivial. [F5, F6, F9, algebra]

1.4 Every finite subgroup $H$ of either affine group fixes a point: average the finite orbit $H x$ for any $x$; affine linearity makes its barycentre fixed by $H$. By [F8]'s strict closed fundamental domain, conjugate this point into $\bar A$. For $p\in\bar A$, let $T(p)$ be the types of facets containing $p$. The affine relation in [F8] gives $T(p)\subsetneq S$. The intersection formula in [F8] and support criterion [F10] show $\operatorname{Stab}(p)=W_{T(p)}$: if $w p=p$, then $p\in w\bar A\cap\bar A$, so every type in $S(w)$ is in $T(p)$, hence $w\in W_{T(p)}$; conversely every generator indexed by $T(p)$ fixes $p$. This stabilizer is finite by [F8]'s proper-parabolic clause. The face containing $p$ has a vertex $v$; all its containing facet reflections fix $v$, so $H\le W_{T(p)}\le\operatorname{Stab}(v)$. Applying the same stabilizer identity to $v$ shows $\operatorname{Stab}(v)=W_{T(v)}$, a finite proper parabolic. At a vertex $v$, the $n$ incident facets have a unique intersection, so their normals span the ambient direction space and their reflections have common fixed-point set exactly $\{v\}$. If a finite group contained $\operatorname{Stab}(v)$, its barycentre would be fixed by this stabilizer, hence would equal $v$; the group would then be contained in $\operatorname{Stab}(v)$. Thus every vertex stabilizer is maximal finite, and every maximal finite subgroup is conjugate to one. [F8, F10, algebra]

2.1 Set $N=2^n n!$ and let $n\ge3$. Deleting the endpoint $v_n$ of the $4$-edge from $\tilde B_n$ leaves the finite all-$3$ $D_n$ graph (at $n=3$, the path $A_3=D_3$). Its parabolic is the stabilizer of the opposite vertex, hence maximal finite by Step 1.4, and has order $N/2$ by Step 1.3. Deleting vertex $v_i$, $0\le i\le n$, from $\tilde C_n$ leaves two finite terminal-$4$ paths of ranks $i$ and $n-i$, interpreted as $B_1=A_1$ or rank-zero trivial blocks at the ends. Its vertex stabilizer has order $2^n i!(n-i)!$ by the restricted presentation [F10] and Step 1.3; the two blocks commute and their presented group is the direct product. At $i=0,n$ this is $N$. For $1\le i\le n-1$, $\binom ni\ge n$: the ratio $\binom n{i+1}/\binom ni=(n-i)/(i+1)$ shows the binomial coefficients increase to the middle and then decrease symmetrically, so their minimum on these indices is $\binom n1=\binom n{n-1}=n$. Hence the order is at most $N/n<N/2$. By Step 1.4 these are all maximal finite subgroup orders in $W_a(C_n)$. The two groups therefore cannot be abstractly isomorphic, since an isomorphism preserves finiteness, maximality and subgroup order. [F1, F9, F10, step 1.3, step 1.4, algebra]

3.1 The different coroot lattices can also be seen directly. For $B_n$ with short roots $\pm e_i$, its coroots are $\pm2e_i$ and $\pm e_i\pm e_j$, whose integer span is $\{z\in\mathbb Z^n:\sum_i z_i\text{ is even}\}$: the generators have even sum, and subtracting $z_i(e_i-e_n)$ for $i<n$ leaves an even multiple of $e_n$. For $C_n$ with long roots $\pm2e_i$, their coroots include all $\pm e_i$, so the lattice is $\mathbb Z^n$. Both finite Weyl groups are the same signed permutation group by [F6], and [F3] identifies their affine facet diagrams with the standard $\tilde B_n,\tilde C_n$ diagrams; Step 1.1 shows these differ in degree data. Step 2.1 proves the stronger abstract distinction for $n\ge3$. Their chambers have dimension $n$; when $n=2$ the common affine diagram yields isomorphic Coxeter groups by [F3,F11] despite the dual coordinate normalizations. The decomposition [F7] identifies the translation lattices in these semidirect products with the two coroot lattices just computed. Finally, label $4$ is the actual product order by [F3], not a bond multiplicity, and the extended weight-lattice group is a distinct comparison object by [F11]. All comparisons use finite coordinates and averaging, without Choice. [F3, F6, F7, F11, step 1.1, step 1.3, step 2.1, algebra] ∎
