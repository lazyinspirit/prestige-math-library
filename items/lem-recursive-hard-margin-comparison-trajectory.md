## Statement

Let $v_1,\ldots,v_n\in\mathbb R^d$, with $n,d\ge1$, be strictly linearly separable, let $w^*$ be their unique Euclidean hard-margin vector, and let $\eta>0$. There are $1\le M\le d$, mutually orthogonal nonzero vectors $h_1,\ldots,h_M$ with $h_1=w^*$, fixed vectors $c,d_{k,m}$, and, for all sufficiently large integers $t$, a comparison sequence
$$G_t=\sum_{m=1}^M h_m\log^{\circ m}t+c+\sum_{1\le k<m\le M}\frac{d_{k,m}}{\prod_{j=k}^{m-1}\log^{\circ j}t},$$
whose margins $v_i^{\mathsf T}G_t$ all tend to $+\infty$. For each of $\mathcal L_{\exp}(w)=\sum_i e^{-v_i^{\mathsf T}w}$ and $\mathcal L_{\log}(w)=\sum_i\log(1+e^{-v_i^{\mathsf T}w})$, the defect
$$\delta_t=G_{t+1}-G_t+\eta\nabla\mathcal L(G_t)$$
is absolutely summable: $\sum_{t\ge T}\|\delta_t\|_2<\infty$ for some finite $T$. The hierarchy is obtained by recursively separating and projecting precisely the active constraints that have zero coefficient in every nonnegative dual representation; it imposes no generic-position assumption. If all first-stage active indices have a simultaneously positive dual representation, then $M=1$ and the correction sum is empty.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The strictly separable finite vectors and the positive fixed number $\eta$ in the Statement.

[F1] [[def-linear-separable-margin-and-hard-margin-svm]]: strict separability gives a vector with all margins positive, and hard-margin constraints require each margin to be at least one.

[F2] [[lem-hard-margin-svm-has-unique-minimizer]]: every finite strictly separable sample has a unique minimum-norm hard-margin vector.

[F3] [[thm-euclidean-heine-borel-pseudocompactness-and-extreme-values]]: nonempty closed bounded Euclidean sets are compact, and continuous real-valued functions attain their extrema there.

[F4] [[thm-farkas-alternative-for-finite-linear-systems]]: exactly one of $A\lambda=b$, $\lambda\ge0$, or $A^{\mathsf T}y\ge0$, $b^{\mathsf T}y<0$, is feasible for a finite real matrix and right-hand side.

## Proof

1.1 Set $H_0=\mathbb R^d$, $R_0=I$, and $T_0=\{1,\dots,n\}$. Suppose $T_{m-1}$ is nonempty and the projected vectors $a_i=R_{m-1}v_i$, $i\in T_{m-1}$, are strictly separable inside $H_{m-1}$. Here $R_{m-1}$ is the orthogonal projector onto $H_{m-1}$. Let $h_m\in H_{m-1}$ be the unique minimum-norm vector with $h_m^Ta_i\ge1$. Existence and uniqueness follow by restricting to a nonempty compact norm sublevel of the closed feasible set and strict convexity, exactly as for the original hard-margin problem. Let $J_m=\{i\in T_{m-1}:h_m^Ta_i=1\}$ and $D_m=T_{m-1}\setminus J_m$. There exist coefficients $\alpha_i\ge0$, $i\in J_m$, with $$h_m=\sum_{i\in J_m}\alpha_i a_i.$$ To prove this dual representation from a proved finite-dimensional supplier, apply [F4] to the matrix with columns $a_i$, $i\in J_m$, and right-hand side $h_m$. If no nonnegative representation existed, the alternative would give a vector $y$ with $a_i^{\mathsf T}y\ge0$ for active indices and $h_m^{\mathsf T}y<0$. Replace $y$ by its orthogonal projection into $H_{m-1}$, preserving these pairings. Then $h_m+\epsilon y$ preserves all active inequalities, preserves the finitely many inactive strict inequalities for sufficiently small positive $\epsilon$, and decreases squared norm because its derivative at zero is $2h_m^{\mathsf T}y<0$. This contradicts minimality, proving the representation. Define $S_m$ as the union of the positive-coordinate supports of all such nonnegative representations, and $T_m=J_m\setminus S_m$. Because there are finitely many indices, averaging one witness representation for each index in $S_m$ gives a single representation $$h_m=\sum_{i\in S_m}\alpha_i a_i,\qquad \alpha_i>0\quad(i\in S_m).$$ In particular $S_m\ne\varnothing$. Set $$E_m=\operatorname{span}\{a_i:i\in S_m\},\qquad H_m=H_{m-1}\cap E_m^\perp.$$ Let $Q_m$ be the orthogonal projector onto $E_m$, and let $R_m=R_{m-1}-Q_m$ be the orthogonal projector onto $H_m$. These are nested orthogonal projections by construction. Define $V_m=E_1\oplus\cdots\oplus E_m$ and its projector $P_m=I-R_m$. Thus $h_m\in E_m$, each $E_m\ne\{0\}$, and distinct $E_m$ are orthogonal. If $T_m\ne\varnothing$, its next projected data $b_i=R_mv_i$ are strictly separable in $H_m$. To prove this crucial assertion, suppose not. The origin then belongs to the convex hull of the $b_i$: otherwise the nearest point $b\ne0$ of that compact convex hull satisfies $b^Tb_i\ge\|b\|^2>0$ for every $i$, a strict separator. Thus there are $c_i\ge0$, not all zero, with $\sum_{i\in T_m}c_i b_i=0$. Consequently $\sum_{i\in T_m}c_i a_i$ lies in $E_m$ and can be written $\sum_{j\in S_m}d_j a_j$. For sufficiently small $\epsilon>0$, each $\alpha_j-\epsilon d_j$ remains positive, and $$h_m=\sum_{j\in S_m}(\alpha_j-\epsilon d_j)a_j+\sum_{i\in T_m}\epsilon c_i a_i.$$ This is an admissible nonnegative dual representation with a positive coefficient at an index of $T_m$, contradicting the definition of $S_m$. This proves strict separability, including that no next projected vector vanishes. Continue until $T_M=\varnothing$. At each nonterminal stage a nonzero orthogonal subspace is removed, so $1\le M\le d$ and the procedure terminates. The sets $S_1,D_1,\dots,S_M,D_M$ partition the indices. The following elementary identities will be used repeatedly: If $i\in S_k$, then $v_i\in V_k$, $v_i^Th_j=1$ for $1\le j\le k$, and $v_i^Th_j=0$ for $j>k$. If $i\in D_k$, then $v_i^Th_j=1$ for $j<k$, $v_i^Th_k>1$, and the later $v_i^Th_j$ are arbitrary finite constants. Indeed membership in the successive remaining sets gives the equalities before removal; $R_{k-1}v_i\in E_k$ gives $v_i\in V_k$ for $i\in S_k$; and orthogonality gives all later zero inner products. Also $h_1=w^*$. This construction deliberately uses spans of the projected support vectors. It never assumes that a product of unrelated raw-data projectors is an orthogonal projector. [given, F1, F2, F3, F4, construct, algebra]

2.1 Inductively choose $c_m\in E_m$ to solve $$h_m=\eta\sum_{i\in S_m}\exp\left(-v_i^T\sum_{j=1}^m c_j\right)Q_mv_i.$$ For existence and uniqueness put $a_i=Q_mv_i=R_{m-1}v_i$ for $i\in S_m$, and $\beta_i=\eta\exp(-v_i^T\sum_{j<m}c_j)>0$. We need $$\sum_{i\in S_m}\beta_i e^{-a_i^Tc_m}a_i=h_m.$$ On $E_m$ consider $$\Phi_m(c)=\sum_{i\in S_m}\beta_i e^{-a_i^Tc}+h_m^Tc=\sum_{i\in S_m}\left(\beta_i e^{-a_i^Tc}+\alpha_i a_i^Tc\right),$$ using the strictly positive $\alpha_i$ from step 1.1. Every scalar function $s\mapsto\beta_i e^{-s}+\alpha_i s$ is bounded below and tends to $+\infty$ as $s\to\pm\infty$. The $a_i$ span $E_m$, so $\max_i|a_i^Tc|\ge\delta_m\|c\|$ for some $\delta_m>0$, by compactness of the unit sphere. Therefore $\Phi_m(c)\to\infty$ when $\|c\|\to\infty$: one scalar summand tends uniformly to infinity and all the others have finite lower bounds. Thus $\Phi_m$ attains its minimum. Its Hessian satisfies $$z^T\nabla^2\Phi_m(c)z=\sum_{i\in S_m}\beta_i e^{-a_i^Tc}(a_i^Tz)^2>0\qquad(0\ne z\in E_m),$$ so the minimizer is unique. Its zero gradient is exactly the required equation. Put $c=\sum_{m=1}^M c_m$ and $b_i=\eta e^{-v_i^Tc}>0$. For $i\in S_m$, later $c_j$ are orthogonal to $v_i$, so the equation becomes $$Q_m\sum_{i\in S_m}b_i v_i=h_m.$$ All factors of $\eta$ are thus included in $b_i$, and $c$ depends on the fixed step as expected. [given, F3, step 1.1, construct, algebra]

3.1 Put $B_m=\sum_{i\in S_m}b_i v_i$ and $A_k=\sum_{i\in S_k}b_i v_iv_i^T$. Since $v_i\in V_k$ for $i\in S_k$, $A_k$ maps into $V_k$. Its diagonal block $Q_kA_k|_{E_k}:E_k\to E_k$ is positive definite: for $z\in E_k\setminus\{0\}$, $$z^TA_kz=\sum_{i\in S_k}b_i((Q_kv_i)^Tz)^2>0.$$ For each $m>1$ define the vectors $d_{k,m}\in E_k$, $1\le k<m$, by $$\sum_{k<m}A_kd_{k,m}=P_{m-1}B_m.$$ This system has exactly one solution. To see this without neglecting its cross blocks, start with $y=P_{m-1}B_m\in V_{m-1}$. For $k=m-1,m-2,\dots,1$ solve the invertible diagonal-block equation $Q_kA_kd_{k,m}=Q_ky$, and replace $y$ by $y-A_kd_{k,m}$. Before that step $y\in V_k$ and afterwards $y\in V_{k-1}$, because $A_kd_{k,m}\in V_k$ and its $E_k$ component has been removed. The final residual is zero. The same backward argument applied to a homogeneous system proves uniqueness. This is a genuinely triangular solution; an $A_kd_{k,m}$ can have nonzero components in earlier $E_j$. Because $B_m\in V_m$ and $Q_mB_m=h_m$, these solutions give the exact cancellation identity $$B_m-\sum_{k<m}A_kd_{k,m}=h_m\qquad(1\le m\le M),$$ with the empty sum understood as zero. All $c_m$ and $d_{k,m}$ are fixed finite vectors. [given, step 1.1, step 2.1, construct, algebra]

4.1 For sufficiently large real $s$ let $L_0(s)=s$, $L_m(s)=\log L_{m-1}(s)$, and $$a_m(s)=L_m'(s)=\frac1{s\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}L_j(s)},\qquad \epsilon_{k,m}(s)=\frac{a_m(s)}{a_k(s)}=\frac1{\prod_{j=k}^{m-1}L_j(s)}\quad(k<m).$$ All iterated logs used here, including $L_M$, are positive and eventually increasing. Each $a_m$ and each $\epsilon_{k,m}$ is positive, decreasing and tends to zero. The summability test used below is elementary. A product $$s^{-\nu_0}\prod_{j=1}^qL_j(s)^{-\nu_j}$$ is integrable and integer-summable on a sufficiently large tail if its first exponent differing from $1$ is greater than $1$. Indeed, if that first exponent occurs at $L_r$ (where $r=0$ denotes $s$), the substitution $y=L_r(s)$ removes all earlier factors and reduces the integral to $y^{-1-\delta}$ times finitely many real powers of iterated logs of $y$. Those later powers are $O(y^{\delta/2})$, since $\log\log y=o(\log y)$, so the integral converges. The original function is eventually decreasing: its logarithmic derivative is dominated by the negative derivative of its earliest positive-power denominator. The integral test gives integer summability. This proves the needed form of source Lemma 16, including arbitrary signs of later exponents. In particular: $$\sum_t\frac{a_k(t)}{L_j(t)}<\infty\quad(j<k),\qquad \sum_t\frac{a_k(t)}{L_k(t)^2}<\infty.$$ For the first series the exponent at $L_j$ is $2$, while all earlier exponents are $1$; for the second the exponent at $L_k$ is $2$. Also $$\sum_t|\epsilon_{k,m}(t+1)-\epsilon_{k,m}(t)|<\infty,$$ by positive monotonicity and telescoping. Finally $$\sum_t\left|L_m(t+1)-L_m(t)-a_m(t)\right|<\infty.$$ Indeed the summand equals $a_m(t)-\int_t^{t+1}a_m(s)\,ds$, is nonnegative, and is at most $a_m(t)-a_m(t+1)$, which telescopes. These observations account explicitly for every discretization term. [given, step 3.1, algebra]

5.1 Define, for all sufficiently large integer $t$, $$G_t=\sum_{m=1}^Mh_mL_m(t)+c+D(t),\qquad D(t)=\sum_{k<m}d_{k,m}\epsilon_{k,m}(t).$$ The correction $D(t)\to0$, and step 4.1 proves $$\sum_t\left\|G_{t+1}-G_t-\sum_{m=1}^Mh_ma_m(t)\right\|<\infty.$$ We next prove, separately and in full, the analogous gradient estimate $$\sum_t\left\|-\eta\nabla L(G_t)-\sum_{m=1}^Mh_ma_m(t)\right\|<\infty.$$ First consider the exponential loss, for which $-\eta\nabla L(G_t)=\sum_i\eta e^{-v_i^TG_t}v_i$ exactly. **Discarded nonsupport indices.** If $i\in D_k$, write $p_i=v_i^Th_k>1$. The identities in step 1.1 give $$e^{-v_i^TG_t}=e^{-v_i^Tc-v_i^TD(t)}\left(\prod_{j<k}L_{j-1}(t)^{-1}\right)L_{k-1}(t)^{-p_i}\prod_{j>k}L_{j-1}(t)^{-v_i^Th_j}.$$ The first factor is bounded above because $D(t)\to0$. In the remaining product the first exponent differing from $1$ is $p_i>1$. step 4.1 therefore shows $\sum_t e^{-v_i^TG_t}<\infty$. This handles all nonsupport gradients, including arbitrary negative later margins. **Retained indices.** For $i\in S_k$, step 1.1 gives the exact factorization $$\eta e^{-v_i^TG_t}=b_i a_k(t)e^{-v_i^TD(t)}.$$ Because $v_i\in V_k$, only the $d_{j,m}$ with $j\le k$ can contribute to $v_i^TD(t)$. Split $$v_i^TD(t)=U_{i,k}(t)+W_{i,k}(t),$$ $$U_{i,k}(t)=\sum_{m>k}v_i^Td_{k,m}\epsilon_{k,m}(t),\qquad W_{i,k}(t)=\sum_{j<k}\sum_{m>j}v_i^Td_{j,m}\epsilon_{j,m}(t).$$ All sums are finite. Since every extra iterated log in a product is eventually at least $1$, $$|U_{i,k}(t)|\le\frac C{L_k(t)},\qquad |W_{i,k}(t)|\le C\sum_{j<k}\frac1{L_j(t)},\qquad |v_i^TD(t)|\le\frac{C'}{L_k(t)}.$$ The last bound follows because $L_j(t)\ge L_k(t)$ for $j<k$. It remains valid when $k=M$, where $U_{i,M}=0$. Taylor's theorem on a fixed neighborhood of zero gives $$e^{-v_i^TD(t)}=1-U_{i,k}(t)+E_{i,k}(t),\qquad |E_{i,k}(t)|\le C\left(\sum_{j<k}\frac1{L_j(t)}+\frac1{L_k(t)^2}\right).$$ Thus $\sum_t a_k(t)|E_{i,k}(t)|<\infty$, by step 4.1. This bound includes every crossed correction with $j<k$, every product of two different corrections, and every higher Taylor remainder; none is silently omitted. Consequently, after summing over the finite sets of indices, $$-\eta\nabla L_{\exp}(G_t)=\sum_{k=1}^M a_k(t)B_k-\sum_{k<m}a_k(t)\epsilon_{k,m}(t)A_kd_{k,m}+e_t,$$ where $\sum_t\|e_t\|<\infty$. Since $a_k\epsilon_{k,m}=a_m$, the exact triangular identity in step 3.1 makes the right side $$\sum_{m=1}^Ma_m(t)\left(B_m-\sum_{k<m}A_kd_{k,m}\right)+e_t=\sum_{m=1}^Mh_ma_m(t)+e_t.$$ This proves the gradient estimate for exponential loss. **Logistic tail correction.** Since $f_{\log}(s)=e^{-s}/(1+e^{-s})$, $$0\le e^{-s}-f_{\log}(s)=\frac{e^{-2s}}{1+e^{-s}}\le e^{-2s}.$$ For every index, $v_i^Th_1\ge1$, and all later $L_m(t)$ are $o(\log t)$; therefore $v_i^TG_t\ge(1-o(1))\log t$. In particular, simultaneously for the finitely many indices and all sufficiently large $t$, $v_i^TG_t\ge\frac34\log t$. Hence $e^{-2v_i^TG_t}\le t^{-3/2}$ is summable. The difference between the logistic and exponential gradient vectors at $G_t$ is thus summable, proving the same gradient estimate for logistic loss. Combining the increment and gradient estimates proves the promised absolutely summable defect: $$\delta_t:=G_{t+1}-F(G_t)=G_{t+1}-G_t+\eta\nabla L(G_t),\qquad \sum_t\|\delta_t\|<\infty.$$ Also $L(G_t)\to0$, because every comparison margin tends to $+\infty$. [given, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.1, step 4.1, algebra] ∎
