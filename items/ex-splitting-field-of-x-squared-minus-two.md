## Example

The splitting field of $x^2-2$ over $\mathbb Q$ is $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$, and its roots are $\sqrt2$ and $-\sqrt2$. Moreover, $1,\sqrt2$ is a $\mathbb Q$-basis.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The polynomial $x^2-2\in\mathbb Q[x]$ and the positive real square root $\sqrt2$.

[F1] The rationals form a field ([[thm-rat-field]]).

[F2] The nonnegative real number $2$ has a unique nonnegative square root $\sqrt2$ with $(\sqrt2)^2=2$ ([[thm-of-square-roots]]).

[F3] Eisenstein's criterion proves a primitive integer polynomial irreducible when a prime divides every nonleading coefficient, its square does not divide the constant coefficient, and it does not divide the leading coefficient ([[thm-eisenstein-irreducibility-criterion]]).

[F4] A simple extension by an algebraic element whose minimal polynomial has degree $n$ has power basis through degree $n-1$ ([[thm-simple-algebraic-extension-quotient-power-basis-and-degree]]).

[F5] A splitting field is generated by all roots of the polynomial ([[def-polynomials-that-split-and-splitting-fields]]).

[F6] The minimal polynomial of an algebraic element is the unique monic irreducible polynomial that vanishes at it ([[thm-evaluation-kernel-and-minimal-polynomial]]).

## Verification

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 By [F2], $x^2-2=(x-\sqrt2)(x+\sqrt2)$ over $\mathbb R$. Its two roots are $\pm\sqrt2$, and both lie in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$. [F1, F2, algebra]

1.2 Eisenstein's criterion [F3] with the prime $2$ makes $x^2-2$ irreducible over $\mathbb Q$. It is monic and vanishes at $\sqrt2$, so [F6] identifies it as the minimal polynomial; [F4] then gives the basis $1,\sqrt2$. [F2, F3, F4, F6]

2.1 The field generated by both roots is $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,-\sqrt2)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$, so [F5] identifies it as the splitting field. [F5, step 1.1] ∎
