## Definition

Work over the rational function field $K=\mathbb Q(q,t)$, where $q$ and $t$ are algebraically independent indeterminates. The rank-one reduced root system is $\Phi=\{\pm\alpha\}$ with $\alpha=2\rho$, weight lattice $P=\mathbb Z\rho$, coroot lattice $Q^\vee=\mathbb Z\alpha^\vee$, and pairing $\langle\rho,\alpha^\vee\rangle=1$. Put $z=X^\rho$, so the lattice algebra is $K[z,z^{-1}]$; the finite Weyl reflection is $s_1(z)=z^{-1}$.

Let $\delta$ be the affine degree with $z^\delta=q^{-2}$, and set $\alpha_0=-2\rho+\delta$. The affine reflections and the length-zero symmetry act on Laurent functions by

$$
s_0 f(z)=f(q^{-2}z^{-1}),\qquad s_1 f(z)=f(z^{-1}),\qquad \pi f(z)=f(q^{-1}z^{-1}).
$$

Thus $s_0^2=s_1^2=\pi^2=1$, $\pi s_0\pi=s_1$, and the affine denominators are $u_0=z^{-\alpha_0}=q^2z^2$ and $u_1=z^{-\alpha_1}=z^{-2}$. The affine Coxeter group generated by $s_0,s_1$ is infinite dihedral, with no finite braid relation between these reflections. The extended affine group is $W_{\mathrm{aff}}\rtimes\Omega$, where $\Omega=\{1,\pi\}$ acts by swapping $s_0,s_1$; every element has a unique form $w\pi^\epsilon$ with $w$ an alternating reduced word in $s_0,s_1$ and $\epsilon\in\{0,1\}$.

For $m\in\mathbb Z$, define the Laurent polynomials $B_{1,m},B_{0,m}$ by

$$
B_{1,0}=B_{0,0}=0,\quad B_{1,m}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}z^{m-2j}\ (m>0),\quad B_{1,-m}=-B_{1,m}\ (m>0),
$$

$$
B_{0,m}=-\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}q^{-2j-2}z^{m-2-2j}\ (m>0),\qquad B_{0,-m}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}q^{2j}z^{-m+2j}\ (m>0).
$$

They are the finite Laurent-polynomial versions of the Bernstein quotients with $s_1(z^m)=z^{-m}$ and $s_0(z^m)=q^{-2m}z^{-m}$. The symmetric Hecke normalization uses the same nonzero parameter $t$ at both affine simple roots: $(T_i-t)(T_i+t^{-1})=0$. The DAHA parameter $q$ is the lattice-translation parameter; it is distinct from the finite Hecke parameter $t$ and from the multiplicative type-A normalization $Q$.

## Remarks

This item selects type $A_1$ for the original design's “selected root system” scope. No specialization of $q$ or $t$ is part of this definition.
