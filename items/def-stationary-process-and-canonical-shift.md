## Definition

Let $S\in\{\mathbb R,\mathbb C\}$ with Borel sigma-algebra $\mathcal E$, let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$ be a
probability space, and let $Y=(Y_n)_{n\ge0}$ be a stochastic process with each
$Y_n:\Omega\to S$ a random element
([[def-stochastic-process-and-finite-dimensional-distributions]]). The process
$Y$ is **strictly stationary** when every finite-dimensional law is unchanged
by a common nonnegative time shift: for every $r\ge1$, every
$0\le n_1<\cdots<n_r$ and every $m\ge0$,

$$\mathcal L(Y_{m+n_1},\ldots,Y_{m+n_r})=\mathcal L(Y_{n_1},\ldots,Y_{n_r}).$$

The **canonical path law** of $Y$ is the pushforward
$\mathbb P_Y=\mathcal L(Y)$ of $\mathbb P$ under the coordinate map
$\omega\mapsto(Y_n(\omega))_{n\ge0}$, a probability measure on the product
space $S^{\mathbb N_0}$ with its product sigma-algebra. The **left shift** is
$\theta(z)_n=z_{n+1}$ for $z=(z_n)_{n\ge0}\in S^{\mathbb N_0}$.

A strictly stationary process is called **ergodic** when the canonical shift $\theta$ is ergodic
for $\mathbb P_Y$ in the sense of
[[def-ergodic-measure-preserving-system]]. The shift-invariance needed for that
definition, namely that $\theta$ is measure preserving for $\mathbb P_Y$, is
proved below for strictly stationary $Y$, so in that case
$(S^{\mathbb N_0},\mathcal E^{\otimes\mathbb N_0},\mathbb P_Y,\theta)$ is a
probability measure-preserving system in the sense of
[[def-measure-preserving-transformation-and-system]].

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A process $Y=(Y_n)_{n\ge0}$ with values in $S\in\{\mathbb R,\mathbb C\}$ on a probability space, its coordinate map, and the left shift $\theta$.

[F1] The finite-dimensional laws are the pushforward laws of the tuples of coordinates, including for the single time $n_1$. ([[def-stochastic-process-and-finite-dimensional-distributions]])

[F2] A measurable self-map $T$ of a measure space is measure preserving when $\mu(T^{-1}E)=\mu(E)$ for every measurable $E$, and the quadruple is then a measure-preserving system. ([[def-measure-preserving-transformation-and-system]])

[F3] A lambda-system containing a generating pi-system contains the sigma-algebra generated by it. ([[thm-dynkin-pi-lambda]])

## Verification

**Proof technique:** verify cylinder invariance and extend by the pi-lambda theorem.

1.1 The coordinate map is measurable, since each of its coordinates is a random element, so $\mathbb P_Y$ is a well-defined probability measure on the product sigma-algebra [F1]. A cylinder $C=\{z:(z_{n_1},\ldots,z_{n_r})\in B\}$, with $0\le n_1<\cdots<n_r$ and measurable $B\subseteq S^r$, is measurable; its preimage $\theta^{-1}C=\{z:(z_{n_1+1},\ldots,z_{n_r+1})\in B\}$ is again a cylinder, and cylinders generate the product sigma-algebra, so $\theta$ is measurable. [F1, F2]

2.1 Suppose $Y$ is strictly stationary and let $C=\{z:(z_{n_1},\ldots,z_{n_r})\in B\}$ be a cylinder as in step 1.1. By the definition of $\mathbb P_Y$ as the pushforward of the coordinate map, $$\mathbb P_Y(\theta^{-1}C)=\mathbb P\bigl((Y_{n_1+1},\ldots,Y_{n_r+1})\in B\bigr).$$ Strict stationarity applied to the time list $(n_1,\ldots,n_r)$ with shift $m=1$ says that the joint law of $(Y_{n_1+1},\ldots,Y_{n_r+1})$ equals the joint law of $(Y_{n_1},\ldots,Y_{n_r})$, and the latter gives $\mathbb P_Y(C)$. Hence $\mathbb P_Y(\theta^{-1}C)=\mathbb P_Y(C)$ for every cylinder. [F1, F2, step 1.1, given]

3.1 Let $\mathcal D=\{A:\theta^{-1}A\text{ is measurable and }\mathbb P_Y(\theta^{-1}A)=\mathbb P_Y(A)\}$. Preimages commute with complements and countable unions, so $\mathcal D$ is a lambda-system: it contains $S^{\mathbb N_0}$, is closed under complements because $\mathbb P_Y$ has total mass one, and is closed under countable disjoint unions by countable additivity. Cylinders form a pi-system generating the product sigma-algebra and lie in $\mathcal D$ by step 2.1, so [F3] gives every product-measurable set. Thus, when $Y$ is strictly stationary, $\theta$ is measurable and measure preserving, and [F2] makes the quadruple a probability measure-preserving system. Constant deterministic processes are included; a nonconstant deterministic process need not be stationary, and the proof of shift invariance uses only the stated finite-dimensional invariance. [F2, F3, step 2.1] ∎
