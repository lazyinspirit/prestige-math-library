## Statement

Assume the Axiom of Choice for the conditional-expectation interface. Let
$(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)$ be a probability space, let
$\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal F$ be a sub-sigma-algebra, let $X$ be an
$(S,\Sigma)$-valued random element that is $\mathcal G$-measurable, and let $Y$
be a $(T,\Tau)$-valued random element whose sigma-algebra $\sigma(Y)$ is
independent of $\mathcal G$. Let $\mu$ be the law of $Y$ on $(T,\Tau)$. If
$h:S\times T\to\mathbb R$ is bounded and $\Sigma\otimes\Tau$-measurable, then
$$H(x):=\int_T h(x,y)\,\mu(dy)$$
is $\Sigma$-measurable and
$$E\bigl[h(X,Y)\bigr|\mathcal G\bigr]=H(X)\qquad\text{almost surely}.$$

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** AC, a probability space, a sub-sigma-algebra $\mathcal G$, a $\mathcal G$-measurable random element $X$, a random element $Y$ with $\sigma(Y)$ independent of $\mathcal G$, and a bounded product-measurable $h$.

[F1] Measurability of $x\mapsto\int_Th(x,y)K(x,dy)$ for a finite kernel $K$, in particular a probability kernel, is theorem-level; the constant map $K(s,A):=\mu(A)$ is a probability kernel because $s\mapsto\mu(A)$ is constant. [[def-measure-kernel-and-probability-kernel]] [[thm-measurability-of-integration-against-a-kernel]]

[F2] A conditional-expectation version is characterized by its $\mathcal G$-event integrals, and versions are unique almost surely. [[def-conditional-expectation-given-a-sigma-algebra]] [[def-conditional-expectation-as-an-ae-class]] [[lem-conditional-expectation-is-unique-almost-surely]]

[F3] Bounded $\mathcal G$-measurable factors come out of the conditional expectation, and conditional expectation is linear on integrable inputs. [[thm-taking-out-what-is-known]] [[thm-basic-algebra-and-order-properties-of-conditional-expectation]]

[F4] If a random variable has the independence rectangle identity against $\mathcal G$, its conditional expectation given $\mathcal G$ is its mean; this applies to $1_C(Y)$ for $C\in\Tau$ because $\sigma(Y)$ is independent of $\mathcal G$. [[lem-conditioning-a-known-variable-and-an-independent-variable]] [[def-independent-sigma-algebras-and-events]] [[def-independent-random-elements]]

[F5] The product sigma-algebra is generated by the measurable rectangles, which form a pi-system containing the whole space; a lambda-system containing a pi-system contains the generated sigma-algebra. [[thm-dynkin-pi-lambda]] [[def-product-sigma-algebra-and-finite-product-sigma-algebras]]

[F6] Sections of product-measurable sets are measurable, and compositions of a measurable map with a measurable function are measurable. [[thm-sections-of-product-measurable-functions-are-measurable]] [[prop-closure-properties-of-measurable-functions-used-by-the-integral]] [[def-law-or-distribution-of-a-random-element]]

[F7] Nonnegative measurable functions are increasing limits of nonnegative simple functions, and monotone convergence passes those limits through integrals. [[thm-increasing-simple-approximation-of-a-nonnegative-measurable-function]] [[thm-monotone-convergence-for-the-integral]]

[F8] AC supplies the conditional-expectation existence used in [F2]. [[def-axiom-of-choice]]

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 Fix a measurable rectangle $A\times C$ with $A\in\Sigma$ and $C\in\Tau$. The indicator $1_{A\times C}(X,Y)=1_A(X)1_C(Y)$ has bounded $\mathcal G$-measurable factor $1_A(X)$, so [F3] and then [F4] give $E[1_{A\times C}(X,Y)|\mathcal G]=1_A(X)E[1_C(Y)|\mathcal G]=1_A(X)P(Y\in C)$ almost surely; since $H_{A\times C}(x)=\int_T1_{A\times C}(x,y)\mu(dy)=\mu(C)1_A(x)$, this is the asserted identity for rectangles. [F3, F4, given]

2.1 Let $\mathcal D$ be the class of $B\in\Sigma\otimes\Tau$ with $E[1_B(X,Y)|\mathcal G]=H_B(X)$ almost surely, where $H_B(x):=\mu(B_x)$ and $B_x=\{y\in T:(x,y)\in B\}$. Each $B_x$ is measurable by [F6], the constant kernel $K(s,\cdot):=\mu$ is a probability kernel and [F1] makes $H_B$ $\Sigma$-measurable, while $H_B(X)$ is then $\mathcal G$-measurable and bounded by [F6]. Step 1.1 shows that $\mathcal D$ contains every measurable rectangle. [F1, F6, step 1.1]

3.1 The class $\mathcal D$ is a lambda-system. It contains $\Omega$ because $H_\Omega=\mu(T)=1$. If $B\in\mathcal D$, then for every $\mathcal G$-event $G_0$ we have $\int_{G_0}1_{B^c}(X,Y)\,dP=P(G_0)-\int_{G_0}1_B(X,Y)\,dP=P(G_0)-\int_{G_0}H_B(X)\,dP=\int_{G_0}(1-H_B(X))\,dP$, and $H_{B^c}=1-H_B$; so $B^c\in\mathcal D$ by [F2]. If $B_1\subseteq B_2\subseteq\cdots$ are in $\mathcal D$ with union $B$, then for each $x$ the numbers $H_{B_n}(x)=\mu((B_n)_x)$ increase to $\mu(B_x)=H_B(x)$, and [F7] applied to the finite measure $P$ restricted to $G_0$ gives $\int_{G_0}1_{B_n}(X,Y)\,dP=\int_{G_0}H_{B_n}(X)\,dP\to\int_{G_0}H_B(X)\,dP$ for every $\mathcal G$-event $G_0$; hence $B\in\mathcal D$ by [F2]. [F2, F7, step 2.1]

4.1 The measurable rectangles form a pi-system containing $\Omega$ and generate $\Sigma\otimes\Tau$, so [F5] applied to the lambda-system $\mathcal D$ gives $\mathcal D=\Sigma\otimes\Tau$; that is, $E[1_B(X,Y)|\mathcal G]=H_B(X)$ almost surely for every product-measurable $B$. [F5, step 2.1, step 3.1]

5.1 Let $h\ge0$ be bounded. By [F7] there are nonnegative simple functions $s_n=\sum_jc_{j,n}1_{B_{j,n}}$ with $s_n\uparrow h$ pointwise, the sets $B_{j,n}$ being product measurable and the sums finite; step 4.1 and linearity of the integral give, for every $\mathcal G$-event $G_0$, $\int_{G_0}s_n(X,Y)\,dP=\sum_jc_{j,n}\int_{G_0}1_{B_{j,n}}(X,Y)\,dP=\sum_jc_{j,n}\int_{G_0}H_{B_{j,n}}(X)\,dP=\int_{G_0}H_{s_n}(X)\,dP$, where $H_{s_n}(x)=\int_Ts_n(x,y)\mu(dy)$. [F7, step 4.1]

6.1 For bounded nonnegative $h$, monotone convergence [F7] applied to $s_n(X,Y)\uparrow h(X,Y)$ and to $H_{s_n}(X)\uparrow H_h(X)$ in step 5.1 gives $\int_{G_0}h(X,Y)\,dP=\int_{G_0}H_h(X)\,dP$ for every $\mathcal G$-event $G_0$. Since $H_h$ is $\Sigma$-measurable by [F1] and bounded by $\|h\|_\infty$, [F2] identifies $H_h(X)$ with $E[h(X,Y)|\mathcal G]$ almost surely. [F1, F2, F7, step 5.1]

7.1 For a general bounded real $h$, apply step 6.1 to the bounded nonnegative functions $h^+$ and $h^-$ and subtract the two almost-sure identities using linearity [F3]; since $H_h=H_{h^+}-H_{h^-}$ pointwise, $H_h$ is $\Sigma$-measurable and $E[h(X,Y)|\mathcal G]=H_h(X)$ almost surely. This is the asserted identity, with $H=H_h$ as displayed in the statement. [F1, F3, step 6.1]

8.1 The boundary cases behave as stated and need no separate treatment: $h\equiv0$ gives $H\equiv0$ and both sides vanish; if $T$ is a single point with its unique probability measure, then $H(x)=h(x,y_0)$ and the statement reduces to $E[h(X,y_0)|\mathcal G]=h(X,y_0)$ for the $\mathcal G$-measurable $X$, which is [F3] with a deterministic factor; if $A=\emptyset$ or $C=\emptyset$ the rectangle identity of step 1.1 reads $0=0$. The independence hypothesis is used exactly once, in step 1.1, and no other selection is made; AC is used only through [F8] in [F2]. [F3, F8, given, step 1.1] ∎

## Source notes

Durrett's proof of Theorem 7.2.1 conditions on the known value of $B_s$ and on the independent increment; van der Vaart, Section 1.4, records the rectangle-to-product-sigma-algebra Dynkin extension in this generality. The proof above isolates that extension as a lemma because the Brownian Markov, future-path and planar arguments all consume it with different state spaces $S$ and $T$.
