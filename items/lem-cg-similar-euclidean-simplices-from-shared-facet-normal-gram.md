## Statement

Let $I$ be a finite set with $|I|=n+1$, and let $E,E'$ be Euclidean affine spaces of dimension $n$ ([[def-dimension]]) with direction spaces $V,V'$ ([[def-affine-subspace-of-a-vector-space]]). Choose origins in $E,E'$ so points are written in $V,V'$. Let $\sigma\subseteq E$ and $\sigma'\subseteq E'$ be nonempty bounded $n$-simplices ([[def-geometric-simplex-spanned-by-affinely-independent-vertices]]) presented as
$$\sigma=\{\varphi\in V:\langle u_i,\varphi\rangle\ge c_i\ \text{for all }i\in I\},\qquad \sigma'=\{\varphi'\in V':\langle u_i',\varphi'\rangle\ge c_i'\ \text{for all }i\in I\},$$
where each half-space defines one of the distinct facets, $u_i\in V$ and $u_i'\in V'$ are unit inward normals, and $c_i,c_i'\in\mathbb R$. Suppose $\langle u_i,u_j\rangle=\langle u_i',u_j'\rangle$ for all $i,j\in I$. Then there are a linear isometry $T:V\to V'$, a point $y_0\in E'$ and a number $t>0$ such that the similarity $f(\varphi)=y_0+t\,T(\varphi)$ satisfies $f(\sigma)=\sigma'$ and carries the facet of $\sigma$ with normal $u_i$ onto the facet of $\sigma'$ with normal $u_i'$ for every $i\in I$. In particular the groups generated by the reflections of $E$ in the facets of $\sigma$ and of $E'$ in the facets of $\sigma'$ are conjugate by the similarity $f$, hence isomorphic as reflection groups acting on Euclidean spaces.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A finite set $I$ with $|I|=n+1$; Euclidean affine spaces $E,E'$ of dimension $n$ with direction spaces $V,V'$, each identified with its direction space by a fixed origin, so that the points of $E$ and $E'$ are written as vectors of $V$ and $V'$; nonempty bounded $n$-simplices $\sigma\subseteq E$, $\sigma'\subseteq E'$ presented by the half-spaces of the statement, with unit normals $u_i\in V$, $u_i'\in V'$, offsets $c_i,c_i'$ and facet hyperplanes $H_i=\{\varphi:\langle u_i,\varphi\rangle=c_i\}$, $H_i'=\{\varphi':\langle u_i',\varphi'\rangle=c_i'\}$; and the common Gram matrix, $\langle u_i,u_j\rangle=\langle u_i',u_j'\rangle$ for all $i,j\in I$.

[F1] A real inner product space satisfies $\langle v,v\rangle\ge0$ for every $v$, with $\langle v,v\rangle=0$ only for $v=0$, and the induced norm is $\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}$ ([[def-real-and-complex-inner-product-space]]).

[F2] A geometric simplex is the convex hull of finitely many affinely independent points, its vertices, and its points are exactly the convex combinations of those vertices ([[def-geometric-simplex-spanned-by-affinely-independent-vertices]]).

[F3] A subset $U$ of a Euclidean space is convex when it contains the segment between any two of its points ([[def-convex-subset-of-euclidean-space]]).

[F4] A face of a convex set $K$ is a nonempty convex subset $F\subseteq K$ such that $(1-t)y+tz\in F$ with $y,z\in K$ and $0<t<1$ implies $y,z\in F$; so a face is closed under the operation of splitting off vertices of convex combinations ([[def-extreme-point-and-face]]).

[F5] The orthogonal complement of a subspace $W$ of an inner product space is $W^\perp=\{v:\langle v,w\rangle=0\text{ for every }w\in W\}$, a linear subspace ([[def-orthogonal-complement]]).

[F6] For every subspace $W$ of a finite-dimensional real inner product space $V$ one has $V=W\oplus W^\perp$: every $v\in V$ is uniquely $w+z$ with $w\in W$ and $z\in W^\perp$ ([[thm-finite-dimensional-orthogonal-decomposition]]).

[F7] A linear map $T:V\to V'$ of inner product spaces is a linear isometry when $\|Tv\|=\|v\|$ for all $v$; if $T$ preserves inner products then it is a linear isometry by [F1] ([[def-linear-isometry-and-orthogonal-or-unitary-operator]]).

[F8] Let $A:V\to W$ be linear with $V$ finite-dimensional. There are a basis $K$ of $\ker A$ and a basis $B$ of $V$ with $K\subseteq B$, and for $C=B\setminus K$ the restriction $A|_C$ is a bijection onto a basis $A[C]$ of $\operatorname{im}A$; hence $\dim V=\dim\ker A+\dim\operatorname{im}A$ ([[lem-kernel-basis-extension-gives-image-basis]]).

[F9] If $U$ is a linear subspace of a finite-dimensional space $V$ with $\dim U=\dim V$, then $U=V$ ([[thm-dimension-of-a-linear-subspace]]).

[F10] A function $T:V\to W$ is linear when $T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v)$ for all scalars $a,b$ and vectors $u,v$ ([[def-linear-map]]).

[F11] A linear map between inner-product spaces is a linear isometry when it preserves the induced norm ([[def-linear-isometry-and-orthogonal-or-unitary-operator]]).

[F12] A map between metric spaces is an isometry when it preserves distances and is bijective ([[def-isometry-and-metric-embedding]]).

## Proof
**Proof technique:** direct; the vertex equations of the two simplices force all coefficients of the unique relation among the normals to have one sign, and the relation then reconstructs the translation.

1.1 (Complements of one normal span.) Fix $k\in I$ and put $W_k:=\operatorname{span}\{u_j:j\ne k\}$. If $v\in W_k^\perp$, then $\langle u_j,v\rangle=0$ for all $j\ne k$. For $\varphi\in\sigma$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb R$, the point $\varphi+\lambda v$ satisfies those other inequalities for every $\lambda$; the $k$-th slack $\langle u_k,\varphi+\lambda v\rangle-c_k$ is affine in $\lambda$ and is nonnegative at $0$, so if $\langle u_k,v\rangle\ne0$ its admissible values contain an unbounded half-line, while if $\langle u_k,v\rangle=0$ they are all of $\mathbb R$. This contradicts boundedness of $\sigma$, so $W_k^\perp=\{0\}$ [F5]. By [F6], every $x\in V$ is $w+z$ with $w\in W_k$ and $z\in W_k^\perp=\{0\}$; hence $W_k=V$. Thus every family obtained by omitting one $u_k$ spans $V$, and in particular all the $u_i$ span $V$. The same argument applied to $\sigma'$ shows that the $u_i'$ span $V'$. [F5, F6, given, algebra]

1.2 (Vertices opposite facets.) Write $\sigma=\operatorname{conv}\{v_0,\dots,v_n\}$ with affinely independent vertices [F2]. Every face $F$ of $\sigma$ is the convex hull of the vertices it contains: if $z=\sum_k\lambda_kv_k\in F$ is a convex combination, a single positive coefficient gives $z=v_k\in F$; otherwise split off any term with $0<\lambda_k<1$ as $z=\lambda_kv_k+(1-\lambda_k)y$. The face property [F4] puts $v_k,y$ in $F$, and induction on the number of positive coefficients puts every vertex used in $z$ in $F$. Thus $z\in\operatorname{conv}(F\cap\{v_0,\dots,v_n\})$; the reverse inclusion follows from convexity [F3]. For the facet $F_i=\sigma\cap H_i$, let $S_i:=F_i\cap\{v_0,\dots,v_n\}$. Then $F_i=\operatorname{conv}(S_i)$ and $S_i$ is nonempty; its points are affinely independent, so its affine span has dimension $|S_i|-1$ (subtracting one point gives $|S_i|-1$ linearly independent vectors spanning the direction space). Since this affine span is $H_i$, of dimension $n-1$, $|S_i|=n$. Hence there is a unique vertex $w_i$ outside $F_i$, and $\langle u_i,w_i\rangle>c_i$. If $j\ne i$ and $w_i\notin S_j$, then $S_j\subseteq S_i$ and both have size $n$, so $S_j=S_i$ and $F_j=F_i$, contradicting that the indexed facets are distinct. Thus $w_i\in S_j$, so $\langle u_j,w_i\rangle=c_j$ for every $j\ne i$. Applying the same argument to $\sigma'$ gives opposite vertices $w_i'$ with $\langle u_j',w_i'\rangle=c_j'$ for $j\ne i$ and $\langle u_i',w_i'\rangle>c_i'$. [F2, F3, F4, given, algebra]

2.1 (A nonzero relation valid for both normal families.) Fix $k\in I$. By step 1.1, $u_k=\sum_{j\ne k}a_ju_j$ for some real $a_j$; put $p_k:=1$ and $p_j:=-a_j$ for $j\ne k$. Then $p\in\mathbb R^I$ is nonzero and $\sum_ip_iu_i=0$, so $p$ is a relation among the normals. The same vector is a relation among the primed normals: $\bigl\|\sum_ip_iu_i'\bigr\|^2=\sum_{i,j}p_ip_j\langle u_i',u_j'\rangle=\sum_{i,j}p_ip_j\langle u_i,u_j\rangle=\bigl\|\sum_ip_iu_i\bigr\|^2=0$, hence $\sum_ip_iu_i'=0$ by [F1]. [F1, step 1.1, algebra]

2.2 (The linear isometry.) Define $T:V\to V'$ on a combination of the $u_i$ by $T\bigl(\sum_ia_iu_i\bigr):=\sum_ia_iu_i'$. This is well defined: if $\sum_ia_iu_i=0$ then $\bigl\|\sum_ia_iu_i'\bigr\|^2=\sum_{i,j}a_ia_j\langle u_i',u_j'\rangle=\sum_{i,j}a_ia_j\langle u_i,u_j\rangle=\bigl\|\sum_ia_iu_i\bigr\|^2=0$, so $\sum_ia_iu_i'=0$ by [F1]. By construction $T$ is linear in the sense of [F10], satisfies $T(u_i)=u_i'$, and preserves inner products: $\bigl\langle T\bigl(\sum_ia_iu_i\bigr),T\bigl(\sum_jb_ju_j\bigr)\bigr\rangle=\sum_{i,j}a_ib_j\langle u_i',u_j'\rangle=\sum_{i,j}a_ib_j\langle u_i,u_j\rangle=\bigl\langle\sum_ia_iu_i,\sum_jb_ju_j\bigr\rangle$; so $T$ is a linear isometry [F1, F7, F10]. Since the $u_i'$ span $V'$ [step 1.1], the image of $T$ is $V'$, and $T$ is bijective. [F1, F7, F10, step 1.1, algebra]

3.1 (Sign consistency and the two offset sums.) Put $R:=\sum_ip_ic_i$ and $R':=\sum_ip_ic_i'$. For each $i$, using $\langle u_j,w_i\rangle=c_j$ for $j\ne i$ from step 1.2 and $\sum_kp_ku_k=0$ from step 2.1, $0=\bigl\langle\sum_kp_ku_k,w_i\bigr\rangle=p_i\langle u_i,w_i\rangle+\sum_{k\ne i}p_kc_k=p_i\bigl(\langle u_i,w_i\rangle-c_i\bigr)+R$. Since $\langle u_i,w_i\rangle-c_i>0$ [step 1.2], the number $R$ is nonzero and $p_i$ has the sign of $-R$, for every $i$: all coefficients $p_i$ are nonzero and of one sign. Applying the same computation to the primed simplex, using the opposite vertices from step 1.2 and the primed relation from step 2.1, gives $p_i(\langle u_i',w_i'\rangle-c_i')=-R'$ for every $i$, so $R'\ne0$ and $p_i$ has the sign of $-R'$. Thus $R'$ and $R$ have the same sign, and $t:=R'/R>0$. [step 2.1, step 1.2, algebra]

4.1 (The translation solving the offset equations.) Put $\Delta:=(c_i'-tc_i)_{i\in I}\in\mathbb R^I$. By step 3.1, $\sum_ip_i\Delta_i=R'-tR=0$, i.e. $\Delta\perp p$ [F5]. Let $A:V'\to\mathbb R^I$ be the linear map $A(y):=(\langle u_i',y\rangle)_{i\in I}$. Its kernel is trivial: if $A(y)=0$ then $\langle u_i',y\rangle=0$ for all $i$, so $y\perp\operatorname{span}\{u_i'\}=V'$ by step 1.1 and $y=0$ by [F1]. By [F8] (applied to the coordinate basis of $\mathbb R^I$, of $|I|=n+1$ elements) $\dim\operatorname{im}A=\dim V'=n$; the kernel of the nonzero functional $\mathbb R^I\to\mathbb R$, $x\mapsto\sum_ip_ix_i$, is $p^\perp$. It is nonzero because $p\ne0$ [step 2.1], so its image is the one-dimensional space $\mathbb R$; [F8] therefore gives $\dim p^\perp=(n+1)-1=n$. Moreover $\operatorname{im}A\subseteq p^\perp$, because for $x=A(y)$ one has $\sum_ip_ix_i=\langle\sum_ip_iu_i',y\rangle=0$ by step 2.1. As $\operatorname{im}A$ and $p^\perp$ are subspaces of $p^\perp$ of the same dimension $n$, [F9] gives $\operatorname{im}A=p^\perp$; since $\Delta\perp p$, there is $y_0\in V'$ with $\langle u_i',y_0\rangle=c_i'-tc_i$ for every $i\in I$. [F5, F8, F9, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.1, algebra]

5.1 (The similarity and the facet correspondence.) For $\varphi\in V$ and $i\in I$, using $T(u_i)=u_i'$ and inner-product preservation of step 2.2 and the point $y_0$ of step 4.1, $\langle u_i',y_0+tT(\varphi)\rangle=(c_i'-tc_i)+t\langle u_i,\varphi\rangle$. Hence $y_0+tT(\varphi)\in\sigma'$ if and only if $\langle u_i,\varphi\rangle\ge c_i$ for all $i$, that is, if and only if $\varphi\in\sigma$; so $f(\varphi):=y_0+tT(\varphi)$ maps $\sigma$ onto $\sigma'$, and $\langle u_i',f(\varphi)\rangle=c_i'$ if and only if $\langle u_i,\varphi\rangle=c_i$, so $f$ carries the facet $F_i$ of $\sigma$ onto the facet $F_i'$ of $\sigma'$. Since $t>0$ and $T$ is a linear isometry, $f$ is a similarity. [step 2.2, step 4.1, given, algebra]

6.1 (Conjugating the facet reflections.) For $i\in I$ define $s_i:V\to V$ by $s_i(\varphi):=\varphi+2(c_i-\langle u_i,\varphi\rangle)u_i$. Its linear part $r_i(\varphi):=\varphi-2\langle u_i,\varphi\rangle u_i$ preserves the norm: using $\|u_i\|=1$ and bilinearity, $\|r_i(\varphi)\|^2=\|\varphi\|^2-4\langle u_i,\varphi\rangle^2+4\langle u_i,\varphi\rangle^2\|u_i\|^2=\|\varphi\|^2$. It fixes $u_i^\perp$ and sends $u_i$ to $-u_i$, so it is the reflection across $u_i^\perp$ and is a linear isometry [F11]. Since $s_i=r_i+2c_i u_i$, it preserves distances; direct substitution gives $s_i^2=\mathrm{id}$, and $s_i$ fixes $H_i$ pointwise. Thus $s_i$ is the affine reflection in $H_i$ and is an isometry [F12]. Let $s_i'$ be the corresponding reflection of $E'$ defined by the primed data. Then for every $\psi\in V'$, writing $f^{-1}(\psi)=T^{-1}((\psi-y_0)/t)$ and using $\langle u_i,f^{-1}(\psi)\rangle=\bigl(\langle u_i',\psi\rangle-c_i'+tc_i\bigr)/t$, $f(s_i(f^{-1}(\psi)))=\psi+2t(c_i-\langle u_i,f^{-1}(\psi)\rangle)u_i'=\psi+2(c_i'-\langle u_i',\psi\rangle)u_i'=s_i'(\psi)$. Thus $f\circ s_i\circ f^{-1}=s_i'$ for every $i$, and the map $C_f:g\mapsto f\circ g\circ f^{-1}$ carries the group generated by the $s_i$ onto the group generated by the $s_i'$. It preserves composition since $C_f(g\circ h)=C_f(g)\circ C_f(h)$, and its inverse is $h\mapsto f^{-1}\circ h\circ f$; hence it is an isomorphism of the two reflection groups. [F11, F12, step 2.2, step 4.1, step 5.1, algebra] ∎
