## Statement

Assume countable choice. A bridge diagram is a smooth regular oriented planar link diagram with nondegenerate height-critical points, distinct heights for all critical points and crossings, and no height-critical crossing preimages. A bridge isotopy is a smooth family of bridge diagrams. A bridge-like rectangular diagram is nonempty and has vertical-over-horizontal crossings, distinct horizontal levels and distinct vertical abscissae; each horizontal segment is either a local maximum or minimum without crossings, or is height-monotone after smoothing and contains exactly one crossing. Smoothing means tilting each crossing-containing horizontal segment slightly in the direction of height travel and rounding corners in disjoint small neighborhoods, retaining all crossings and their decorations.

Every nonempty bridge diagram admits a bridge-like rectangular diagram whose smoothing is bridge isotopic to it. Any two bridge-like rectangular diagrams with bridge-isotopic smoothings give grids related by finitely many legal column commutations, after replacing their ordered levels and abscissae by consecutive grid coordinates. In particular this construction is independent, up to grid commutations, of every coordinate choice. The orientations determine the alternating $O,X$ markings by the row $O$-to-$X$ and column $X$-to-$O$ convention of the grid-diagram definition.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Countable choice and the finite oriented bridge diagrams or bridge isotopy in the statement. A legal column commutation exchanges adjacent columns whose closed vertical intervals are disjoint or one is contained in the interior of the other.

## Proof

1.1 A nonempty compact closed component has a height maximum and minimum, so there are at least two special points and the grid number below is at least two. Cut each underpassing branch at each crossing, but do not cut the overpassing branch there, and cut the diagram at all height-critical points. The resulting finitely many open pieces are strictly height-monotone. Associate to each piece a closed interval of heights, whose endpoints are its two incident special heights. The interval has positive length: special heights are distinct, and a strictly monotone piece cannot return to its initial height. The desired rectangular replacement of a piece is a single vertical segment with precisely this interval. At an extremum, join its two incident segments by a horizontal cap. At a crossing, join the two underpassing incident segments by one horizontal segment crossing the continuing overpassing segment. No other horizontal segment is introduced. Thus the number of vertical pieces equals the number of horizontal joins, since every piece has two ends and every join has two incident ends. [given, construct]

2.1 Specify inequalities for the abscissae $x_i$ of the vertical pieces. Whenever two closed height intervals overlap in an interval with nonempty interior, prescribe their left-to-right order on a regular horizontal line in that overlap. This order is constant throughout the overlap: otherwise the two pieces would intersect, and an intersection is either an underpassing cut already made or a forbidden extra crossing. If intervals only meet at a special height, prescribe their order in the local cap or crossing picture. At a cap the two endpoints have the order of its two nearby branches. At a crossing, the two underpassing endpoints lie on opposite sides of the continuing overpassing piece, in the order given by the original crossing; all other pieces have their continued order outside that small crossing neighborhood. This gives a coherent total order on every collection of intervals containing a common height. In particular the two cap endpoints are consecutive among the pieces meeting its height, and at a crossing the only piece strictly between its underpassing endpoints is its overpassing piece. To see these last assertions, take a sufficiently small disk around the special point, disjoint from every other branch, and then a sufficiently close regular height: the incident branches are consecutive there except for the specified crossing branch. [step 1.1, given, construct]

3.1 The directed inequalities in step 2.1 have no directed cycle. Here is the finite interval argument, including endpoint intersections. Three pairwise intersecting closed real intervals have a common point: the largest left endpoint does not exceed the smallest right endpoint. Thus the orders on any triangle are consistent by step 2.1. The intersection graph of finitely many intervals has no induced cycle of length at least four. Indeed choose in such a cycle an interval with largest left endpoint. Its two cycle neighbors both contain that endpoint, so they intersect each other and give a chord. If a shortest directed cycle had length at least four, this chord, in whichever direction its inequality points, would combine with one of the two directed paths around the cycle to give a shorter directed cycle. Length two is impossible, and length three was excluded. Hence the inequalities admit a total ordering: repeatedly remove a vertex with no incoming edge, which exists since following incoming edges indefinitely in a finite graph would give a cycle. Assign distinct integers in this order. They satisfy every prescribed strict inequality. [step 2.1, construct, cases, algebra]

4.1 Use these abscissae for the vertical segments and use the given special heights for the horizontal joins. Step 2.1 makes every cap disjoint from the other vertical segments and every crossing join meet exactly its designated overpassing segment. It also makes distinct joins disjoint, since their heights differ. The result is therefore an embedded decorated rectangular diagram except at the designated transverse vertical-over-horizontal crossings. Each vertical segment has exactly two incident horizontal ends, and no horizontal end lies in the interior of another segment. The incidence and orientations agree with the original link. A cap has no crossing and its two incident pieces lie on the same side in height; a crossing join has exactly one crossing and one incident piece above and one below. Consequently this diagram is bridge-like. Replace the ordered horizontal and vertical coordinates separately by consecutive grid coordinates, using increasing coordinate functions. Label the corners by their orientation: a corner where the oriented row starts is $O$ and where it ends is $X$. Every column then starts at $X$ and ends at $O$, so these labels give precisely [F1]. [F1, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.1, construct]

5.1 We verify that the smoothing has the asserted bridge class, rather than just the same undecorated incidence graph. Choose disjoint thin horizontal bands around the special heights. In the complement the diagram consists of ordered graphs $x=f_i(y)$ with nonzero height derivative; their order agrees with the ordered vertical segments. At a cap, the one-dimensional Morse coordinate is explicit: after translating its height, write $Y(q)=q^2a(q)$ with $a(0)>0$ (or its negative), and replace $q$ by $q\sqrt{a(q)}$. The two local branches are then indexed by $q=\pm\sqrt{|y|}$. At a crossing both branches are graphs in $y$ and their abscissa difference has a simple zero, because the tangents are transverse and neither is horizontal. Smooth the rectangular model in the bands so that it has the same critical heights, graph orders and crossing slopes, with the chosen underpassing branch passing through the crossing monotonically. Such smoothing is made by rounding each corner in a small empty disk and replacing each crossing join by a slightly tilted arc; its slope and height can be chosen so that the crossing is at the specified band center. For a cap, first prescribe its very small central neighborhood as a positive horizontal affine image of the original Morse cap, and join it to the two vertical legs by smooth monotone branches in the remaining empty cap neighborhood. This permits arbitrarily small height displacement of the horizontal cap, and ensures that the local matching below is affine with constant coefficients near its critical point. [step 2.1, step 4.1, given, construct]

6.1 For clarity, the ordered graph matching in step 5.1 gives an actual bridge isotopy. Near a crossing use the affine map of the horizontal coordinate matching the two graph values. Its slope is the quotient of their abscissa differences, hence extends smoothly and positively across their common simple zero. Near a cap use the constant-coefficient positive affine map prescribed in step 5.1. It is already defined on both sides of the critical height. Away from that central neighborhood the two branches are separated regular graphs, so no extension across a square-root singularity is needed. These local horizontal affine maps may be restricted to small disjoint neighborhoods. On the remaining compact regular bands all matching graph values have a positive separation. Extend the prescribed horizontal maps to increasing smooth diffeomorphisms on each fiber: between consecutive prescribed neighborhoods, choose a small positive constant derivative near the ends, interpolate the prescribed positive derivatives in smaller end collars, and add a nonnegative smooth bump in the gap to make the derivative integral equal the required positive image gap. Shrink the collars first so their integrals are smaller than that image gap. The lower separation bound makes this construction smooth in $y$; finite bands and smooth partitions in their overlapping $y$ intervals patch the maps by convex combinations. Such combinations still have positive horizontal derivative and still agree with all required graph values. Outside a large rectangle choose the identity, using the same gap construction. Thus there is a smooth family $h_y$ of increasing horizontal diffeomorphisms carrying one smoothed diagram to the other after an increasing reparameterization of their special heights. The maps $(x,y)\mapsto((1-t)x+t h_y(x),y)$ are plane diffeomorphisms and preserve all special heights and crossing decorations. Increasing reparameterizations of height are likewise isotopic to the identity through increasing functions. They prove bridge isotopy. All choices here are finite. [step 5.1, construct, algebra]

7.1 The same interval pieces and inequalities are associated to every diagram in a bridge isotopy. Critical points and crossings continue uniquely, their height order cannot change, and their incidence, orientations and graph orders cannot change without a forbidden special-height coincidence or crossing degeneracy. Identify corresponding pieces once and for all. Any bridge-like rectangular diagram whose smoothing has this bridge class satisfies precisely these inequalities: its vertical segment gives the corresponding piece and its local horizontal cap or crossing gives the order at an interval endpoint. Conversely steps 4.1–6.1 realize every solution with distinct abscissae. The allowed height vectors form the convex cone $y_1<\cdots<y_N$; the allowed abscissa vectors form the open convex set defined by the finitely many strict inequalities $x_i<x_j$ from step 2.1. Thus two realizations can first have their heights moved to the same ordered values and then have their abscissa vectors joined within this convex set. Moving heights within their fixed order never changes the associated grid. [step 2.1, step 4.1, step 5.1, step 6.1, given, construct]

8.1 Choose a piecewise linear path between the two abscissa vectors which crosses the finitely many hyperplanes $x_i=x_j$ one at a time and transversely. This can be done within the open convex set by choosing one intermediate point outside the finitely many affine hyperplanes which would force a parallel or simultaneous intersection; a small ball inside the open set is not covered by finitely many proper affine hyperplanes, as is seen by restricting their defining polynomials to a generic line. Pairs whose intervals intersect have a prescribed strict inequality and can never meet on this path. Therefore each equality encountered is between pieces with disjoint closed vertical intervals. At the instant of equality they are adjacent in abscissa order, since simultaneous equalities were excluded. Their grid columns exchange by a legal disjoint-interval commutation. Between these finitely many instants both coordinate orders are fixed and the normalized grid is identical. This gives the claimed finite commutation sequence, with markings transported by the unchanged oriented incidence. No assertion about birth/death, crossing-slide, or Reidemeister events is needed for this lemma. [F1, step 3.1, step 7.1, construct, cases] ∎
