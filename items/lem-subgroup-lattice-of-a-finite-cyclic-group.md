## Statement

Let $G=\langle g\rangle$ be a cyclic group of finite order $n\ge1$
([[def-generated-subgroup]]). For every positive divisor $d$ of $n$
([[def-divides-in-z]]):

$$\langle g^{n/d}\rangle \text{ is a subgroup of } G \text{ of order } d,$$

it is the **only** subgroup of $G$ of order $d$, and every subgroup of $G$ is of
this form for exactly one such $d$. Moreover, for positive divisors $d$ and $e$
of $n$,

$$\langle g^{n/d}\rangle\subseteq\langle g^{n/e}\rangle\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d\mid e.$$

The two extremes are instances rather than exceptions: $d=1$ gives the trivial
subgroup $\{e\}$ and $d=n$ gives $G$ itself, and at $n=1$ the only divisor is
$d=1$, where $G$ is trivial.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A cyclic group $G=\langle g\rangle$ whose underlying set is finite of order $n\ge1$; divisibility of integers is that of [[def-divides-in-z]], and orders are those of [[def-order-in-a-group]].

[A1] $|G|=n$, and since $G=\langle g\rangle$, [L1] gives $\operatorname{ord}(g)=|\langle g\rangle|=n$.

[L1] For an element $x$ of finite order $m$ in a group: $x^{k}=e$ if and only if $m\mid k$; the powers $x^{0},x^{1},\dots,x^{m-1}$ are pairwise distinct; and $\langle x\rangle=\{\,x^{s}:s\in\mathbb N,\ s<m\,\}$, so $\langle x\rangle$ is finite with $|\langle x\rangle|=m=\operatorname{ord}(x)$ ([[lem-order-characterisation]]).

[L2] $\langle x\rangle=\{\,x^{k}:k\in\mathbb Z\,\}$: the cyclic subgroup generated by $x$ is exactly the set of integer powers of $x$ ([[lem-cyclic-subgroup-is-the-set-of-powers]]).

[L3] For a finite group $G$ and $H\le G$ one has $|G|=[G:H]\,|H|$; consequently $|H|$ divides $|G|$ ([[thm-lagrange]]).

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 Fix a positive divisor $d$ of $n$ and put $c:=n/d$, an integer with $c\ge1$ and $n=cd$. [given, algebra]

2.1 The element $g^{c}$ has order exactly $d$: first $(g^{c})^{d}=g^{cd}=g^{n}=e$ by [A1] and [L1]; and if $1\le j<d$ then $1\le cj<cd=n$, so $n\nmid cj$ and hence $(g^{c})^{j}=g^{cj}\ne e$ by [L1]. Therefore $|\langle g^{c}\rangle|=d$ by [L1]. [step 1.1, A1, L1, algebra]

2.2 The set of $x\in G$ with $x^{d}=e$ is exactly $\langle g^{c}\rangle$: writing $x=g^{m}$ with $m\in\mathbb Z$ by [L2] and [A1], the condition $x^{d}=g^{md}=e$ says $n\mid md$ by [L1], that is $cd\mid md$, that is $c\mid m$; and $\{\,g^{m}:c\mid m\,\}=\{\,(g^{c})^{k}:k\in\mathbb Z\,\}=\langle g^{c}\rangle$ by [L2]. [step 1.1, A1, L1, L2, algebra]

2.3 For positive divisors $d,e$ of $n$ with $d\mid e$, write $e=df$ with $f\ge1$; then $n/d=(n/e)f$, so $g^{n/d}=(g^{n/e})^{f}\in\langle g^{n/e}\rangle$ by [L2], and therefore $\langle g^{n/d}\rangle\subseteq\langle g^{n/e}\rangle$, the latter being a subgroup containing $g^{n/d}$. [step 1.1, L2, algebra]

3.1 If $H\le G$ has $|H|=d$, then $H=\langle g^{c}\rangle$: each $h\in H$ has $\operatorname{ord}(h)=|\langle h\rangle|$ dividing $|H|=d$ by [L1] and [L3], so $h^{d}=e$ by [L1] and hence $h\in\langle g^{c}\rangle$ by step 2.2; thus $H\subseteq\langle g^{c}\rangle$, and both sets have exactly $d$ elements by step 2.1, so they are equal. [step 2.1, step 2.2, L1, L3]

4.1 Every subgroup $H\le G$ has this form for exactly one positive divisor of $n$: $H$ is a subset of the finite set $G$, so $d:=|H|$ is defined and divides $n$ by [L3] and [A1], and step 3.1 gives $H=\langle g^{n/d}\rangle$; the divisor is determined by $H$, being its order. [step 2.1, step 3.1, A1, L3]


5.1 Conversely, if $\langle g^{n/d}\rangle\subseteq\langle g^{n/e}\rangle$ then $d\mid e$, since by step 2.1 the two subgroups have orders $d$ and $e$ and [L3] applied to the subgroup $\langle g^{n/d}\rangle$ of $\langle g^{n/e}\rangle$ makes $d$ divide $e$. Together with steps 2.1, 3.1, 4.1 and 2.3 this proves every clause of the lemma. [step 2.1, step 3.1, step 2.3, step 4.1, L3] ∎

## Remarks

- **What the divisor lattice buys.** The clause that matters downstream is not existence but uniqueness: a subgroup of a finite cyclic group is pinned down by its order alone, so the Galois correspondence turns "subgroups of $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb F_{q^{n}}/\mathbb F_q)$" into "divisors of $n$" with nothing left to choose ([[thm-intermediate-fields-of-an-extension-of-finite-fields]]).

- **Where cyclicity is used.** Uniqueness fails without it. In the Klein four-group there are three distinct subgroups of order two, and the argument breaks at step 2.2, where the solutions of $x^{2}=e$ form the whole group rather than a single cyclic subgroup.
