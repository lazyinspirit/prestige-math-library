## Statement

Let $\Delta\le\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb R)$ be discrete, let $p\in\mathbb H$ have trivial stabilizer, and suppose $\Delta\backslash\mathbb H$ has finite hyperbolic area $A$. Let
$$D=\{z\in\mathbb H:d(z,p)\le d(z,\delta p)\text{ for every }\delta\in\Delta\}.$$
Then $D$ is a closed finite-sided convex geodesic fundamental polygon. Its closure in the closed-disc compactification has finitely many ideal points, and it has finitely many ordinary corners. The number of its ideal points is at most $\lfloor A/\pi\rfloor+2$. No finite-generation or torsion-free hypothesis is assumed.

## Facts & Assumptions

[F1] The Dirichlet domain is convex, contains an open neighborhood of its free center, has area $A$, and its translates form a locally finite geodesic tiling inside $\mathbb H$ with disjoint interiors. Orbits meet compact sets in finite sets. Each regular side has a unique adjacent tile and a group side pairing. [[lem-discrete-fuchsian-orbits-have-locally-finite-dirichlet-tilings]]

[F2] Closed hyperbolic balls are compact. A compact convex geodesic polygon $P$ has $\operatorname{area}(P)=\sum_v(\pi-\alpha_v)-2\pi$, where $\alpha_v$ are its interior angles. An ideal $r$-gon has area $(r-2)\pi$. A point stabilizer in the orientation-preserving isometry group is the compact rotation group. [[lem-upper-half-plane-metric-and-polygon-area-calculus]]

## Proof

**Given:** The discrete group, free center and finite area in the Statement. All selections in this proof are finite; no choice axiom is used.

1.1 If $r\ge3$ distinct ideal points belong to the closure of $D$, their ideal convex hull lies in $D$: approximate the endpoints by points of the convex domain and take limits of geodesic segments, using closedness inside $\mathbb H$. Its area is $(r-2)\pi$ by F2. Therefore $r\le A/\pi+2$. For $r\le2$ this bound is automatic since $A>0$. An infinite set contains finite subsets of every prescribed finite size, which requires only finite choices, so this bound proves the ideal boundary is finite and gives the stated integer bound. [F1, F2, given, algebra]

1.2 Take any finite collection of distinct ordinary corners of $D$, and take its convex hull. Each selected corner is an extreme point of $D$, because its two distinct supporting sides form an angle strictly below $\pi$; hence it remains a vertex of the hull. Enlarge the hull by finitely many points in the open interior of $D$ if necessary to make a compact two-dimensional polygon $P$. Its angle at each selected corner is at most the angle there in $D$, since $P\subseteq D$. All polygon-corner deficits are nonnegative, so F2 gives $\sum_{v\text{ selected}}(\pi-\alpha_D(v))\le\operatorname{area}(P)+2\pi\le A+2\pi$. Thus every finite sum of the nonnegative ordinary-corner deficits is bounded by $A+2\pi$. No area formula for an infinite polygon has been applied. [F1, F2, construct, algebra]

1.3 Fix an ordinary corner $v\in D$. Its orbit has only finitely many representatives in $D$. Indeed, if $w=\delta v\in D$, the Dirichlet inequality against $\delta p$ gives $d(p,w)\le d(\delta p,w)=d(p,v)$. These representatives lie in one compact ball by F2 and are finite by F1. Write them as $v_1,\ldots,v_n$. The stabilizer $\Delta_v$ is a discrete subgroup of the compact rotation group, so is finite; it is cyclic of some order $m\ge1$, since a finite subgroup of the circle is generated by its least positive rotation angle, by division with remainder. [F1, F2, construct, algebra]

2.1 The tiles incident at $v$ are precisely the tiles obtained by carrying one of $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ to $v$. For each $v_i$ there are exactly $m$ carrying transformations, a coset of $\Delta_v$, and they give distinct tiles because the center has trivial stabilizer. All incident cells have genuine corners. Normalize $v$ to the center of the disc model. The finitely many nearest sites lie on one Euclidean circle of positive radius, with at least three distinct sites. Comparing distances to two such sites is equivalent, to first order at the center, to maximizing the radial dot product with the site direction. Thus the tangent sector for each site extends between the angular midpoints of its two adjacent circular gaps; its angle is half the sum of those gaps. That sum is positive and strictly below $2\pi$, because at least one other positive gap remains. Consequently each sector has angle strictly between zero and $\pi$; no incident cell has the point only in the interior of a face or in a zero-angle wedge. Local finiteness in F1 lets the incident sectors partition a sufficiently small circle around $v$. Hence, for $\alpha_i=\alpha_D(v_i)$, one has $m\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i=2\pi$. [F1, F2, step 1.3, algebra]

3.1 The total deficit of this complete ordinary vertex orbit is $n\pi-2\pi/m$. It is positive because each $\alpha_i<\pi$, and is at least $\pi/3$. For $n=1$, positivity forces $m\ge3$; for $n=2$, it forces $m\ge2$; for $n\ge3$, the deficit is at least $\pi$. If there were more than $3(A+2\pi)/\pi$ distinct ordinary vertex orbits, take a finite collection exceeding that bound and take all their finitely many representatives in $D$. Their deficit sum would contradict step 1.2. Thus there are finitely many ordinary vertex orbits, each with finitely many representatives by step 1.3, so there are finitely many ordinary corners. [step 1.2, step 1.3, step 2.1, algebra]

4.1 Every maximal geodesic face has endpoints either at ordinary corners or in the ideal boundary. An endpoint inside $\mathbb H$ without a corner would have a neighborhood containing only finitely many bisectors by F1, so the same face would continue there. Its maximality excludes that possibility. Steps 1.1 and 3.1 give finitely many possible endpoints. Each face has two distinct endpoints, and their pair determines at most one geodesic face. Therefore there are finitely many maximal faces. Together with F1 this proves that $D$ is the asserted closed finite-sided convex fundamental polygon. [F1, step 1.1, step 3.1, algebra] ∎

## Remark

For an ordinary vertex orbit, write $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ for its representatives in $D$, let $m$ be the order of their finite cyclic stabilizers, and let $\alpha_i$ be the interior angle at $v_i$. Proof steps 1.2, 1.3 and 2.1 establish $0<\alpha_i<\pi$ and $m\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i=2\pi$, so the orbit's total exterior-angle deficit is $n\pi-2\pi/m$. Step 1.2 also proves that every finite set $S$ of ordinary corners satisfies $\sum_{v\in S}(\pi-\alpha(v))\le A+2\pi$. These statements concern actual vertex orbits and do not assert that arbitrary pairs $(n,m)$ are realizable.
