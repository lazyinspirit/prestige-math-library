## Statement

For the finite Weyl reflection action on $S=\mathbb C[V]$, put $S^{\det}=\{p:w\cdot p=\det(w)p\text{ for all }w\in W\}$. Then
$$S^{\det}=\Delta S^W.$$
The invariant quotient is unique. This includes rank zero and the zero polynomial, and is choice-free.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The finite Weyl action and polynomial conventions.

[F1] The discriminant is nonzero, has one nonproportional linear factor per reflecting hyperplane and satisfies $w\cdot\Delta=\det(w)\Delta$, by [[def-weyl-discriminant-and-reflecting-hyperplane-arrangement]].

[F2] Polynomial functions and the substitution action are those of [[def-finite-linear-invariant-and-coinvariant-polynomial-algebras]].

## Proof

1.1 Let $p\in S^{\det}$ and let $\ell_\alpha$ be any factor of $\Delta$. On its reflecting hyperplane $s_\alpha v=v$, so $p(v)=p(s_\alpha v)=-p(v)$ and therefore $p(v)=0$. Extend $\ell_\alpha$ to a linear coordinate system $x_1=\ell_\alpha,x_2,\ldots,x_r$. The constant coefficient in $x_1$ vanishes at every $(x_2,\ldots,x_r)$, hence is the zero polynomial by F2's polynomial-function faithfulness. Thus $\ell_\alpha$ divides $p$. [F1, F2, given, algebra]

1.2 In a polynomial ring over a field a nonzero product is nonzero: choose, for example, the lexicographically highest monomial of each factor, whose product is the unique highest monomial of the product and has nonzero coefficient. Also the ideal generated by a nonzero linear form is prime, since a linear coordinate change identifies its quotient with a polynomial ring in one fewer variable, which is a domain by that same argument. Two nonproportional linear forms do not divide each other, since a quotient between degree-one polynomials would have degree zero. Consequently if each of finitely many pairwise nonproportional linear forms divides $p$, their product divides $p$: induct, and use primeness to divide the remaining quotient by the next factor, since it divides none of the preceding factors. These statements include $p=0$ by taking quotient zero. [F2, algebra]

2.1 Steps 1.1–1.2 give $p=\Delta q$. For $w\in W$, F1 and anti-invariance yield $\det(w)\Delta(w\cdot q)=w\cdot p=\det(w)\Delta q$. The determinant is nonzero and the polynomial ring is a domain, so $w\cdot q=q$. Thus $q\in S^W$, and domain cancellation also proves uniqueness. Conversely if $q$ is invariant, F1 gives $w\cdot(\Delta q)=\det(w)\Delta q$, so it is anti-invariant. When the rank is zero, $\Delta=1$ and both sides are $\mathbb C$. All coordinate extensions and products are finite, so no AC enters. [step 1.1, step 1.2, F1, F2, algebra] ∎
