## Statement

Let $R$ be a commutative ring and let $\mathfrak p$ be an ideal of $R$ that is
maximal in the set $\Sigma$ of non-finitely-generated ideals of $R$
([[lem-maximal-non-finitely-generated-ideal]]): that is, $\mathfrak p$ is not
finitely generated, and every ideal of $R$ strictly containing $\mathfrak p$ is
finitely generated. Then $\mathfrak p$ is a prime ideal
([[def-prime-and-maximal-ideals]]).

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A commutative ring $R$ and an ideal $\mathfrak p$ maximal in the set $\Sigma$ of ideals of $R$ that are not finitely generated ([[lem-maximal-non-finitely-generated-ideal]]). For an ideal $\mathfrak a$ and $a\in R$, write $(\mathfrak a:a):=\{r\in R:ra\in\mathfrak a\}$ and $a\mathfrak a:=\{ax:x\in\mathfrak a\}$.

[L1] A proper ideal $P\subsetneq R$ of a commutative ring is **prime** when $ab\in P$ implies $a\in P$ or $b\in P$ ([[def-prime-and-maximal-ideals]]).

[L2] In a commutative ring, $(S)$ consists of finite sums $\sum r_is_i$, and $(a)=Ra$; the empty sum is included and equals $0$ ([[thm-generated-ideal-description-in-a-commutative-ring]]).

[L3] For $S\subseteq R$, $(S)$ is the intersection of all two-sided ideals containing $S$, so $S\subseteq(S)$; $(\{a\})$ is written $(a)$ ([[def-generated-and-principal-ideals]]).

[L4] For ideals $I,J$ of $R$, the sum is $I+J=\{i+j:i\in I,\ j\in J\}$ ([[def-sum-and-product-of-ideals]]).

[L5] A nonempty subset $I\subseteq R$ is a two-sided ideal exactly when it is closed under $x-y$ and under $rx,xr$ for all $r\in R$, $x,y\in I$ ([[lem-ideal-criterion-and-intersections]]).

## Proof

**Proof technique:** contradiction.

1.1 $\mathfrak p$ is a proper ideal: the unit ideal $R=(1)$ is generated by one element, so $R\notin\Sigma$, whereas $\mathfrak p\in\Sigma$. [L2, L3, given]

1.2 Suppose $\mathfrak p$ is not prime. By the previous step it is proper, so there are $a,b\in R$ with $ab\in\mathfrak p$, $a\notin\mathfrak p$ and $b\notin\mathfrak p$. [assume-contra, L1, given]

2.1 The ideal $\mathfrak p+(a)$ strictly contains $\mathfrak p$, since $a$ lies in it and not in $\mathfrak p$, so by maximality it is finitely generated. Every element of $\mathfrak p+(a)$ has the form $x+wa$ with $x\in\mathfrak p$ and $w\in R$, because $(a)=Ra$; so a finite generating list may be written $x_1+w_1a,\ldots,x_n+w_na$ with $x_i\in\mathfrak p$, $w_i\in R$ and $n\in\mathbb N$. [L2, L3, L4, step 1.2]

2.2 The set $\mathfrak q:=(\mathfrak p:a)$ is an ideal of $R$: it contains $0$, and if $ra,r'a\in\mathfrak p$ then $(r-r')a=ra-r'a\in\mathfrak p$ and $(sr)a=s(ra)\in\mathfrak p$ for every $s\in R$. It contains $\mathfrak p$, since $xa\in\mathfrak p$ for $x\in\mathfrak p$, and it contains $b$, since $ba=ab\in\mathfrak p$; as $b\notin\mathfrak p$ the containment $\mathfrak p\subseteq\mathfrak q$ is strict, so by maximality $\mathfrak q$ is finitely generated, say $\mathfrak q=(q_1,\ldots,q_m)$ with $m\in\mathbb N$. [L2, L5, step 1.2]

3.1 The set $a\mathfrak q$ is an ideal, being closed under differences and under multiplication by $R$ because $\mathfrak q$ is, and it is generated by $aq_1,\ldots,aq_m$: any $q\in\mathfrak q$ is $\sum_jr_jq_j$, so $aq=\sum_jr_j(aq_j)$. Moreover $a\mathfrak q\subseteq\mathfrak p$ by the definition of $\mathfrak q$. [L2, L5, step 2.2]

3.2 $\mathfrak p=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)+a\mathfrak q$. The inclusion from right to left holds because each $x_i$ lies in $\mathfrak p$ and $a\mathfrak q\subseteq\mathfrak p$. For the other inclusion take $z\in\mathfrak p\subseteq\mathfrak p+(a)$ and write $z=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_i(x_i+w_ia)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_ix_i+ya$ with $c_i\in R$ and $y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_iw_i$; then $ya=z-\sum_ic_ix_i$ lies in $\mathfrak p$, so $y\in\mathfrak q$ and $ya\in a\mathfrak q$, whence $z\in(x_1,\ldots,x_n)+a\mathfrak q$. [L2, L4, step 2.1, step 2.2]

4.1 Both summands are finitely generated, so $\mathfrak p=(x_1,\ldots,x_n,aq_1,\ldots,aq_m)$ is finitely generated, contradicting $\mathfrak p\in\Sigma$. The supposition of step 1.2 is therefore untenable: whenever $ab\in\mathfrak p$, either $a\in\mathfrak p$ or $b\in\mathfrak p$, and with step 1.1 this makes $\mathfrak p$ prime. [L1, L2, L4, step 1.1, step 3.1, step 3.2, discharge-contradiction] ∎

## Remarks

- **Where each maximality use goes.** Maximality of $\mathfrak p$ in $\Sigma$ is used exactly twice, in step 2.1 on $\mathfrak p+(a)$ and in step 2.2 on the colon ideal $(\mathfrak p:a)$; both are strictly larger than $\mathfrak p$ precisely because $a\notin\mathfrak p$ and $b\notin\mathfrak p$.

- **The generators of $\mathfrak p+(a)$ are normalised, not merely chosen.** Writing them as $x_i+w_ia$ with $x_i\in\mathfrak p$ is what lets step 3.2 separate the part of $z$ lying in $(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ from the multiple of $a$; an unnormalised list would not split that way.

- **No Noetherian hypothesis anywhere.** The lemma is used inside a proof whose conclusion is that the ring is Noetherian, so assuming a chain condition here would be circular.
