## Example

Let $\mathcal X=\{0,1\}$ with its power-set sigma-algebra. For $p\in[0,1]$, define the Bernoulli law $P_p(\{1\})=p$ and $P_p(\{0\})=1-p$, and use the estimand $\psi(p)=p$. Suppose a common probability space carries an IID sequence with one-observation law $P_p$. Under its IID sample-size experiments, the estimator $T_n(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ is the sample proportion. It satisfies the finite-sample bound
$$P_p^{\otimes n}(|T_n-p|\ge\varepsilon)\le\frac{p(1-p)}{n\varepsilon^2}\le\frac{1}{4n\varepsilon^2},\qquad\varepsilon>0,$$
so it is consistent, including at $p=0$ and $p=1$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** $p\in[0,1]$, the two-point observation space, the displayed Bernoulli masses, and a common probability space carrying an IID sequence with law $P_p$.

[F1] [[def-sigma-algebra]]: The power set of a set is a sigma-algebra.

[F2] [[def-measurable-space]]: A measurable space is a set together with a sigma-algebra.

[F3] [[def-measure]]: A measure assigns zero to the empty set and is countably additive on pairwise disjoint measurable sets.

[F4] [[def-probability-measure]]: A probability measure is a measure of total mass one.

[F5] [[def-statistical-experiment]]: An experiment is a measurable observation space and a parameter-indexed family of probability measures on it.

[F6] [[def-parametric-statistical-model]]: The model is the parameter map $p\mapsto P_p$.

[F7] [[def-estimand-on-a-statistical-model]]: An estimand is a specified function from the parameter set to a target set.

[F8] [[def-estimator-sequence-in-iid-experiments]]: Under IID sampling, the size-$n$ law is $P_p^{\otimes n}$ and an estimator is a measurable map on $\mathcal X^n$.

[F9] [[def-partial-sums-and-sample-means]]: For real random variables, $\overline X_n=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$ is the sample mean.

[F10] [[def-identically-distributed-and-iid-random-variables]]: IID means identical laws and mutual independence.

[F11] [[thm-independent-random-elements-have-product-joint-law]]: A finite independent family has joint law equal to the product of its marginal laws.

[F12] [[def-integral-of-a-nonnegative-simple-function]]: The integral of a nonnegative simple function is the sum of its values times the measures of the corresponding disjoint level sets.

[F13] [[def-moments-variance-and-covariance]]: For square-integrable $X$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[(X-\mathbb E X)^2]$.

[F14] [[cor-iid-finite-variance-weak-law]]: For IID square-integrable real variables with variance $\sigma^2$, $P(|\overline X_n-\mathbb E X_1|\ge\varepsilon)\le\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)$.

[F15] [[thm-iid-sample-mean-consistency]]: An IID real sample with finite first absolute moment has a sample mean consistent in probability for its mean.

[F16] [[def-consistency-in-probability]]: Consistency at $p$ means the probability of an estimation error greater than each fixed $\varepsilon>0$ tends to zero under $P_p^{\otimes n}$.

[F17] [[def-product-sigma-algebra-and-finite-product-sigma-algebras]]: The finite product sigma-algebra is generated by measurable rectangles, so its coordinate projections are measurable.

[F18] [[thm-arithmetic-and-lattice-operations-preserve-measurability]]: Pointwise sums and real scalar multiples of measurable real-valued functions are measurable whenever defined.

## Verification

1.1 The power set of $\{0,1\}$ is a sigma-algebra by [F1], so it defines a measurable space by [F2]. Because $0\le p\le1$, the two singleton masses are nonnegative and sum to one. For any pairwise disjoint sequence of subsets, each of $0$ and $1$ occurs in at most one set, so the mass formula is countably additive and gives zero to the empty set by [F3]. Thus $P_p$ is a probability measure by [F4], and the family is a statistical experiment with model map $p\mapsto P_p$ and target $\psi(p)=p$ by [F5, F6, F7]. [F1, F2, F3, F4, F5, F6, F7, given]

1.2 For $X\sim P_p$, $X=1_{\{1\}}$ and $X^2=X$, so [F12] gives $\mathbb E_pX=p$ and $\mathbb E_pX^2=p$. By [F13], $\operatorname{Var}_p(X)=p-p^2=p(1-p)$, which is finite for all $p\in[0,1]$. [F12, F13, given, algebra]

2.1 By [F17], each coordinate projection on $\mathcal X^n$ is measurable; [F18] makes their finite sum and scalar multiple $T_n$ measurable, as required by [F8]. The displayed map is the sample mean of [F9]. The given common sequence satisfies the IID hypothesis of [F14] by [F10], and [F11] identifies the law of its first $n$ observations with $P_p^{\otimes n}$. Applying [F14] with $\sigma^2=p(1-p)$ from step 1.2 and transferring through this joint-law identity gives $P_p^{\otimes n}(|T_n-p|\ge\varepsilon)\le p(1-p)/(n\varepsilon^2)$. Since $p(1-p)=1/4-(p-1/2)^2\le1/4$, this is at most $1/(4n\varepsilon^2)$. [F8, F9, F10, F11, F14, F17, F18, step 1.2, given, algebra]

3.1 For fixed $\varepsilon>0$, the bound in step 2.1 tends to zero, so [F16] gives consistency; this is also the special case of [F15]. By [F8] and [F11], at $p=0$ and $p=1$ the product law assigns probability one to the all-zero and all-one samples respectively, so $T_n=p$ almost surely at each endpoint. For the finite check $n=2,p=1/2$, the same rectangle formula gives mass $1/4$ to each ordered pair, hence $T_2$ has masses $1/4,1/2,1/4$ at $0,1/2,1$; the probability of error at least $1/2$ is $1/2$, matching the bound in step 2.1. [F8, F11, F15, F16, step 1.2, step 2.1, given, algebra] ∎
