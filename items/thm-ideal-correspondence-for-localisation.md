## Statement

Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $S\subseteq R$ multiplicative. For an ideal $I\subseteq R$, write
$$ S^{-1}I=\{r/s:r\in I,\ s\in S\},\qquad I^{\mathrm{sat}}=\{r\in R:sr\in I\text{ for some }s\in S\}. $$
An ideal is **$S$-saturated** when $I=I^{\mathrm{sat}}$. Extension $I\mapsto S^{-1}I$ and contraction $J\mapsto\lambda_S^{-1}(J)$ give inverse inclusion-preserving bijections between $S$-saturated ideals of $R$ and ideals of $S^{-1}R$. Moreover, they restrict to inverse bijections
$$ \{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}R:\mathfrak p\cap S=\varnothing\}\longleftrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R),\qquad \mathfrak p\longmapsto S^{-1}\mathfrak p. $$

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A commutative ring $R$, a multiplicative subset $S$, and the localisation map $\lambda_S$.

[F1] Localisation has the usual fraction arithmetic, equality is detected by a witness in $S$, and every $s/1$ is a unit ([[thm-localisation-equivalence-and-ring-laws]], [[prop-localisation-zero-equality-and-kernel-criteria]]).

[F2] Ideals are additive subgroups absorbing multiplication; a prime ideal is proper and contains one factor whenever it contains a product ([[def-left-right-and-two-sided-ideal]], [[def-prime-and-maximal-ideals]]).

[L1] Each ideal of $S^{-1}R$ is $S^{-1}I$ for its inverse-image ideal $I$ in $R$ (The Stacks Project, Lemma 10.9.16).

[L2] The map $R\to S^{-1}R$ induces a bijection from the prime ideals of $S^{-1}R$ to the prime ideals of $R$ disjoint from $S$, with inverse $\mathfrak p\mapsto S^{-1}\mathfrak p$ (The Stacks Project, Lemma 10.17.5).

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct extension and contraction.

1.1 The displayed $S^{-1}I$ is an ideal: common-denominator addition preserves numerator membership in $I$, and multiplication by an arbitrary fraction does likewise. It is also the ideal generated by the elements $i/1$ with $i\in I$, since $i/s=(i/1)(1/s)$. [F1, F2]

1.2 For completeness, let $\mathfrak p$ be prime and disjoint from $S$. It is saturated, since $sr\in\mathfrak p$ with $s\notin\mathfrak p$ forces $r\in\mathfrak p$. Its extension is proper: $1=i/s$ with $i\in\mathfrak p$ would give $u(s-i)=0$, hence $us=ui\in\mathfrak p$, contradicting $u,s\in S$. [F1, F2]

2.1 An element $r$ lies in the contraction of $S^{-1}I$ exactly when $r/1=i/s$ for some $i\in I$. Equality gives $u(rs-i)=0$, hence $(us)r=ui\in I$, so $r\in I^{\mathrm{sat}}$. Conversely, if $tr\in I$ for $t\in S$, then $r/1=tr/t\in S^{-1}I$. Thus the contraction is exactly $I^{\mathrm{sat}}$. [F1, step 1.1]

3.1 If $J\subseteq S^{-1}R$ is an ideal and $I=\lambda_S^{-1}(J)$, then $r/s\in J$ implies $r/1=(s/1)(r/s)\in J$, while $r/1\in J$ implies $r/s=(1/s)(r/1)\in J$. Hence $J=S^{-1}I$, as in [L1]. Step 2.1 also makes $I$ saturated. [F1, F2, L1]

3.2 If $(a/s)(b/t)\in S^{-1}\mathfrak p$, contraction and saturation give $uab\in\mathfrak p$ for some $u\in S$. Since $u\notin\mathfrak p$, primality gives $a\in\mathfrak p$ or $b\in\mathfrak p$, so one factor lies in $S^{-1}\mathfrak p$. Thus the extension is prime. [F1, F2, step 2.1, step 1.2]

4.1 Steps 2.1 and 3.1 prove that extension and contraction are inverse on the stated ideals. Both operations visibly preserve inclusion. [step 2.1, step 3.1]

5.1 Conversely, the contraction $\mathfrak q^c$ of a prime $\mathfrak q\subseteq S^{-1}R$ is proper because $1/1\notin\mathfrak q$. If $ab\in\mathfrak q^c$, then $(a/1)(b/1)\in\mathfrak q$, so primality gives $a\in\mathfrak q^c$ or $b\in\mathfrak q^c$. It also misses $S$, because each $s/1$ is a unit and no proper ideal contains a unit. Together with steps 3.2 and 4.1, this proves the prime bijection stated in [L2]. [F1, F2, L2, step 3.2, step 4.1] ∎
