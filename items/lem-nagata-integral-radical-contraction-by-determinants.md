## Statement

Let $A\subseteq B$ be an integral inclusion of commutative rings and let $I$ be any ideal of $A$. Then
$$\sqrt{IB}\cap A=\sqrt I.$$
More generally, every $b\in\sqrt{IB}$ satisfies a monic equation
$$b^d+c_1b^{d-1}+\cdots+c_d=0\qquad(c_i\in I).$$
If $b_1,\ldots,b_r\in B$ generate the unit ideal in $B$, then they already generate the unit ideal in $A[b_1,\ldots,b_r]$. No Noetherianity or finiteness of $B$ over $A$ is required, and zero rings and empty generating families are included.

## Statement references

[Alonso–Coquand–Lombardi, Lemma2.1, pp.4–5](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.00597)

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The integral inclusion and ideal, and for the last assertion a supplied finite unit-ideal generating family.

[F1] A subalgebra generated by finitely many integral elements is module-finite. [[lem-algebra-generated-by-finitely-many-integral-elements-is-module-finite]]

[F2] For a finite module generating family, a matrix describing multiplication gives an annihilating characteristic polynomial by its adjugate identity. [[lem-determinant-trick-for-nakayama]]

[F3] Radical membership means that some positive power lies in the ideal; an element of an extended ideal is a finite sum of ideal coefficients times ring elements. [[def-radical-of-an-ideal]] [[def-sum-and-product-of-ideals]]

## Proof

1.1 Let $b\in\sqrt{IB}$. By [F3], write $b^n=\sum_{j=1}^s a_jv_j$ with $n\geq1$, $a_j\in I$ and $v_j\in B$. The algebra $E=A[b,v_1,\ldots,v_s]\subseteq B$ is module-finite by integrality and [F1]. Take a finite module generating family including $1$. Multiplication by $b^n$ sends $E$ into $IE$, so relative to these generators it is represented by a square matrix $M$ with all entries in $I$. The adjugate identity [F2] shows that the monic polynomial $P(T)=\det(T\mathbf1-M)$ annihilates multiplication by $b^n$ on $E$. Its nonleading coefficients belong to $I$, since each term contributing to them contains an entry of $M$. Applying to $1\in E$ gives $P(b^n)=0$, which, viewed as a polynomial in $b$, has precisely the monic form claimed, with zero coefficients inserted in the missing degrees. [F1, F2, F3, given, construct, algebra]

2.1 If also $b=a\in A$, the equation from step 1.1 holds in $A$ because the inclusion $A\subseteq B$ is injective. Reducing it modulo $I$ gives $a^d=0$ modulo $I$, so $a\in\sqrt I$. This proves $\sqrt{IB}\cap A\subseteq\sqrt I$. Conversely $a^n\in I$ implies $a^n\in IB$, proving the reverse inclusion directly. This proves the displayed equality without a prime-ideal existence or lying-over theorem. [F3, step 1.1, algebra]

3.1 Put $A'=A[b_1,\ldots,b_r]$ and $J=(b_1,\ldots,b_r)A'$. Every element of $B$ remains integral over $A'$ by its original monic equation over $A\subseteq A'$. The hypothesis is $JB=B$. Applying step 2.1 to $A'\subseteq B$ gives $\sqrt J=A'$. In particular $1\in\sqrt J$, and hence $1=1^n\in J$, which is the unit-ideal assertion. If $B$ is the zero ring, injectivity forces $A$ and $A'$ to be zero too, so all assertions hold. An empty unit-generating family similarly forces $B=0$. Empty membership sums in step 1.1 give the zero matrix and a pure power equation. Thus zero ideals, unit ideals and empty families introduce no exception. [F3, step 2.1, construct, algebra] ∎
