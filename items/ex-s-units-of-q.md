## Example

Assume the Axiom of Choice. Let $S=\{p_1,\dots,p_m\}$ be a finite set of primes,
let
$$M=\{p_1^{e_1}\cdots p_m^{e_m}:e_1,\dots,e_m\in\mathbb N\}$$
be the multiplicative subset of $\mathbb Z$ generated by $S$ (the empty product
gives $1$, so that $M=\{1\}$ when $m=0$), and let
$\mathbb Z[S^{-1}]=M^{-1}\mathbb Z$ be the localisation of $\mathbb Z$ at $M$.
Then, under the canonical embedding of $\mathbb Z[S^{-1}]$ in $\mathbb Q$,
$$\mathbb Z[S^{-1}]^\times=\Bigl\{\pm p_1^{n_1}\cdots p_m^{n_m}:n_1,\dots,n_m\in\mathbb Z\Bigr\}\cong\{\pm1\}\times\mathbb Z^m,$$
the group law on the right being addition of the exponent vector. This agrees
with the $S$-unit theorem for $K=\mathbb Q$: applied to the finite set
$S_{\mathbb Q}=\{(p_1),\dots,(p_m)\}$ of nonzero prime ideals of
$\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q}=\mathbb Z$ it gives
$\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q,S_{\mathbb Q}}^\times\cong\mu(\mathbb Q)\times\mathbb Z^{m}$,
of rank $r_1+r_2-1+|S_{\mathbb Q}|=1+0-1+m=m$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The Axiom of Choice, a finite set $S=\{p_1,\dots,p_m\}$ of primes ([[def-prime]]), the multiplicative subset $M\subseteq\mathbb Z$ generated by $S$, and the localisation $R:=\mathbb Z[S^{-1}]=M^{-1}\mathbb Z$ ([[def-multiplicative-subset-and-localisation]]).

[F1] $M$ is multiplicative and its elements are exactly the products $p_1^{e_1}\cdots p_m^{e_m}$ with all $e_i\in\mathbb N$, including the empty product $1$. Elements of $R$ are classes $a/b$ with $a\in\mathbb Z$, $b\in M$, with $a/b+a'/b'=(ab'+a'b)/(bb')$ and $(a/b)(a'/b')=(aa')/(bb')$; two classes are equal, $a/b=a'/b'$, exactly when $u(ab'-a'b)=0$ for some $u\in M$; the localisation map $a\mapsto a/1$ is a ring homomorphism, and every $s\in M$ maps to a unit, $(s/1)^{-1}=1/s$ ([[def-multiplicative-subset-and-localisation]]).

[F2] A product of two nonzero integers is nonzero, so $ut=0$ with $u,t\in\mathbb Z$ and $u\ne0$ forces $t=0$; since $0\notin M$, the equality criterion of [F1] reduces to $ab'=a'b$ ([[lem-int-cancellation]]).

[F3] Every positive integer $n\ge1$ is a finite product of primes, and the factorisation is unique up to order; a prime is an integer $p>1$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$ ([[thm-fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic]], [[def-prime]]).

[F4] The units of $\mathbb Z$ are exactly $1$ and $-1$ ([[lem-units-of-z]]).

[F5] For every prime $q$ the principal ideal $(q)=q\mathbb Z$ is a nonzero prime ideal of $\mathbb Z$, indeed a maximal one: $\mathbb Z/(q)$ is a field ([[thm-z-mod-p-is-a-field]]) and a quotient ring is a field exactly when the ideal is maximal ([[thm-quotient-is-field-iff-ideal-maximal]]), while a maximal ideal is prime ([[cor-maximal-ideals-are-prime]]); the ideal is nonzero because $q\ne0$ and proper because $q>1$. Distinct primes give distinct ideals, since $q\in(q')$ forces $q'=q$. The localisation $\mathbb Z_{(q)}$ of $\mathbb Z$ at the prime ideal $(q)$ consists of the fractions $m/n$ with $q\nmid n$, and every element of $\mathbb Z$ outside $(q)$ becomes a unit there ([[def-localisation-at-a-prime-ideal]], [[def-multiplicative-subset-and-localisation]]). Moreover, assuming the Axiom of Choice, $\mathbb Z=\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q}$ is a Dedekind domain ([[cor-ring-of-integers-is-a-dedekind-domain]]), each localisation $\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$ at a nonzero prime is a discrete valuation ring ([[lem-dedekind-localisation-at-nonzero-prime-is-dvr]]), and every nonzero fractional ideal $I$ carries prime-ideal valuations defined by $I_{\mathfrak p}=\mathfrak p^{v_{\mathfrak p}(I)}\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$ ([[def-fractional-ideal]], [[def-prime-ideal-valuations-on-fractional-ideals]]); the nonzero ideals of the discrete valuation ring $\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$ are the powers $\mathfrak p^n\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$ with $n\ge0$ ([[thm-ideals-in-a-dvr]]), and for a nonzero rational the principal fractional ideal $(x)=x\mathbb Z$ localises to $(x)_{\mathfrak p}=x\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$ ([[def-localisation-of-a-module]]).

[F6] For a number field $K$ ([[def-number-field]]) and a finite set $S$ of nonzero prime ideals of $\mathcal O_K$, $$\mathcal O_{K,S}=\{0\}\cup\{x\in K^\times:v_{\mathfrak p}(x)\ge0\text{ for every nonzero prime }\mathfrak p\notin S\}$$ consists of zero and the nonzero elements whose principal fractional ideal involves no prime outside $S$ in a denominator, and $\mathcal O_{K,S}^\times=\{x\in K^\times:v_{\mathfrak p}(x)=0$ for every nonzero prime $\mathfrak p\notin S\}$; no infinite place belongs to $S$, and the rank formula is $r_1+r_2-1+|S|$ ([[def-s-integers-and-s-units-of-a-number-field]]).

[F7] Assume the Axiom of Choice. For a number field $K$ of signature $(r_1,r_2)$ and a finite set $S$ of nonzero prime ideals, $\mathcal O_{K,S}^\times\cong\mu(K)\times\mathbb Z^{r_1+r_2-1+|S|}$ ([[thm-s-unit-theorem]]).

[F8] $\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q}=\mathbb Z$: the ring of integers $\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q}$ is the integral closure of $\mathbb Z$ in $\mathbb Q$ ([[def-ring-of-integers-of-a-number-field]]), a rational number integral over $\mathbb Z$ is an integer ([[cor-rational-algebraic-integers-are-integers]]), and every integer $n$ is a root of the monic polynomial $X-n$. Also $\mathbb Q$ has a single archimedean place, which is real, so its signature is $(1,0)$ ([[def-archimedean-embeddings-and-number-field-signature]]).

[F9] For a field $K$ and $n\ge1$, $\mu_n(K)=\{x\in K:x^n=1\}$ and $\mu(K)$ is the group of all roots of unity in $K$, the union of the $\mu_n(K)$ ([[def-roots-of-unity-in-a-field]]).

[A1] The Axiom of Choice is assumed. It is used through the $S$-unit theorem [F7] and through the Dedekind structure of $\mathbb Z$ quoted in [F5], where the ring-of-integers corollary is itself AC-qualified; the localisation, exponent and sign computations of the verification use no choice ([[def-axiom-of-choice]]).

## Verification

**Proof technique:** identify the localisation with a subring of $\mathbb Q$, express membership in it and in its unit group through the exponents of the prime factorisation of a rational, and then compare the resulting monomial group with the $S$-unit theorem applied to $K=\mathbb Q$.

1.1 The localisation $R$ embeds in $\mathbb Q$ by $\varphi(a/b)=a/b$: the map is well defined because if $a/b=a'/b'$ in $R$, then $u(ab'-a'b)=0$ for some $u\in M$ by [F1] and hence $ab'=a'b$ by [F2], so the two rationals agree; it respects the fraction arithmetic of [F1], which is the arithmetic of the field $\mathbb Q$; and it is injective, since $\varphi(a/b)=0$ forces $a=0$, and then $1\cdot(a\cdot1-0\cdot b)=0$ gives $a/b=0/1$ by [F1]. Hence $R$ is identified with the subring $\{a/b\in\mathbb Q:a\in\mathbb Z,\ b\in M\}$ of $\mathbb Q$, in which $1/s$ is the inverse of $s$ for every $s\in M$. [F1, F2, algebra]

1.2 Fix $x\in\mathbb Q^\times$ and write its prime factorisation as $$x=\pm\prod_{q\ \mathrm{prime}}q^{e_q(x)},\qquad e_q(x)\in\mathbb Z,$$ with all but finitely many exponents zero; existence and uniqueness of the triple (sign, exponent vector) is [F3], applied to the numerator and denominator of $x$ in lowest terms. Writing $x=a/b$ in lowest terms with $b>0$ and fixing a prime $q$, one has $e_q(x)=v_q(a)-v_q(b)$; if $q\nmid b$ this is the nonnegative integer $v_q(a)$, while if $q\mid b$ then $v_q(b)>0$ and $v_q(a)=0$ by coprimality, so $e_q(x)<0$. Thus $e_q(x)\ge0$ if and only if $q\nmid b$. [F3, algebra]
1.3 For a prime $q$, the localisation $\mathbb Z_{(q)}$ consists of the fractions $m/n$ with $q\nmid n$ by [F5], so for $x=a/b\in\mathbb Q^\times$ in lowest terms with $b>0$ one has $x\in\mathbb Z_{(q)}$ if and only if $q\nmid b$: if $q\nmid b$ then $x=a/b$ has the required form, while if $x=a/b=m/n$ with $q\nmid n$, then $an=bm$, and $q\mid b$ would give $q\mid an$ and hence $q\mid a$, contradicting coprimality. [F5, algebra]
1.4 For a nonzero prime ideal $\mathfrak p$ of $\mathbb Z$ and $x\in\mathbb Q^\times$, [F5] writes $v_{\mathfrak p}(x)=v_{\mathfrak p}((x))$ as the unique integer $n$ with $x\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}=(x)_{\mathfrak p}=\mathfrak p^n\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$. The powers $\mathfrak p^n\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$ with $n\ge0$ are exactly the ideals of the discrete valuation ring $\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$, and for $n<0$ the fractional ideal $\mathfrak p^n\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$ properly contains $\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$; hence $v_{\mathfrak p}(x)\ge0$ if and only if $x\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}\subseteq\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$, that is, if and only if $x\in\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$. [F5, algebra]

2.1 An element $x\in\mathbb Q^\times$ lies in $R$ if and only if $e_q(x)\ge0$ for every prime $q\notin S$. If $x=a/b$ with $a\in\mathbb Z$ and $b\in M$, then $b$ has no prime divisor outside $S$, so for $q\notin S$ one has $e_q(x)=v_q(a)-v_q(b)=v_q(a)-0\ge0$, where the exponents are those of the factorisations of $a$ and $b$; conversely, if $e_q(x)\ge0$ for all $q\notin S$ and $x=a/b$ is in lowest terms with $b>0$, then a prime $q\mid b$ satisfies $v_q(a)=0$ and $v_q(x)=-v_q(b)<0$, so $q\in S$; hence every prime divisor of $b$ lies in $S$, that is $b\in M$, and $x=a/b\in R$. [F1, F3, F5, step 1.1, step 1.2, algebra]

2.2 For the comparison with the $S$-unit theorem, evaluate its torsion factor and its signature input. $\mathbb Q$ is a number field with signature $(1,0)$ and $\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q}=\mathbb Z$ by [F8]; the roots of unity in $\mathbb Q$ are $\pm1$, since $x=a/b\in\mathbb Q^\times$ in lowest terms with $x^n=1$ gives $a^n=b^n$, whence $|a|^n=|b|^n$ and, by uniqueness of prime factorisation, $|a|=|b|$, while coprimality forces $|a|=|b|=1$; conversely $\pm1$ are roots of unity. Thus $\mu(\mathbb Q)=\{\pm1\}$ by [F9]. [F3, F4, F8, F9, step 1.2, algebra]

2.3 The same group is obtained from the $S$-unit theorem. By [F5] the ideals $(p_1),\dots,(p_m)$ are nonzero prime ideals of $\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q}=\mathbb Z$ by [F8], so $S_{\mathbb Q}:=\{(p_1),\dots,(p_m)\}$ is a finite set of nonzero prime ideals. The two rings agree on $\mathbb Q^\times$, and both contain $0$. Indeed, let $0\ne x=a/b\in R$ with $a\in\mathbb Z$ and $b\in M$, and let $\mathfrak p\notin S_{\mathbb Q}$ be a nonzero prime ideal: then $b\notin\mathfrak p$, because otherwise some prime $p_i$ with $p_i\mid b$ lies in $\mathfrak p$, so $(p_i)\subseteq\mathfrak p$, and maximality of $(p_i)$ with $\mathfrak p$ proper forces $\mathfrak p=(p_i)\in S_{\mathbb Q}$, a contradiction. Hence $b/1$ is a unit of $\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$ and $x=(a/1)(b/1)^{-1}\in\mathbb Z_{\mathfrak p}$, so $v_{\mathfrak p}(x)\ge0$ by step 1.4 and $x\in\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q,S_{\mathbb Q}}$. Conversely, let $0\ne x=a/b\in\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q,S_{\mathbb Q}}$ be in lowest terms with $b>0$, and suppose a prime $q\notin S$ divides $b$; then $(q)$ is a nonzero prime ideal of $\mathbb Z$ and $(q)\notin S_{\mathbb Q}$ by the distinctness in [F5], so $v_{(q)}(x)\ge0$ and step 1.4 gives $x\in\mathbb Z_{(q)}$, contradicting step 1.3. Therefore every prime divisor of $b$ lies in $S$, that is $b\in M$ and $x=a/b\in R$. Thus $\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q,S_{\mathbb Q}}=R$ as subrings of $\mathbb Q$, and their unit groups inside $\mathbb Q$ coincide: $\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q,S_{\mathbb Q}}^\times=R^\times$. [F5, F6, F8, step 1.3, step 1.4]

3.1 Consequently an element $x\in\mathbb Q^\times$ is a unit of $R$ if and only if $e_q(x)=0$ for every prime $q\notin S$: a unit of $R$ lies in $R$ together with its inverse, so step 2.1 gives $e_q(x)\ge0$ and $e_q(x^{-1})=-e_q(x)\ge0$ for $q\notin S$, and conversely the two inequalities $e_q(x)\ge0$, $-e_q(x)\ge0$ for $q\notin S$ place both $x$ and $x^{-1}$ in $R$. [F1, step 2.1, algebra]

4.1 Therefore $R^\times=\{x\in\mathbb Q^\times:e_q(x)=0$ for all $q\notin S\}=\{\pm p_1^{n_1}\cdots p_m^{n_m}:n_i\in\mathbb Z\}$: for the first description, an element with vanishing exponents outside $S$ has prime factorisation involving only primes of $S$ and a sign, and conversely a monomial $\pm p_1^{n_1}\cdots p_m^{n_m}$ has all exponents outside $S$ equal to zero; the sign and the exponents in such an expression are unique by the uniqueness clause of [F3]. [F3, step 3.1, algebra]

5.1 The assignment $(\varepsilon,n_1,\dots,n_m)\mapsto\varepsilon p_1^{n_1}\cdots p_m^{n_m}$ is an isomorphism $\{\pm1\}\times\mathbb Z^m\to R^\times$: it is a group homomorphism because the exponents add, it is surjective by step 4.1, and it is injective because $\varepsilon p_1^{n_1}\cdots p_m^{n_m}=1$ forces $\varepsilon=1$ and $n_1=\cdots=n_m=0$ by the uniqueness of the factorisation of the positive integer obtained after moving negative exponents to the other side. [F3, step 4.1, algebra]

6.1 By the $S$-unit theorem [F7] applied to $K=\mathbb Q$ and $S_{\mathbb Q}$, $$\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q,S_{\mathbb Q}}^\times\cong\mu(\mathbb Q)\times\mathbb Z^{r_1+r_2-1+|S_{\mathbb Q}|}=\{\pm1\}\times\mathbb Z^{1+0-1+m}=\{\pm1\}\times\mathbb Z^m,$$ of rank $m$. Combined with step 2.3 this agrees with the explicit computation of steps 4.1 and 5.1, and the $m$ generators are the classes of $p_1,\dots,p_m$. [F7, step 5.1, step 2.3, step 2.2]

7.1 Scope and boundary cases: for $m=0$ the set $M=\{1\}$ and $R=\mathbb Z$, and the computation returns $\mathbb Z^\times=\{\pm1\}\cong\{\pm1\}\times\mathbb Z^0$ by [F4], while $S_{\mathbb Q}=\emptyset$ has rank $0$; for $m\ge1$ the exponents $n_i$ range over $\mathbb Z$ and negative exponents are allowed, the generators being units because $p_i/1\in M$ has inverse $1/p_i$ in $R$. Choice enters through the $S$-unit theorem [F7] and through the AC-qualified Dedekind interface of [F5]; the identification of $R$ with a subring of $\mathbb Q$, the exponent bookkeeping and the enumeration of signs use no choice. [A1, F5, F7, step 1.1, step 5.1] ∎
