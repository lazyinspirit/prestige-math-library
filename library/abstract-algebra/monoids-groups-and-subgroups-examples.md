**Objective.** This page is the worked companion to Binary Operations, Monoids,
Groups and Subgroups, linked under Prerequisites above. That page fixes the
axioms and proves the general theory with no example in sight; this one supplies
the examples, and the witnesses for every place where the general page had to
carry a hypothesis. Seven examples and four refutations, three counterexamples
and one false statement, are recorded here.

**The number systems, re-read as groups.** The library already owns
$\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}$, so the first task is to
see the group axioms inside them rather than to build anything new.
[[ex-additive-and-multiplicative-groups-of-a-field]] does it in one move: the
field axioms already assert that $(F,+)$ and $(F \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$ are
abelian groups, and all that has to be checked is that the equations those axioms
spell out really give the group axioms, including the one thing left implicit,
that a product of nonzero elements is nonzero. Applied to $\mathbb{Q}$ and to
$\mathbb{R}$ this gives four groups at once.
[[ex-integers-under-addition]] treats $\mathbb{Z}$, where the multiplicative
story is different: $(\mathbb{Z},\cdot)$ is only a monoid, and its group of units
is $\{1,-1\}$, which takes a genuine argument from the order.

**Cancellation is not invertibility.**
[[cex-cancellative-monoid-not-a-group]] is the sharpest form of the observation
that $(\mathbb{N},+)$ is not a group: it is a *cancellative* commutative monoid
and still not a group. The weaker statement would leave open the possibility that
cancellation is exactly what a monoid is missing, and it is not. In a group
cancellation is a theorem, so the implication runs one way only, and the
construction of $\mathbb{Z}$ from $\mathbb{N}$ is precisely the repair.

**The first finite groups.** [[ex-integers-mod-n-additive-group]] builds
$(\mathbb{Z}/n, +)$ for $n \ge 1$ from the congruence relation, showing it is
abelian of order $n$ and cyclic, generated by the class of $1$. The hypothesis
$n \ge 1$ is carried in the title and in the statement, because at $n = 0$ the
relation is equality and the group is infinite. Only division with remainder is
used, never a greatest common divisor, which is not available at this point in
the reading order. [[ex-symmetric-group-on-three-letters]] works out
$\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3\})$ in full: six elements, listed and shown to
exhaust the group, not abelian, with element orders $1$, $2$ and $3$.

**Where the hypotheses on the general page earn their keep.**
[[cex-power-of-a-product-in-a-nonabelian-group]] takes two transpositions in
$\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3\})$ and computes $(gh)^{2} \ne g^{2}h^{2}$, which is
the witness behind the commuting hypothesis in
[[lem-group-power-laws]]. At the exponent $2$ the law holds for a pair exactly
when the pair commutes, so any non-abelian group would do.
[[cex-subset-closed-under-the-operation-not-a-subgroup]] takes the nonnegative
integers inside $(\mathbb{Z},+)$: nonempty, containing the identity, closed under
the operation, and not a subgroup, which is why the one-step test of
[[lem-subgroup-criterion]] tests $gh^{-1}$ rather than $gh$.
[[fs-union-of-two-subgroups-is-a-subgroup]] refutes the union statement with
$2\mathbb{Z}$ and $3\mathbb{Z}$, whose union contains $2$ and $3$ but not $5$,
and records the exact condition under which a union of two subgroups is a
subgroup: one of them contains the other.

**Two groups built inside a symmetric group.** [[ex-klein-four-group]] realises
the Klein four-group as the four permutations
$\mathrm{id}, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)$ of $\{1,2,3,4\}$, abelian of order
$4$, with every non-identity element of order $2$, and therefore not cyclic. It
is the witness that the converse of the general page's "every cyclic group is
abelian" is false: an abelian group need not be cyclic. [[ex-symmetries-of-a-square]] realises the eight vertex permutations of
a square as the subgroup generated by the $4$-cycle $(1\,2\,3\,4)$ and the
diagonal swap $(1\,3)$, non-abelian of order $8$, with the relation
$sr = r^{-1}s$ doing all the work. Both are built inside
$\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3,4\})$ rather than as products or as plane figures,
because neither the direct product of groups nor the Euclidean plane is available
at this point in the reading order. The square is treated combinatorially: its
edges are four pairs of vertices, and the claim proved is that these eight
permutations are exactly the permutations preserving that edge relation.

**The subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$, completely.**
[[ex-subgroups-of-the-integers]] shows $n\mathbb{Z} = \langle n \rangle$ is a
subgroup for every integer $n$ and that these are all of them, the converse half
being the standard least-positive-element argument driven by
[[thm-division-algorithm-in-z]]. In particular every subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z},+)$
is cyclic, and inclusion among these subgroups is divisibility. This example is
what supplies the two subgroups used in the false statement above.
