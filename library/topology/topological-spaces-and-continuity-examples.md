**Objective.** This page is the worked companion to Topological Spaces and
Continuity, linked under Prerequisites above. That page fixes the axioms and
proves the general theory; this one exhibits it. Seven examples carry out full
computations in concrete spaces, and seven counterexamples supply the witnesses
behind the false statements and behind the non-reversal claims that the general
page deliberately declined to assert.

**The examples run from the two extremes to the two standard pathologies.** The
discrete and indiscrete topologies bracket every topology on a set: in the first
every subset is clopen and every map out of the space is continuous, in the
second the closure of every nonempty set is everything and every map into the
space is continuous ([[ex-discrete-and-indiscrete-topologies]]). The cofinite
topology on an infinite set makes every infinite subset dense and leaves no two
nonempty open sets disjoint, while keeping all its points closed
([[ex-cofinite-topology]]). Its countable analogue on $\mathbb{R}$ is the source
of most of the trouble on the general page: there the closed sets are exactly the
at most countable sets together with $\mathbb{R}$, and a sequence converges only
when it is eventually constant ([[ex-cocountable-topology-on-r]]). Sierpinski
space and the particular-point topology complete the small-space picture, and the
continuous maps into Sierpinski space turn out to be exactly the open subsets of
the source ([[ex-sierpinski-and-particular-point-spaces]]).

**Two examples build topologies from the order of $\mathbb{R}$, and one settles a
counting question.** The order topology of a totally ordered set is generated by
the open rays, and on $\mathbb{R}$ it is the usual topology, not a new one
([[ex-order-topology]]); together with [[rem-r-native-topology-scope]], quoted
there, this pins the metric, the order and the order-native descriptions of
$\mathbb{R}$'s topology as one collection of open sets. The Sorgenfrey line takes
the half-open intervals $[a,b)$ as a basis instead, and is strictly finer, first
countable, and possessed of an at most countable dense subset, with sequences that
converge only from the right ([[ex-sorgenfrey-line]]); the word *separable* is not
used, since no item in this library defines it. The counting question is
Kuratowski's: closure and complement generate at most fourteen sets from any
subset of any space, and $(0,1) \cup (1,2) \cup \{3\} \cup ([4,5] \cap \mathbb{Q})$
attains fourteen in $\mathbb{R}$ ([[ex-kuratowski-fourteen-sets]]).

**The counterexamples pay the general page's debts, one for one.** The sequential
closure can be strictly smaller than the closure: in the cocountable topology on
$\mathbb{R}$ the sequential closure of $[0,1]$ is $[0,1]$ and its closure is
everything ([[cex-sequential-closure-strictly-inside-closure]]). Sequential
continuity does not imply continuity: the identity from that topology to the
usual one is sequentially continuous and not continuous
([[cex-sequentially-continuous-not-continuous]]). Sequential limits are not
unique: in the indiscrete topology every sequence converges to every point, and in
the cofinite topology on an infinite set every injective sequence does, the
instance on $\mathbb{N}$ with the identity sequence needing no choice principle
([[cex-sequential-limits-not-unique]]). A continuous bijection need not be a
homeomorphism, on $\mathbb{R}$ with the discrete and the usual topologies
([[cex-continuous-bijection-not-a-homeomorphism]]). The closed pasting lemma
genuinely needs finiteness: $\mathbb{R}$ covered by its closed singletons defeats
it with the indicator of $\{0\}$
([[cex-pasting-fails-for-an-infinite-closed-cover]]). The interior of a union can
be strictly larger than the union of the interiors, with $\mathbb{Q}$ and its
complement inside $\mathbb{R}$ ([[cex-interior-of-a-union-is-larger]]). And not
every topology is metrizable, the indiscrete topology on two points being induced
by no metric ([[cex-non-metrizable-topology]]).

**Where the choice principles are, and are not.** No item on this page invokes a
choice principle in its own argument. The one place a choice hypothesis is
actually appealed to is on the general page, where topology-hood of the
cocountable family is verified by citing the countable-union theorem; the instance used there is a union
of two sets, for which the hypothesis is not needed, and the definition says so.
Two items here come close and say so as well: the cofinite clause of
[[cex-sequential-limits-not-unique]] is stated for a sequence that is *given* to
be injective, and is instantiated on $\mathbb{N}$ by the identity sequence rather
than by extracting a countable subset from an arbitrary infinite set, which is not
a theorem of ZF; and the countability arguments about the cocountable topology use
only that a subset of an at most countable set is at most countable and that the
range of a sequence is at most countable, both choice-free.
