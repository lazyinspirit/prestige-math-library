The published clique and stable-set numbers of a finite graph, the notions of a hereditary class and of an $H$-free class, the complement of a graph and of a class, and the facts that complementation preserves hereditary classes and exchanges cliques with stable sets are the setting for what follows; induced embeddings compose. Finite Ramsey theory supplies the binomial bound on Ramsey numbers; the Erdős–Rényi random graph, the probability that a fixed pattern occurs, and the first-moment method supply the probabilistic counterpart; and the published theory of real powers and logarithms, including change of base and the fact that a logarithm grows more slowly than every positive power, is what makes $\log n$ and $n^{\epsilon}$ comparable.

A homogeneous set is a clique or a stable set, and $\operatorname{hom}(G)$ is the larger of the two numbers; a hereditary class has the Erdős–Hajnal property when some positive exponent $\epsilon$ forces $\operatorname{hom}(G)\ge|V(G)|^{\epsilon}$ throughout the class. The page proves that the admissible exponents are downward closed, that every nonempty $n$-vertex graph satisfies $\operatorname{hom}(G)\ge\tfrac12\log_2 n$, that $K_t$-free classes have the property and every $P_3$-free graph satisfies $\operatorname{hom}(G)\ge\sqrt{|V(G)|}$, and that for $n\ge16$ some $n$-vertex graph has $\operatorname{hom}(G)<3\log_2 n$, so the class of all graphs does not have the property. It then establishes complement invariance, passage to hereditary subclasses, monotonicity under induced pattern containment, the property for every graph on at most three vertices, and the equivalence of the single-pattern and finite-family formulations, closing with a remark stating the conjecture.
