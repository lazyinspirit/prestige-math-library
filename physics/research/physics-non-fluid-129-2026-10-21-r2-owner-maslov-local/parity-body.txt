## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The finite-mode conventions of the Statement. Work in grouped Schrödinger coordinates $Q=\sqrt\hbar x$, $P=-i\sqrt\hbar\partial_x$, transported by the given Fock–Schrödinger unitary when necessary. Write $p=-i\partial_x$. A normalized quadratic evolution is taken along a compact parameter interval, with a piecewise-smooth real symmetric coefficient matrix $K(t)$; the equation holds on each smooth piece on $\mathcal S(\mathbb R^r)$, with continuous matching at the joins. Its propagator and inverse preserve Schwartz space, and its trajectories are differentiable there on those pieces. No scalar or linear Hamiltonian is permitted.

[F1] The finite-mode definition supplies AC, the quadrature and Weyl conventions, the Schwartz domain and the regular irreducible Weyl system. [[def-nrqmx-finite-mode-symplectic-data]]

[F2] For a symplectic block matrix with $A$ invertible, $Y=CA^{-1}$ and $X=A^{-1}B$ are symmetric and $S=L_YD_AU_X$. Every symplectic matrix is a finite product of the elementary matrices. [[lem-nrqmx-finite-symplectic-generator-factorization]]

[F3] The Weyl system is irreducible; two unitaries inducing the same symplectic Weyl covariance differ by a scalar of modulus one. [[lem-nrqmx-finite-symplectic-implementers]]

[F4] Fourier transformation is unitary on $L^2$ and is a topological automorphism of Schwartz space. We use the unitary angular-frequency normalization $(2\pi)^{-r/2}\int e^{-ik\cdot x}f(x)\,dx$, obtained from the suppliers' frequency convention by a real dilation. [[thm-plancherel]] [[cor-fourier-transform-is-a-topological-automorphism-of-schwartz-space]]

## Proof

**Proof technique:** construct quadratic evolution from elementary operators near the identity, then determine its possible scalar phase from the transported Gaussian, without an imported oscillatory-kernel or Maslov-index theorem.

1.1 Put $J_0=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\-I&0\end{pmatrix}$. Near the identity the block $A$ is invertible with positive determinant. For the factorization in [F2] define $C_Yf(x)=e^{ix^TYx/2}f(x)$, $E_Af(x)=(\det A)^{-1/2}f(A^{-1}x)$ and $T_X=\mathcal F^{-1}e^{-ik^TXk/2}\mathcal F$. Each is unitary by change of variables or [F4]. The product rule and linear change of variables show that each factor and its inverse act continuously on Schwartz space and depend smoothly on their parameters there. For example every derivative of a chirp is a polynomial times the same chirp, giving bounds by finitely many Schwartz seminorms, uniformly on compact parameter sets; differentiation in a parameter inserts only another polynomial or a first-order differential operator. The same argument applies in Fourier coordinates to $T_X$ by [F4]. Direct differentiation or substitution gives $C_Y^*(Q,P)^TC_Y=L_Y(Q,P)^T$, $E_A^*(Q,P)^TE_A=D_A(Q,P)^T$, and $T_X^*(Q,P)^TT_X=U_X(Q,P)^T$. The same substitutions in the explicit Weyl formula give covariance on all $L^2$. Thus $L(S):=C_YE_AT_X$ is a smooth local unitary Schwartz lift of $S$, with $L(I)=I$. [F1, F2, F4, construct, algebra]

1.2 Along a smooth local path the three logarithmic generators $i\hbar\dot C_YC_Y^{-1}$, $i\hbar\dot E_AE_A^{-1}$ and $i\hbar\dot T_XT_X^{-1}$ are respectively $-\hbar x^T\dot Yx/2$, $-i\hbar((\dot AA^{-1}x)\cdot\partial_x+\operatorname{tr}(\dot AA^{-1})/2)$ and $\hbar p^T\dot Xp/2$. The half-trace in the middle expression is exactly the symmetric ordering of the mixed position-momentum term. Product differentiation conjugates and adds these expressions. The covariance from step 1.1 replaces each quadrature by a real linear combination, so a symmetrically ordered homogeneous quadratic stays exactly such a quadratic, with no extra scalar. If its coefficient matrix is $K_L$, differentiating the covariance and using $[R_j,R_k]=i\hbar(J_0)_{jk}$ gives $\dot S=J_0K_LS$, hence $K_L=-J_0\dot SS^{-1}$. Equality of the quadratic coefficients is literal: the coefficients of second derivatives, of $x_j\partial_k$, and of $x_jx_k$ determine the three real blocks, including the symmetric-order half-trace. [F1, step 1.1, algebra]

1.3 Given any piecewise-smooth symmetric $K(t)$, let $S(0)=I$ solve the finite matrix equation $\dot S=J_0K(t)S$. Its solution exists throughout the compact interval by the usual integral series: on a piece where $\|J_0K\|\le C$, the $n$th iterated integral is bounded by $C^nt^n/n!$; the series and its derivative converge uniformly. Differentiating $S^TJ_0S$ gives zero, so $S$ stays symplectic. A finite subdivision makes every $S(t)S(t_j)^{-1}$ lie in the local neighborhood of step 1.1 on its subinterval. Define $U(0)=I$ and $U(t)=L(S(t)S(t_j)^{-1})U(t_j)$ there. Step 1.2 gives exactly $i\hbar\dot U=H_KU$, where $H_K=\tfrac14\sum_{a,b}K_{ab}(R_aR_b+R_bR_a)$; these are unitary Schwartz automorphisms, continuous at the joins. The expression is symmetric on Schwartz space by integration by parts. Two Schwartz solutions of the same equation with the same initial vector agree, because the derivative of their squared $L^2$ difference is zero. This proves uniqueness first on Schwartz space and then, by density and unitarity, on $L^2$. The constructed propagator has covariance $U(t)^*W(z)U(t)=W(S(t)^Tz)$ by the finite product covariance; every normalized propagator agrees with this one by uniqueness. This also proves existence and covariance rather than assuming a time-slicing limit. [F1, F4, step 1.1, step 1.2, construct, algebra]

2.1 Write $K=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b^T&d\end{pmatrix}$ with $a,d$ real symmetric, and $S=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{pmatrix}$. Set $F=A+iB$ and $G=C+iD$. The symplectic block relations give $F^*G-G^*F=2iI$ and $F^TG=G^TF$. If $Fv=0$, multiplying the first identity by $v^*$ and $v$ forces $\|v\|^2=0$; thus $F$ is invertible at every time, even at a singular generating chart. Consequently $Z:=GF^{-1}$ is symmetric and $\operatorname{Im}Z=(Z-Z^*)/(2i)=F^{-*}F^{-1}>0$. The classical equation gives $\dot F=(b^T+dZ)F$ and $\dot Z=-a-bZ-Zb^T-ZdZ$, with $F(0)=I$ and $Z(0)=iI$. [F1, step 1.3, algebra]

2.2 Let $c(0)=1$ and $\dot c/c=-\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(b+dZ)$, defining $c$ by the exponential of the integral of this continuous, piecewise-smooth coefficient. It never vanishes. Define $g(t,x)=\pi^{-r/4}c(t)\exp(ix^TZ(t)x/2)$. Positive definiteness of $\operatorname{Im}Z$ makes $g$ Schwartz, smoothly in time on each piece; compact time sets have a uniform positive lower eigenvalue, so all required Schwartz and $L^2$ differentiations are justified by polynomial times Gaussian bounds. In these dimensionless coordinates $H_K/\hbar=\tfrac12(x^Tax+x^Tbp+p^Tb^Tx+p^Tdp)$. Applying this differential expression to $g$ gives $H_Kg/\hbar=\bigl(x^T(a+bZ+Zb^T+ZdZ)x/2-i\operatorname{tr}(b+dZ)/2\bigr)g$. Steps 2.1 and the equation for $c$ identify this with $i\partial_tg$. As $g(0)=g_0:=\pi^{-r/4}e^{-|x|^2/2}$, the uniqueness in step 1.3 gives $U(t)g_0=g(t)$. No physical-coordinate factor of $\hbar$ is missing: both the Schrödinger equation and the quadratic contain that common factor. [F1, step 1.3, step 2.1, construct, algebra]

2.3 Differentiating the determinant by its finite polynomial formula gives $(\det F)'=(\det F)\operatorname{tr}(F^{-1}\dot F)$. Since the trace of $b^T$ equals the trace of $b$, steps 2.1 and 2.2 give $(c^2\det F)'=0$. The initial value is one, hence $c(t)^2\det(A(t)+iB(t))=1$. This identity holds through all subdivision joins by continuity. It uses neither a choice of a global square root nor a real generating-chart denominator. [step 2.1, step 2.2, algebra]

3.1 For the two endpoint propagators of the Statement, step 1.3 supplies the same Weyl covariance at their common $S$. By [F3], $U_2=\lambda U_1$ with $|\lambda|=1$. Step 2.1 gives the same endpoint $Z$ for both Gaussian vectors, and step 2.3 gives $c_2^2=c_1^2=1/\det(A+iB)$. Since $c_1\ne0$, $\lambda=c_2/c_1$ and $\lambda^2=1$, so $U_2=\pm U_1$. This proves the required invertible-$B$ claim and in fact the same phase conclusion at every endpoint. Multiplying both unitaries by any fixed normalized quadratic propagator preserves their relative sign, proving the final clause. [F3, step 1.3, step 2.1, step 2.3, algebra] ∎

## Remarks

The historical title records the phase-parity application. The proof establishes the stated sign ambiguity by an ordinary finite Gaussian differential equation; it imports no Maslov-index, path-integral convergence, or metaplectic-cover theorem. The cited literature remains attribution for the established statement. No new full-text source reading or external proof acceptance is asserted by this local reconstruction.
