## Definition

Specialize the finite-mode assignment to $M=1$, identify the coordinate vector $(z)\in\mathbb C^1$ with its single component $z\in\mathbb C$, and write $\mathcal F_s(\mathbb C)$ for this one-mode Fock space. Write $|n\rangle$ for the occupation vector with $n$ quanta in its unique mode. Let $\rho$ be a density operator (a positive trace-class operator of trace one) on $\mathcal F_s(\mathbb C)$. Let $\Pi_n:=|n\rangle\langle n|$ be the ideal number-count PVM, with probabilities $p_n:=\operatorname{tr}(\rho\Pi_n)$. Suppose $\bar n:=\sum_{n\ge0}np_n$ is finite and strictly positive, and $m_2:=\sum_{n\ge0}n(n-1)p_n$ is finite. Define the model's equal-time second factorial statistic by $g^{(2)}(0;\rho):=m_2/\bar n^2$. On finite-particle number vectors the one-mode maps satisfy $a|0\rangle=0$, $a|n\rangle=\sqrt n|n-1\rangle$ for $n\ge1$, and $a^*|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle$ for $n\ge0$. Normal ordering here means placing creation maps to the left of annihilation maps; these actions give $a^*a^*aa|n\rangle=n(n-1)|n\rangle$ for $n\ge0$. Thus $m_2$ is the corresponding number-basis factorial moment, defined here directly by its probability sum rather than by an unqualified expectation of an unbounded operator product. This uses only the ideal counting model; it asserts no time-resolved photocurrent law, empirical detection, or detector realization.

## Example

Let $\mathfrak g=\mathbb R t$ be the one-dimensional Abelian real Lie algebra and take $V_+=V_-=\mathbb C$. For $q\in\mathbb R$, define complex-linear representations by $\rho_\pm(zt)=\pm qz\,\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb C}$ for $z\in\mathbb C$, and attach the left-handed bookkeeping label to both. This opposite-charge pair is called vectorlike at the representation-data level here. It does not construct spinor fields or a charge-conjugation map; an interpretation as a field pair requires a separately specified field model and convention. The coefficient sums satisfy $q^3+(-q)^3=0$ and $q+(-q)=0$. With the no-half anticommutator convention $\{A,B\}=AB+BA$, the cubic coefficient is $d=2(q^3+(-q)^3)=0$, and the separate linear representation coefficient is $\ell=q+(-q)=0$. This verifies cancellation of these two algebraic coefficients in that interface only; it does not identify them with a regulated anomaly, prove an all-orders theorem, or establish a nonperturbative gauge theory or cancellation of global anomalies.

## Statement

Let $\rho>0$ be a dimensionless scale and put $t=\log\rho\in\mathbb R$. Take $I\subseteq\mathbb R$ to be an interval containing $0$. Let $g:I\to\mathbb R$ be a differentiable real coupling and let $b,g_0\in\mathbb R$ be dimensionless constants; require $g$ to satisfy the truncated scalar initial-value problem
$$\frac{dg}{dt}=bg^2,\qquad g(0)=g_0.$$
A solution through $(0,g_0)$ is **maximal** if there is no strictly larger interval $J\supsetneq I$ and solution $G:J\to\mathbb R$ of the same initial-value problem with $G|_I=g$. If $c:=bg_0=0$, the unique maximal real solution is $g(t)=g_0$ on $\mathbb R$. If $c\ne0$, the unique maximal solution through $t=0$ is
$$g(t)=\frac{g_0}{1-bg_0t}$$
on $(-\infty,c^{-1})$ when $c>0$ and on $(c^{-1},\infty)$ when $c<0$.

For the formal beta-series calculation, let $u$ be an indeterminate and work in $\mathbb R[ [u] ]$. Write $\beta(u)=bu^2+O(u^3)$ and let $\Phi(u)=u+au^2+O(u^3)$ be an invertible formal change of dimensionless coupling, with dimensionless $a\in\mathbb R$. The notation $O(u^k)$ means an element of $u^k\mathbb R[ [u] ]$, so its terms have degree at least $k$; the real trajectory $g(t)$ is distinct from the formal indeterminate $u$. Put $\widetilde u=\Phi(u)$, let $\Phi'$ be the termwise formal derivative, and define the transformed formal vector field in $\mathbb R[ [\widetilde u] ]$ by
$$\widetilde\beta(\widetilde u)=\bigl(\Phi'(u)\beta(u)\bigr)\big|_{u=\Phi^{-1}(\widetilde u)}.$$
The quadratic coefficient of $\widetilde\beta$ is $b$. The real ODE is exact only for the displayed truncation; neither it nor the formal scheme-change statement determines $b$ from a QFT graph or bounds omitted higher-order terms. Its pole is a property of this truncated equation, not a theorem about an ultraviolet completion.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** $b,g_0\in\mathbb R$, $c=bg_0$, dimensionless $\rho>0$ with $t=\log\rho$, and the formal change $\Phi(u)=u+au^2+O(u^3)$ in $\mathbb R[ [u] ]$.

[A1] The vector field is the prescribed truncation $F(t,g)=bg^2$ on $D=\mathbb R^2$; no higher-order beta coefficients or measured coupling are specified.

[A2] The formal beta transformation is defined in $\mathbb R[ [u] ]$ by push-forward under the invertible formal series $\Phi$; its termwise derivatives and substitutions are coefficientwise, and no formal series is assumed to converge.

[F1] The Statement takes $\rho>0$ dimensionless and $t=\log\rho$, and separates the real trajectory $g(t)$ from the formal indeterminate $u\in\mathbb R[ [u] ]$. The scalar coefficient $b$ is prescribed independently; no renormalization cocycle or QFT coefficient calculation is assumed.

[F2] [[def-first-order-ode-initial-value-problem-and-solution]]: a solution on an interval is a differentiable real function satisfying the stated equation and initial condition.

[F3] [[def-locally-lipschitz-in-the-state-variable]]: a vector field is locally Lipschitz in the state variable when one finite Lipschitz constant works on each compact time-state cylinder.

[F4] [[thm-picard-lindelof-local-existence-and-uniqueness]]: a continuous vector field locally Lipschitz in the state variable has a unique local solution, and two solutions with the same initial data agree on every common interval containing the initial time.

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct verification of the rational solution, ODE uniqueness, and formal re-expansion.

**Given:** [A1]–[A2] and [F1]–[F4].

1.1 Under [A1], [F1] fixes $t=\log\rho$ as the dimensionless scale coordinate and the vector field is the prescribed $F(t,g)=bg^2$. If $c=0$, the constant function $g(t)=g_0$ has derivative zero and $bg_0^2=0$, so it solves the IVP on all of $\mathbb R$. If $c\ne0$, set $\gamma(t)=g_0/(1-ct)$ on the interval specified in the Statement. For every interior $t$,
$$\frac{\gamma(t+h)-\gamma(t)}{h}=\frac{g_0c}{(1-ct)(1-c(t+h))}\longrightarrow\frac{g_0c}{(1-ct)^2}=b\gamma(t)^2,$$
and $\gamma(0)=g_0$, so it solves the IVP. [A1, F1, F2, algebra]

2.1 The vector field $F(t,x)=bx^2$ is continuous on the open domain $D=\mathbb R^2$. On any compact time-state cylinder with $|x|,|y|\le R$,
$$|F(t,x)-F(t,y)|=|b||x-y||x+y|\le2|b|R|x-y|,$$
so it is locally Lipschitz in the state variable by [F3]. Uniqueness from [F4] shows that every solution through $(0,g_0)$ agrees with step 1.1 on its common interval. If $c\ne0$, any strictly larger interval containing the stated interval must contain $c^{-1}$. A solution $G$ on such an interval would be continuous at $c^{-1}$, while uniqueness forces $G=\gamma$ on the original interval and $|\gamma(t)|\to\infty$ as $t$ approaches $c^{-1}$ from within it, a contradiction. When $c=0$, the constant solution already exists on all of $\mathbb R$. These facts establish the stated maximal intervals. [A1, F2, F3, F4, step 1.1]

3.1 Termwise differentiation in $\mathbb R[ [u] ]$ gives $\Phi'(u)=1+2au+O(u^2)$. Since $\Phi$ has linear term $u$, recursive coefficient matching gives $\Phi^{-1}(\widetilde u)=\widetilde u+O(\widetilde u^2)$, hence $u^2=\widetilde u^2+O(\widetilde u^3)$. Therefore $\Phi'(u)\beta(u)=bu^2+O(u^3)$ and re-expression in $\widetilde u$ yields $\widetilde\beta(\widetilde u)=b\widetilde u^2+O(\widetilde u^3)$. The coefficient $b$ is unchanged; no coefficient at higher order is determined here. [A2, algebra] ∎

## Example

Let $\theta(\mathbf k)=\arccos(k_z/|\mathbf k|)\in[0,\pi]$. For $\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}$, $\theta_0\in[0,\pi]$, and $0<\delta\le\pi$, a normalized helicity-$\sigma$ packet of angular width $\delta$ is a vector $f\in\mathcal H_T^\nu$ with $\|f\|_\nu=1$, $P_\sigma f=f$ almost everywhere, and $f(\mathbf k)=0$ almost everywhere unless $|\theta(\mathbf k)-\theta_0|\le\delta$. Such packets exist for every $\theta_0$ and $\delta$. Each satisfies
$$\left|\langle f,S_z'f\rangle_\nu-\hbar\sigma\cos\theta_0\right|\le\hbar\delta.$$
Consequently, for any sequence $0<\delta_m\le\pi$ with $\delta_m\downarrow0$ and normalized helicity-$\sigma$ packets $f_m$ of widths $\delta_m$, $\langle f_m,S_z'f_m\rangle_\nu\to\hbar\sigma\cos\theta_0$. The exact cone $C_{\theta_0}:=\{\mathbf k\in M:\theta(\mathbf k)=\theta_0\}$ is $\nu$-null. Therefore every $g\in\mathcal H_T^\nu$ that vanishes $\nu$-almost everywhere on $M\setminus C_{\theta_0}$ is the zero element, and no such $g$ can be normalized.

## Facts & Assumptions

## Verification

**Given:** [A1], [F1]–[F8], $\theta_0\in[0,\pi]$, $0<\delta\le\pi$, and $\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}$.

1.1 By [F7], full Choice supplies Countable Choice to the bundle definition [F1]. The set $\{\mathbf n\in S^2:|\theta(\mathbf n)-\theta_0|<\delta\}$ is a nonempty open angular neighborhood of a direction with polar angle $\theta_0$. Choose a smaller coordinate ball whose closure lies in that neighborhood and in a trivializing patch for the helicity line from [F1]. Let $u_\sigma$ be its smooth unit section, choose a nonzero smooth bump $\chi$ supported in the ball by [F6], and choose a nonzero smooth radial bump $\eta$ supported in $1<r<2$. The field $\widetilde f(\mathbf k)=\eta(r)\chi(\mathbf n(\mathbf k))u_\sigma(\mathbf n(\mathbf k))$, extended by zero outside the patch, is smooth, compactly supported away from $\mathbf k=0$, transverse, and satisfies $P_\sigma\widetilde f=\widetilde f$. It is nonzero on an open set of positive $d\nu$ measure by [F4], so $0<\|\widetilde f\|_\nu<\infty$; normalizing it gives a packet of width $\delta$. Repeating for each $\delta_m$ with $0<\delta_m\le\pi$ proves the asserted existence of sequences. [A1, F1, F4, F6, F7, given, construct]

1.2 Under the full-Choice hypothesis [A1], the Fock supplier [F2] gives the projected-spin expectation formula; with $\|f\|_\nu=1$ it yields $\left|\langle f,S_z'f\rangle_\nu-\hbar\sigma\cos\theta_0\right|=\hbar\left|\int_M(\cos\theta(\mathbf k)-\cos\theta_0)|f(\mathbf k)|^2\,d\nu(\mathbf k)\right|$. On the support of $f$, [F3] bounds $|\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0|\le|\theta-\theta_0|\le\delta$. Taking the absolute value inside the integral and using normalization gives the claimed bound $\hbar\delta$. [A1, F2, F3, given, algebra]

2.1 For any sequence $0<\delta_m\le\pi$ with $\delta_m\downarrow0$, step 1.2 gives $|\langle f_m,S_z'f_m\rangle_\nu-\hbar\sigma\cos\theta_0|\le\hbar\delta_m\to0$, so the expectations converge to $\hbar\sigma\cos\theta_0$. [step 1.2, algebra]

3.1 Put $E_{\theta_0}=\{\mathbf n\in S^2:\theta(\mathbf n)=\theta_0\}$. If $0<\theta_0<\pi$, cover this latitude by two overlapping azimuth-coordinate charts. In each chart its coordinate preimage lies in a bounded rectangle $\{\theta_0\}\times[a,b]$, which is Lebesgue null by [F8]; the chart-area formula $\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$ in [F4] therefore gives zero surface measure. If $\theta_0=0$ or $\pi$, $E_{\theta_0}$ is a single pole. A smooth regular sphere chart centered at that pole has a singleton preimage, null in $\mathbb R^2$ by [F8]; integrating the chart area density over that singleton again gives zero surface measure by the chart-measure identification in [F4]. Thus $E_{\theta_0}$ is surface-null for every stated $\theta_0$. For each integer $m\ge2$, the annulus $1/m\le r\le m$ has finite $\nu$-measure by [F1] and [F4], so the cone indicator restricted to it is integrable. The polar decomposition in [F4] and Fubini [F5] give
$$\nu\bigl(C_{\theta_0}\cap\{1/m\le r\le m\}\bigr)=\int_{1/m}^{m}\frac r2\,dr\int_{S^2}\mathbf1_{E_{\theta_0}}\,d\Omega=0.$$
These annuli cover $M$, so countable subadditivity gives $\nu(C_{\theta_0})=0$. If $g\in\mathcal H_T^\nu$ vanishes $\nu$-almost everywhere on $M\setminus C_{\theta_0}$, its squared norm is the sum of the integrals of $|g|^2$ over that complement and the null cone, both zero. Hence $\|g\|_\nu=0$ and $g$ is the zero Hilbert-space element; in particular it cannot be normalized. This is the exact null-cone conclusion in the Example. [F1, F4, F5, F7, F8, given, algebra] ∎

## Statement

Let $p:\mathcal X\to\mathcal Y$ and $q:\mathcal Y\to\mathcal Z$ be functors of finite groupoids. The iterated homotopy fiber over any $z\in\mathcal Z$ is equivalent to the homotopy fiber of $q\circ p$, so $(q\circ p)_!=q_!p_!$. Consequently, for composable spans of finite groupoids, the induced push-pull maps compose by the homotopy-pullback composite span. If $\mathcal X\xleftarrow{a}\mathcal E\xrightarrow{b}\mathcal Y$ is a span, equip invariant functions with

$$\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal X}:=\sum_{[x]\in\pi_0(\mathcal X)} \frac{\overline{f([x])}\,g([x])}{|\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal X}(x)|}.$$

Then the adjoint of $b_!a^*$ is $a_!b^*$, the map of the transposed span.

## Statement references

[[def-qftx-finite-groupoid-integration]]

[[def-qftx-finite-homotopy-pullback]]

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Finite groupoids and functors as in the Statement, and invariant complex-valued functions on their isomorphism classes.

[F1] [[def-qftx-finite-groupoid-integration]]: homotopy-fiber objects are pairs $(c,\alpha:p(c)\cong d)$; integration weights each class by the reciprocal automorphism-group order; a span acts by pushforward after pullback.

[F2] [[def-qftx-finite-homotopy-pullback]]: the homotopy pullback of $b:\mathcal E\to\mathcal Y$ and $c:\mathcal F\to\mathcal Y$ has objects $(e,f,\alpha:b(e)\cong c(f))$ and compatible pairs of arrows.

## Proof

1.1 For fixed $z\in\mathcal Z$, an object of the iterated fiber is $(y,\beta:q(y)\to z,x,\alpha:p(x)\to y)$. The functor $(y,\beta,x,\alpha)\mapsto(x,\beta\circ q(\alpha))$ maps it to $(\mathcal X)_z$. A quasi-inverse sends $(x,\gamma:q(p(x))\to z)$ to $(p(x),\gamma,x,\operatorname{id}_{p(x)})$. The composite on $(\mathcal X)_z$ is the identity. For any iterated object, the isomorphism $\alpha:p(x)\to y$ and $\operatorname{id}_x$ give a natural isomorphism from the quasi-inverse image back to that object. Hence the functor is an equivalence. [given, F1, construct]

1.2 Fix an isomorphism class $[e]$ of $\mathcal E$, set $y=b(e)$, and write $A=\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal Y}(y)$, $B=\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal E}(e)$, $I=\operatorname{im}(B\to A)$, and $N=\ker(B\to A)$. The classes in the homotopy fiber $\mathcal E_y$ represented by this $[e]$ are the orbits of $I$ on the $A$-torsor of isomorphisms $b(e)\cong y$, so there are $|A|/|I|$ of them; each has automorphism group $N$. Their contribution after the outer factor $1/|A|$ is $f([e])/(|I||N|)=f([e])/|B|$, by the finite homomorphism counting identity $|B|=|I||N|$. Summing over $[e]$ proves $\int_{y\in\mathcal Y} b_!f(y)=\int_{e\in\mathcal E} f(e)$. [given, F1, algebra]

2.1 Compose spans $\mathcal X\xleftarrow{a}\mathcal E\xrightarrow{b}\mathcal Y$ and $\mathcal Y\xleftarrow{c}\mathcal F\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal Z$, and let $P=\mathcal E\times^h_{\mathcal Y}\mathcal F$ with projections $\pi_E,\pi_F$. For fixed $z\in\mathcal F$, the homotopy fiber of $\pi_F$ over $z$ has objects $((e,f',\alpha),\tau:f'\cong z)$. The functor to $\mathcal E_{c(z)}$ sends this object to $(e,c(\tau)\circ\alpha)$ and sends an arrow $(u,v)$ to $u$. Such an arrow satisfies $\tau'\circ v=\tau$ and $c(v)\circ\alpha=\alpha'\circ b(u)$, so its image satisfies $\beta'\circ b(u)=\beta$. Conversely, for any such $u$ between images $(e,\beta)$ and $(e',\beta')$, the only possible lift has $v=(\tau')^{-1}\circ\tau:f'\to f''$; the equation $\beta'\circ b(u)=\beta$ implies $c(v)\circ\alpha=\alpha'\circ b(u)$, so this lift exists and is unique. The functor is fully faithful; every object $(e,\beta)$ is its image from $((e,z,\beta),\operatorname{id}_z)$, so it is essentially surjective. It is therefore an equivalence. By [F1], equivalent fibers have equal finite integrals, giving $c^*b_!g=(\pi_F)_!\pi_E^*g$ for every invariant function $g$ on $\mathcal E$. Applying step 1.1 to $\pi_F$ and $d$ now gives $d_!c^*b_!a^*f=(d\circ\pi_F)_!(a\circ\pi_E)^*f$, which is the push-pull map of the homotopy-pullback composite span. [F1, F2, step 1.1, construct]

2.2 Put $\phi([e]):=\overline{f([a(e)])}g([b(e)])$, which is invariant on $\pi_0(\mathcal E)$. Expanding the left inner product by the definition of $b_!$ gives $\langle b_!a^*f,g\rangle_{\mathcal Y}=\sum_{[y]}\frac{1}{|\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal Y}(y)|}\sum_{[(e,\eta)]\in\pi_0(\mathcal E_y)}\frac{\overline{f([a(e)])}g([y])}{|\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal E_y}(e,\eta)|}$. Since $\eta:b(e)\cong y$ and $g$ is invariant, $g([y])=g([b(e)])$, so this is $\int_{y\in\mathcal Y}b_!\phi(y)=\int_{e\in\mathcal E}\phi(e)=\sum_{[e]}\frac{\phi([e])}{|\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal E}(e)|}$ by step 1.2. On the other side, expanding $a_!b^*g$ and using $\xi:a(e)\cong x$ to replace $\overline{f([x])}$ by $\overline{f([a(e)])}$ gives $\langle f,a_!b^*g\rangle_{\mathcal X}=\sum_{[x]}\frac{1}{|\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal X}(x)|}\sum_{[(e,\xi)]\in\pi_0(\mathcal E_x)}\frac{\phi([e])}{|\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal E_x}(e,\xi)|}=\int_{x\in\mathcal X}a_!\phi(x)=\int_{e\in\mathcal E}\phi(e)$, again by step 1.2. Both inner products therefore equal the same finite weighted sum, proving the adjoint identity. [F1, step 1.2, algebra]

3.1 Step 2.2 proves that the adjoint of $b_!a^*$ is $a_!b^*$, and steps 1.1 and 2.1 prove composition through homotopy-pullback spans. [step 1.1, step 2.1, step 2.2] ∎
