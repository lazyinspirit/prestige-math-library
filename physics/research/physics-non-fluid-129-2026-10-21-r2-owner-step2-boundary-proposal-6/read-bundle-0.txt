## Definition

Assume the surface-measure convention with $\mathrm{AC}_\omega$ of the mathematical definition of the stationary surface distribution. Let $S\subset\mathbb R^3$ be a compact embedded $C^2$ hypersurface without manifold boundary, with a chosen continuous unit normal $n$ and its finite surface measure $dS$. At $x\in S$, define the tangent plane

$$T_xS:=\{v\in\mathbb R^3:v\cdot n(x)=0\}.$$

For $\phi\in C^1(S)$, meaning that its composition with each local $C^2$ surface chart is $C^1$, its surface gradient $\nabla_S\phi(x)\in T_xS$ is the unique vector satisfying

$$v\cdot\nabla_S\phi(x)=d\phi_x(v)\qquad\text{for every }v\in T_xS,$$

where $d\phi_x$ is the derivative of $\phi$ in tangent directions. For a measurable tangential field $K:S\to\mathbb R^3$ with $\int_S|K|\,dS<\infty$, define its **weak surface divergence** $\operatorname{Div}_S K$ as the linear functional on $C^1(S)$

$$\langle\operatorname{Div}_S K,\phi\rangle:=-\int_S K\cdot\nabla_S\phi\,dS.$$

This functional is well-defined because $S$ is compact, $\nabla_S\phi$ is continuous and bounded, and $K$ is integrable. If it has a continuous density $g:S\to\mathbb R$, meaning

$$\langle\operatorname{Div}_S K,\phi\rangle=\int_S g\phi\,dS\qquad\text{for every }\phi\in C^1(S),$$

then $g$ is called a continuous representative of the weak surface divergence. The definition itself does not assert that such a representative exists or is unique; a result using pointwise $\operatorname{div}_S K$ must establish both.

## Statement

Use the finite Lorentz oscillator closure in its homogeneous material rest frame. Let $N\ge1$ be finite, with $\varepsilon_\infty>0$ in F m$^{-1}$, $a_\ell>0$ in F m$^{-1}$ s$^{-2}$, $\omega_\ell>0$ in s$^{-1}$ and $\gamma_\ell\ge0$ in s$^{-1}$ as in that model. Assume Countable Choice; for the strictly damped integrable-kernel dispersion clause also assume Dependent Choice, as required by the causal-response supplier. For the time convention $e^{-i\omega t}$, let $z$ denote complex angular frequency in s$^{-1}$ and define the absolute oscillator susceptibility, with units F m$^{-1}$, by

$$\chi_{\mathrm{abs}}(z):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{a_\ell}{\omega_\ell^2-z^2-i\gamma_\ell z},\qquad \varepsilon(z):=\varepsilon_\infty+\chi_{\mathrm{abs}}(z).$$

The dimensionless transfer function in the causal-response theorem is $\chi_{\mathrm{norm}}(z):=\chi_{\mathrm{abs}}(z)/\varepsilon_0$, so that it has the theorem's convention $P=\varepsilon_0\chi_{\mathrm{norm}}E$ for harmonic histories.

The function $\chi_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is analytic for $\operatorname{Im}z>0$ for every $\gamma_\ell\ge0$. For real angular frequency $\omega>0$ in s$^{-1}$ away from undamped poles, $\chi_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega)$ is the scalar multiplier for a harmonic particular solution: if $E(t)=\operatorname{Re}(e e^{-i\omega t})$ and $P_\ell(t)=\operatorname{Re}(p_\ell e^{-i\omega t})$, then $p_\ell=a_\ell e/(\omega_\ell^2-\omega^2-i\gamma_\ell\omega)$ and $p=\sum_\ell p_\ell=\chi_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega)e$. This coefficient calculation is not by itself a zero-past response to an eternal harmonic input. In the undamped branch the retarded onset solution is specified by initial data and the real poles are excluded; when every $\gamma_\ell>0$, the separately stated integrable retarded kernels realize the same harmonic multiplier.

$$\operatorname{Im}\varepsilon(\omega)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{a_\ell\gamma_\ell\omega}{(\omega_\ell^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma_\ell^2\omega^2}\ge0.$$

When every $\gamma_\ell>0$, each causal impulse-response kernel is exponentially decaying, satisfies the integrable-kernel hypotheses of the causal-response theorem, and $\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\infty$ obeys its unsubtracted Hilbert-transform relations. When $\gamma_\ell=0$, that oscillator's poles lie at $z=\pm\omega_\ell$ on the real boundary; the transfer function has no pointwise value at those poles, and no pointwise dispersion formula there is asserted. The theorem makes no universal group-velocity bound.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The finite homogeneous Lorentz oscillator model with real parameters $\varepsilon_\infty,a_\ell,\omega_\ell>0$, $\gamma_\ell\ge0$, real electric fields, and the temporal Fourier convention $e^{-i\omega t}$. For the Hilbert-transform conclusion, also assume the Countable and Dependent Choice hypotheses carried by the integrable branch of the causal-response supplier.

[A1] At each material point, the polarization solves $P_{\ell,tt}+\gamma_\ell P_{\ell,t}+\omega_\ell^2P_\ell=a_\ell E$ with initial oscillator state specified; the medium is homogeneous and spatially local, and the time-domain model is adopted, not derived ([[post-emx-lorentz-medium]]).

[L1] For the declared memory branch, an integrable real causal kernel $K$ gives normalized response $P(t)=\varepsilon_0\int_0^\infty K(s)E(t-s)\,ds$; when $K$ is locally absolutely continuous with $K,K'\in L^1$ and $sK\in L^1$, the transfer $\chi(z)=\int_0^\infty K(s)e^{izs}ds$ is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Im}z>0$, has the stated real-boundary regularity, and obeys the unsubtracted principal-value dispersion relations ([[pthm-em-causal-dispersive-response]], clauses (a)–(b)).

[F1] Countable Choice is assumed for the smooth comparison route, and Dependent Choice is assumed in the integrable-kernel branch used for the causal convolution and dispersion result ([[def-countable-choice]], [[def-dependent-choice]], [[pthm-em-causal-dispersive-response]]).

[F2] The real exponential has derivative $\exp'=\exp$, the complex exponential is defined by its series and is entire with the same derivative, sine and cosine have their usual derivatives, products satisfy the product rule, and integration by parts applies componentwise on finite intervals to the oscillator kernels and $C^2$ histories used below ([[def-complex-exponential]], [[thm-derivative-of-exponential]], [[thm-complex-exponential-is-entire-with-derivative-itself]], [[thm-sine-and-cosine-derivatives]], [[thm-algebra-of-derivatives]], [[thm-integration-by-parts]]).

[F3] Every nonnegative real has its unique nonnegative square root; for $x\to+\infty$ and $\lambda>0$, $x/\exp(\lambda x)\to0$ ([[thm-of-square-roots]], [[thm-exponential-beats-every-polynomial]]).

[F4] If an integrand is differentiable in a parameter and its derivative has a common integrable majorant, differentiation passes through the integral ([[thm-differentiation-under-the-integral-sign]]).

## Proof
**Proof technique:** solve the harmonic oscillator algebraically, locate its characteristic roots, construct its retarded Green kernel under positive damping, and apply the exact integrable causal-response theorem only in that branch.

1.1 For a real harmonic history $E(t)=\operatorname{Re}(e e^{-i\omega t})$, complexify the real linear ODE [A1] by extending its real coefficients complex-linearly and use the ansatz $\widetilde P_\ell(t)=p_\ell e^{-i\omega t}$. By the derivative of the complex exponential and the chain rule, one and two time derivatives multiply the amplitude by $-i\omega$ and $-\omega^2$, respectively, so the complex amplitude equation is $(\omega_\ell^2-\omega^2-i\gamma_\ell\omega)p_\ell=a_\ell e$. Its real part is a real harmonic particular solution of [A1]. Wherever this scalar denominator is nonzero, the amplitude is uniquely $p_\ell=\frac{a_\ell}{\omega_\ell^2-\omega^2-i\gamma_\ell\omega}e$, including when $e=0$. Summing gives the stated harmonic particular-solution multiplier without dividing by the vector amplitude. This does not identify the undamped coefficient with a retarded zero-past response. [given, A1, F2, algebra]

1.2 The roots of $\omega_\ell^2-z^2-i\gamma_\ell z=0$ are $z=-i\gamma_\ell/2\pm\sqrt{\omega_\ell^2-\gamma_\ell^2/4}$. If $0\le\gamma_\ell<2\omega_\ell$, the square root is real and both roots have imaginary part $-\gamma_\ell/2\le0$; at $\gamma_\ell=0$ they are the real values $\pm\omega_\ell$. If $\gamma_\ell=2\omega_\ell$, the repeated root is $-i\omega_\ell$. If $\gamma_\ell>2\omega_\ell$, write $\beta=\sqrt{\gamma_\ell^2/4-\omega_\ell^2}$; then $0<\beta<\gamma_\ell/2$ and the roots $-i(\gamma_\ell/2\mp\beta)$ both have strictly negative imaginary part. Hence no denominator vanishes in the open upper half-plane, proving analyticity there; the undamped real poles are exactly the stated boundary exceptions. [given, A1, F3, algebra]

1.3 Fix one oscillator, denote its parameters by $a,\omega_0,\gamma$, assume $\gamma>0$, and set $g(t)=0$ for $t<0$. For $t\ge0$, define the retarded kernel by $g(t)=e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin(\alpha t)/\alpha$ if $0<\gamma<2\omega_0$, $g(t)=te^{-\omega_0t}$ if $\gamma=2\omega_0$, and $g(t)=\bigl(e^{-(\gamma/2-\beta)t}-e^{-(\gamma/2+\beta)t}\bigr)/(2\beta)$ if $\gamma>2\omega_0$, where $\alpha=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4}$ and $\beta=\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2}$ in their respective cases. Differentiating with [F2] gives $g(0)=0$, $g'(0+)=1$ and $g''+\gamma g'+\omega_0^2g=0$ for $t>0$. The underdamped $g,g'$ decay at rate $\gamma/2$; the critical formula and derivative are bounded by $Ce^{-\omega_0t/2}$; the overdamped formula and derivative are bounded by $Ce^{-(\gamma/2-\beta)t}$, with $\gamma/2-\beta>0$. Hence $g,g'\in L^1(0,\infty)$. For each positive decay rate $\lambda$, $te^{-\lambda t}$ is integrable by integration by parts on $[0,T]$ and [F3] as $T\to\infty$, so also $tg\in L^1(0,\infty)$. [given, A1, F2, F3, algebra]

2.1 For each real vector history $E\in C^2(\mathbb R;\mathbb R^3)$ with $E,E',E''$ bounded, set $P(t)=a\int_0^\infty g(s)E(t-s)\,ds$. Apply [F4] componentwise: the derivative integrands are measurable and bounded, uniformly in $t$, by $\|E'\|_\infty|g|$ and $\|E''\|_\infty|g|$, which are integrable by step 1.3. Thus $P_{tt}+\gamma P_t+\omega_0^2P=a\int_0^\infty[g(s)E''(t-s)+\gamma g(s)E'(t-s)+\omega_0^2g(s)E(t-s)]\,ds$. [F4, step 1.3, algebra]

2.2 For real $\omega>0$, put $A_\ell=\omega_\ell^2-\omega^2$ and $B_\ell=\gamma_\ell\omega$. Away from an undamped pole, $A_\ell-iB_\ell\ne0$ and $\frac{a_\ell}{A_\ell-iB_\ell}=\frac{a_\ell(A_\ell+iB_\ell)}{A_\ell^2+B_\ell^2}$, so $\operatorname{Im}(a_\ell/(A_\ell-iB_\ell))=a_\ell\gamma_\ell\omega/(A_\ell^2+\gamma_\ell^2\omega^2)\ge0$. Since $\varepsilon_\infty$ is real, summing these expressions proves the displayed nonnegative imaginary part of $\varepsilon$. At $\gamma_\ell=0$ and $\omega=\omega_\ell$ the denominator vanishes, so this pointwise computation and pointwise formula do not apply. [given, A1, step 1.1, algebra]

3.1 Integrating by parts twice on $[0,T]$ and taking $T\to\infty$ is valid because $g,g'$ decay by step 1.3, $g''=-\gamma g'-\omega_0^2g\in L^1$, and $E,E'$ are bounded. The boundary values $g(0)=0$, $g'(0+)=1$ give $\int_0^\infty g(s)E''(t-s)\,ds=E(t)+\int_0^\infty g''(s)E(t-s)\,ds$ and $\int_0^\infty g(s)E'(t-s)\,ds=\int_0^\infty g'(s)E(t-s)\,ds$. Substitution in step 2.1 cancels the integral by $g''+\gamma g'+\omega_0^2g=0$, leaving $P_{tt}+\gamma P_t+\omega_0^2P=aE$. [step 1.3, step 2.1, F2, algebra]

4.1 The normalized kernel $K=(a/\varepsilon_0)g$ is real, causal and locally absolutely continuous; step 1.3 gives $K,K'\in L^1$ and $sK\in L^1$. For $\operatorname{Im}z>0$, exponential decay makes the boundary terms vanish in integration by parts, so $\int_0^\infty g'(s)e^{izs}ds=-izG(z)$ and $\int_0^\infty g''(s)e^{izs}ds=-1-z^2G(z)$, where $G(z)=\int_0^\infty g(s)e^{izs}ds$. The Green equation gives $(\omega_0^2-z^2-i\gamma z)G(z)=1$, hence the normalized transfer is $(a/\varepsilon_0)/(\omega_0^2-z^2-i\gamma z)$. Summing the finite kernels gives $\chi_{\mathrm{norm}}=\chi_{\mathrm{abs}}/\varepsilon_0$; with [F1], this kernel satisfies every hypothesis of [L1], which yields causality, upper-half-plane analyticity and the unsubtracted Hilbert relations for the strictly damped branch. Multiplication by $\varepsilon_0$ gives the same relations for $\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\infty=\chi_{\mathrm{abs}}$. [A1, step 1.3, step 3.1, F1, L1, algebra]

5.1 Steps 1.1–4.1 establish the rational susceptibility, its upper-half-plane analyticity, the nonnegative loss for every real positive frequency where the pointwise response exists, and the unsubtracted dispersion relation exactly in the strictly damped integrable-kernel branch. The undamped poles and their pointwise exclusions remain as stated; no assertion about a universal group-velocity bound follows from these calculations. [step 1.1, step 1.2, step 2.2, step 4.1] ∎

## Statement

Assume Countable Choice. Let $a,c,\mu_0>0$, let $\varepsilon_0=(\mu_0c^2)^{-1}$, and let $\rho:\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ and $J:\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^3$ be jointly $C^\infty$. Suppose $\rho(y,t)=J(y,t)=0$ whenever $|y|>a$ and suppose the continuity equation $\partial_t\rho+\operatorname{div}_yJ=0$ holds everywhere.

Adopt the smooth vacuum Maxwell equations and the retarded, no-free-addition selection as the physical model assumptions. In the SI laboratory convention, define

$$\varphi(x,t):=\frac{\mu_0c^2}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb R^3}\frac{\rho(y,t-|x-y|/c)}{|x-y|}\,dy,\qquad A(x,t):=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb R^3}\frac{J(y,t-|x-y|/c)}{|x-y|}\,dy.$$

The integrals converge absolutely at every $(x,t)$. The resulting potentials are jointly $C^\infty$ and satisfy $W\varphi=\rho/\varepsilon_0$, $WA=\mu_0J$, and $\operatorname{div}A+c^{-2}\partial_t\varphi=0$ on $\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R$, where $W=c^{-2}\partial_t^2-\Delta$. Consequently $E=-\nabla\varphi-\partial_tA$ and $B=\operatorname{curl}A$ are jointly smooth and satisfy the smooth vacuum Maxwell equations for $(\rho,J)$ everywhere. This is the selected retarded solution, with no independent homogeneous field added. No compact support in time is assumed.

[F1] The source pair consists of smooth volume density and current maps of the framework; $C^\infty$ means that every iterated coordinate partial derivative exists and is continuous, and finite products of smooth maps are smooth. The source definition does not itself impose conservation. Here conservation and fixed-ball support are explicit hypotheses. ([[def-em-sources]], [[def-ck-euclidean-maps-and-diffeomorphisms]], [[thm-ck-euclidean-maps-closed-under-algebra-and-composition]])

[F2] For every $h\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R)$, $K*h(x,t):=\int h(y,t-|x-y|/c)/(4\pi|x-y|)\,dy$ is absolutely convergent, is jointly $C^\infty$, and satisfies $W(K*h)=h$ pointwise, with $W=c^{-2}\partial_t^2-\Delta$. ([[lem-em-retarded-wave-kernel]] (b))

[F3] For a compact subset $C$ of an open set $O\subseteq\mathbb R$, there is $\chi\in C_c^\infty(O)$ with $0\le\chi\le1$ and $\chi=1$ on a neighborhood of $C$. Closed bounded Euclidean boxes are compact. ([[lem-test-function-cutoffs-and-euclidean-localization]], [[thm-heine-borel-rn]])

[F4] Translation is a $C^1$ change of variables with unit Jacobian; the mean value theorem bounds each scalar component's difference quotient by a derivative supremum. ## Proof

**Proof technique:** localize each arbitrary history to the finite time interval sampled near an observation event, use the compactly supported retarded kernel theorem there, and derive the Lorenz condition from continuity.

1.1 Fix $(x,t)$. The source integrands vanish off $\overline B_a$. On this compact ball the retarded time $t-|x-y|/c$ lies in the bounded interval $[t-(|x|+a)/c,t]$, so smoothness bounds both source functions there by a finite constant $M$. The triangle inequality and [F5] give $\int_{B_a}|h(y,t-|x-y|/c)|/|x-y|\,dy\le M\int_{B(x,|x|+a)}|x-y|^{-1}dy<\infty$ for $h=\rho$ and each component of $J$; the possible point $y=x$ is harmless because $|z|^{-1}$ is locally integrable in three dimensions. Thus both retarded integrals are absolutely convergent. [given, F1, F5, A1]

2.1 Fix an event $(x_0,t_0)$ and choose a bounded open ball $O$ around it. Its closure is compact by [F3], as is the source ball $\overline B_a$. On $\overline O\times\overline B_a$, both observation times and all values $t-|x-y|/c$ lie in one bounded interval because $|x-y|\le|x|+a$; take a closed finite interval containing these times. By [F3] choose a smooth compactly supported time cutoff $\chi$ equal to one on a neighborhood of this interval. Then $h_\rho(y,s):=\chi(s)\rho(y,s)$ and $h_J(y,s):=\chi(s)J(y,s)$ belong to $C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R)$ by [F1], since their supports are contained in $\overline B_a\times\operatorname{supp}\chi$. On $O$, every retarded source value and every observation-time value sees $\chi=1$, so the potentials in the Statement equal $\mu_0c^2 K*h_\rho$ and $\mu_0K*h_J$, and the localized sources equal $\rho$ and $J$. By [F2] these potentials are smooth on $O$ and satisfy $W\varphi=\mu_0c^2\rho=\rho/\varepsilon_0$ and $WA=\mu_0J$ there. Since $(x_0,t_0)$ was arbitrary, the potentials are smooth globally and the two wave equations hold everywhere. [step 1.1, F1, F2, F3, F7, A1]

3.1 On a smaller neighborhood of the same event, change variables $z=x-y$ in the localized convolution: $K*h(x,t)=\int h(x-z,t-|z|/c)/(4\pi|z|)\,dz$. For a spatial difference quotient, the mean value theorem bounds the quotient of $h(x-z,t-|z|/c)$ by the supremum of $|\nabla_yh|$ on a common compact neighborhood, times $1/(4\pi|z|)$; for a time difference quotient the same bound uses the supremum of $|\partial_th|$. These bounds are integrable on a fixed ball by [F5]. The pointwise limits are the corresponding derivatives of $h$, so [F4] permits dominated convergence and yields $\partial_{x_i}(K*h)=K*(\partial_{y_i}h)$ and $\partial_t(K*h)=K*(\partial_th)$ on that neighborhood. For $h_J=\chi J$, $\operatorname{div}_y h_J=\chi\operatorname{div}_yJ$; for $h_\rho=\chi\rho$, $\partial_t h_\rho=\chi'\rho+\chi\partial_t\rho$. At every retarded argument sampled on the neighborhood, $\chi=1$ and $\chi'=0$ by the cutoff choice in step 2.1. Therefore $\displaystyle \operatorname{div}A+c^{-2}\partial_t\varphi=\mu_0 K*(\operatorname{div}_yJ+\partial_t\rho)=0$ there by the assumed continuity equation. The event was arbitrary, so the Lorenz condition holds everywhere. [step 2.1, F1, F2, F3, F4, F5, F7, A1]

4.1 The pair is jointly smooth by step 2.1, satisfies the two wave equations there, and is in Lorenz gauge by step 3.1. Applying [F6] on $\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R$ gives the two sourced Maxwell equations; the other two equations are the automatic identities of the potential representation included in [F6]. The selection postulate [F7] identifies this retarded solution with the model's physical branch and excludes an independent homogeneous addition. [step 2.1, step 3.1, F6, F7, A1] ∎

## Definition

Fix a named positively oriented inertial laboratory chart with coordinates
$(x,y,z,t)$, spatial domain $\mathbb R^3$, and time domain $\mathbb R$. Adopt SI
units, a vacuum speed $c>0$, an angular frequency $\omega>0$, and
$k_0:=\omega/c$ (so $[k_0]=\mathrm{m}^{-1}$). Let
$\mathbf x_\perp:=(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2$. Use the unitary two-dimensional
Fourier transform $\mathcal F_2$ whose Schwartz-space convention is
$$\widehat f(\boldsymbol\xi)=\int_{\mathbb R^2}f(\mathbf x_\perp) e^{-2\pi i\mathbf x_\perp\cdot\boldsymbol\xi}\,d^2\mathbf x_\perp,$$
and whose inverse is its unitary $L^2$ extension. The variable
$\boldsymbol\xi$ is in cycles per metre; its associated angular spatial
wavevector is $\mathbf q:=2\pi\boldsymbol\xi$ in $\mathrm{m}^{-1}$, with
$|\mathbf q|^2:=q_x^2+q_y^2$.

Choose $0<\delta<1$ and $u_0\in L^2(\mathbb R^2;\mathbb C)$ with
$\mathcal F_2u_0$ supported, up to null sets, in
$D_\delta:=\{\boldsymbol\xi:|2\pi\boldsymbol\xi|\le\delta k_0\}$.
For $\boldsymbol\xi\in D_\delta$ put
$$k_z(\boldsymbol\xi):=\sqrt{k_0^2-|2\pi\boldsymbol\xi|^2},$$
which is real and strictly positive there. For each $z\in\mathbb R$, define the
exact multiplier on all of $\mathbb R^2$ by
$$m_{\mathrm{exact}}(z,\boldsymbol\xi):=\begin{cases}e^{izk_z(\boldsymbol\xi)},&\boldsymbol\xi\in D_\delta,\\0,&\boldsymbol\xi\notin D_\delta,\end{cases}$$
and define the paraxial multiplier on all of $\mathbb R^2$ by
$$m_{\mathrm{par}}(z,\boldsymbol\xi):=e^{ik_0z}e^{-iz|2\pi\boldsymbol\xi|^2/(2k_0)}.$$
The exact scalar and paraxial scalar propagators are the $L^2$ equivalence
classes
$$u_{\mathrm{exact}}(z):=\mathcal F_2^{-1}\!\left[m_{\mathrm{exact}}(z,\boldsymbol\xi)\,\mathcal F_2u_0(\boldsymbol\xi)\right],\qquad u_{\mathrm{par}}(z):=\mathcal F_2^{-1}\!\left[m_{\mathrm{par}}(z,\boldsymbol\xi)\,\mathcal F_2u_0(\boldsymbol\xi)\right].$$
The associated real scalar fields are the real parts of these phasors multiplied
by $e^{-i\omega t}$. If $u_0$ carries $\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$, both propagated
fields carry the same unit. “Exact” here names the exact positive axial
dispersion multiplier inside this scalar model; it does not assert exact
Maxwell validity. Forward propagation is $z\ge0$; the formulas also define the
mathematical continuation to negative $z$.

At a fixed $\boldsymbol\xi\in D_\delta$, write
$\mathbf K(\boldsymbol\xi):=(2\pi\xi_x,2\pi\xi_y,k_z(\boldsymbol\xi))$.
A candidate monochromatic vector plane-wave mode at frequency $\omega$ has
complex amplitudes $\mathbf e,\mathbf b\in\mathbb C^3$ in the real-field
encoding $e^{i(\mathbf K\cdot(x,y,z)-\omega t)}$. It is called an exact
source-free vacuum mode only when its associated real fields satisfy the
adopted source-free vacuum Maxwell equations. The longitudinal electric
coefficient is $e_z$, and
$\mathbf e_\perp:=(e_x,e_y)$ has norm
$|\mathbf e_\perp|^2:=|e_x|^2+|e_y|^2$. No scalar/vector polarization
factorization is part of this definition.

## Verification

1.1 For $\boldsymbol\xi\in D_\delta$, put $\mathbf q:=2\pi\boldsymbol\xi$, $a:=k_0^2-|\mathbf q|^2$ and $b:=k_0^2(1-\delta^2)$. The support condition gives $0<b\le a\le k_0^2$. 1.2 The transverse and axial coordinates are measured in metres, while $\mathbf q,k_z,k_0$ are in $\mathrm{m}^{-1}$; thus every propagation-phase exponent is dimensionless. The multipliers preserve the unit of $u_0$. [given, F2, algebra]

1.3 [A1] fixes a separate scalar optical model, while the present Definition additionally restricts its plane data by $L^2$ and $D_\delta$ support; hence no existence or band-limit claim for every compact-aperture output is being made. [given, A1]

2.1 For $\boldsymbol\xi,\boldsymbol\eta\in D_\delta$, rationalizing gives $$|k_z(\boldsymbol\xi)-k_z(\boldsymbol\eta)|= \frac{\bigl||2\pi\boldsymbol\xi|^2-|2\pi\boldsymbol\eta|^2\bigr|} {k_z(\boldsymbol\xi)+k_z(\boldsymbol\eta)}.$$ The denominator is at least $2k_0\sqrt{1-\delta^2}>0$ by step 1.1, and the numerator is continuous in $(\boldsymbol\xi,\boldsymbol\eta)$; hence $k_z$ is continuous on $D_\delta$. By [F10], $|e^{izk_z(\boldsymbol\xi)}-e^{izk_z(\boldsymbol\eta)}|\le|z|\,|k_z(\boldsymbol\xi)-k_z(\boldsymbol\eta)|$, so the exact exponential branch is continuous on $D_\delta$; since $D_\delta$ is closed, its zero extension $m_{\mathrm{exact}}$ is measurable on $\mathbb R^2$. The paraxial phase is a continuous real polynomial in the spatial frequencies; applying the same [F10] estimate to its real phase values makes $m_{\mathrm{par}}$ continuous and hence measurable on $\mathbb R^2$. [F9] gives modulus one for $m_{\mathrm{exact}}$ on $D_\delta$ and for $m_{\mathrm{par}}$ everywhere; $m_{\mathrm{exact}}$ is zero off $D_\delta$. [step 1.1, F9, F10, algebra]

3.1 Both multipliers are measurable and bounded by one, so their products with $\mathcal F_2u_0$ are in $L^2$. Since $\mathcal F_2u_0$ vanishes almost everywhere off $D_\delta$, the exact multiplier's modulus one on $D_\delta$ and the paraxial multiplier's modulus one everywhere show that each product has the same $L^2$ norm as $\mathcal F_2u_0$. The unitary inverse in [F1] defines the propagated classes. For each fixed $t$, [F9] gives $|\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\omega t}u)|\le|e^{-i\omega t}u|=|u|$, so the real scalar fields are in $L^2$ and independent of the representative. [step 2.1, F1, F5, F9, algebra]

4.1 The fixed-frequency modes named above are not identified with the three-dimensional smooth Fourier packets of [F3]; their Maxwell constraint and the longitudinal estimate are established conditionally from the real field postulate and plane-wave convention in [F4] and [A2]. [given, F3, F4, A2] ∎

## Definition

For $u,v\in\mathbb C^3$, extend the real Euclidean dot and right-handed cross products complex-bilinearly, with coordinate formulas
$$u\cdot v:=\sum_{j=1}^3u_jv_j,\qquad u\times v:=(u_2v_3-u_3v_2,\ u_3v_1-u_1v_3,\ u_1v_2-u_2v_1).$$
The dot product in these formulas uses no complex conjugation; it differs from the Hermitian product $\langle u,v\rangle_H:=\sum_{j=1}^3\overline{u_j}v_j$. Write $\|z\|_H:=\sqrt{\langle z,z\rangle_H}$ for the associated nonnegative norm on $\mathbb C^3$.

Let $S^2:=\{n\in\mathbb R^3:n\cdot n=1\}$ with the smooth structure given by the six graph charts solving $n_j=\pm\sqrt{1-\sum_{k\ne j}n_k^2}$ on the hemispheres $\pm n_j>0$. Orient it outward using the ordered spatial basis $x^1,x^2,x^3$. Its tangent plane is $T_nS^2=\{X\in\mathbb R^3:n\cdot X=0\}$. Physically, $n=k/|k|$ for a nonzero spatial wavevector $k$ in the SI Fourier description, where $k$ has units $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$; hence $n$ is dimensionless and the radial magnitude is discarded. For $\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}$, set $C_nz:=i\,n\times z$, $P_T(n)z:=z-n(n\cdot z)$, and $P_\sigma(n):=\tfrac12(P_T(n)+\sigma C_n)$ on $\mathbb C^3$. Define the helicity fibre $L_\sigma(n):=\operatorname{range}P_\sigma(n)$ and the total space $L_\sigma:=\{(n,z):n\in S^2,\ z\in L_\sigma(n)\}$, projected to $S^2$ by $(n,z)\mapsto n$.

For an open set $U\subseteq S^2$, a unit frame of $L_\sigma$ over $U$ is a smooth map $v:U\to\mathbb C^3$ such that $v(n)\in L_\sigma(n)$ and $\langle v(n),v(n)\rangle_H=1$ for every $n\in U$. Since each fibre is a complex line, this vector spans that fibre. A local unit frame is such a map on an open neighborhood in $S^2$; a global unit frame is one defined on all of $S^2$.

Fibrewise addition and scalar multiplication are inherited from $\mathbb C^3$. A unit frame $v$ gives the local trivialization $(n,a)\mapsto(n,a v(n))$ from $U\times\mathbb C$ to $L_\sigma|_U$, with inverse $(n,z)\mapsto(n,\langle v(n),z\rangle_H)$. The construction in the Proof supplies such a frame near every $n$, so $L_\sigma$ is a smooth rank-one complex vector subbundle of the product bundle $S^2\times\mathbb C^3\to S^2$, whose projection is $(n,z)\mapsto n$.

On a spherical chart with $0<\theta<\pi$ and a smooth longitude branch $\phi$, write $n=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)$, $e_\theta=(\cos\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta\sin\phi,-\sin\theta)$, and $e_\phi=(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0)$. The local unit helicity frame is $v_\sigma:=(e_\theta+i\sigma e_\phi)/\sqrt2$. For any unit frame $v$ over an open set $U$, write $v^\dagger d v:=\sum_{j=1}^3\overline{v_j}\,d v_j$, where $d v_j$ is the exterior derivative of its $j$th component. Define its Berry connection one-form by $A:=i v^\dagger d v$. A smooth phase change $v'=e^{i\chi}v$, with real smooth $\chi$, changes this local form to $A'=A-d\chi$. The local Berry curvature is the real two-form $F:=dA$ on $U$; no global gluing assertion is made here.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The oriented unit direction sphere, its standard Hermitian space $\mathbb C^3$, and one fixed $\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}$.

[F1] [[pthm-emx-helicity-projection]] defines $C_n=i n\times$, $P_Tz=z-n(n\cdot z)$, and $P_\sigma=\tfrac12(P_T+\sigma C_n)$ for unit directions; it proves that $P_\sigma$ is smooth and is an orthogonal rank-one projection onto the $\sigma$ helicity eigenspace.

[F2] [[def-emx-transverse-fourier-field]] defines $k$ as a spatial wavevector with SI units $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and uses nonzero $k$ for its transverse Fourier amplitudes; normalizing $k$ gives the dimensionless direction $n$.

[F3] [[def-emx-lorentz-star]] fixes the ordered coframe $dx^0,dx^1,dx^2,dx^3$ and volume $dx^0\wedge dx^1\wedge dx^2\wedge dx^3$; restricting to the spatial subspace gives the ordered orientation used here.

[F4] [[def-cross-product-in-r3]] fixes the coordinate formula for the right-handed cross product; [[def-euclidean-inner-product]] fixes the real Euclidean dot product and norm.

[F5] [[def-oriented-smooth-manifold-and-oriented-chart]] defines an orientation as a smoothly chosen orientation ray; the outward normal and fixed spatial orientation specify that ray here.

[F6] [[def-smooth-differential-k-form]] defines smooth $k$-forms as smooth sections of $\bigwedge^kT^*S^2$; [[def-wedge-product-of-differential-forms]] defines their wedge product pointwise, and [[def-exterior-derivative-by-the-invariant-vector-field-formula]] defines $d$.

## Proof

1.1 The six graph charts in the Definition are smooth coordinate charts on open hemispheres and cover $S^2$; their square-root transition formulas are smooth on overlaps. The outward tangent orientation ray is smooth by the nonvanishing normal $n$, with ambient spatial orientation fixed in [F3] and orientation-ray convention [F5]. By [F1], each $P_\sigma(n)$ is a smooth rank-one projection, so each fibre is a complex line. [F2] identifies the physical direction index with a nonzero SI wavevector normalized to unit length. At any $n_0$, choose a fixed $w\in\mathbb C^3$ with $P_\sigma(n_0)w\ne0$, which exists because the projection has nonzero range. Continuity makes $P_\sigma(n)w\ne0$ on a neighborhood of $n_0$, and $P_\sigma(n)w/\|P_\sigma(n)w\|$ is a smooth unit local section there. This proves local triviality without choosing a frame family over the whole sphere. [F1, F2, F3, F5, given, construct]

1.2 The coordinate vectors satisfy $n\cdot e_\theta=n\cdot e_\phi=e_\theta\cdot e_\phi=0$, $\|e_\theta\|=\|e_\phi\|=1$, and $n\times e_\theta=e_\phi$, $n\times e_\phi=-e_\theta$, by the coordinate formulas in [F4]. Hence $v_\sigma^\dagger v_\sigma=1$, $P_Tv_\sigma=v_\sigma$, and $C_nv_\sigma=\sigma v_\sigma$. Substitution gives $P_\sigma v_\sigma=v_\sigma$, so this is a unit local frame of the stated fibre. [F1, F4, algebra]

2.1 For a smooth section $s$ on a chart, define $\nabla s:=P_\sigma d s$. Since $P_\sigma s=s$, this is again $L_\sigma$-valued. For every smooth scalar $f$, the product rule gives $\nabla(fs)=P_\sigma(df\,s+f\,ds)=df\,s+f\nabla s$, so $\nabla$ is a connection on the line bundle. In a unit frame $v$, writing $s=a v$ gives $\nabla s=(da+a v^\dagger dv)v=(da-iAa)v$ because $A=i v^\dagger dv$. [F1, F6, step 1.1, step 1.2, algebra]

3.1 Differentiating $\langle v,v\rangle_H=1$ gives $2\operatorname{Re}(\sum_j\overline{v_j}\,d v_j)=0$, so $A=i\sum_j\overline{v_j}\,d v_j$ is real-valued. Under $v'=e^{i\chi}v$, direct differentiation gives $v'^\dagger d v'=i\,d\chi+v^\dagger dv$, hence $A'=i v'^\dagger d v'=A-d\chi$. This verifies the declared local gauge transformation and completes the definition of the local connection and curvature conventions. [given, F6, step 2.1, algebra] ∎
