## Mathematical conventions and framework scope

An affine space $A$ modeled on a real vector space $V$ is a nonempty set with a free and transitive $V$-action. For events $a,b\in A$, their difference $a-b\in V$ is the unique vector carrying $b$ to $a$. Its affine coordinate charts have smooth transition maps. A spatial Euclidean metric on a subspace $K\subset V$ is a symmetric positive-definite bilinear map $g:K\times K\to\mathbb R$; the length of $w\in K$ is $\sqrt{g(w,w)}$. An orientation of $K$ selects one of the two classes of ordered bases, with equivalence determined by a positive change-of-basis determinant.

A smooth $n$-manifold (taken without boundary unless stated) is a Hausdorff second-countable space with a compatible smooth atlas of homeomorphisms to open subsets of $\mathbb R^n$ whose coordinate transitions are smooth. For a smooth manifold $Q$, $T_qQ$ consists of equivalence classes of $C^1$ curves through $q$, where equivalent curves have the same coordinate derivative; $TQ=\bigsqcup_{q\in Q}T_qQ$ has the induced bundle charts. $T_q^*Q$ is the linear dual of $T_qQ$, and $T^*Q=\bigsqcup_qT_q^*Q$. A regular level $G^{-1}(0)$ means that $DG$ is surjective at every point of that set; if the level is nonempty, the published regular-level theorem gives its embedded-manifold structure and dimension. In coordinates, $C^r$ means that all derivatives through order $r$ exist and are continuous. These definitions specify curve regularity, tangent spaces and force-map domains before use.

The intended physical scope is a Newtonian model with absolute time and Euclidean spatial geometry. Its mathematical conclusions are conditional on the adopted equations. Applying them to a real system additionally requires a stated regime where nonrelativistic kinematics, the point-particle idealization, and any chosen constraint or constitutive assumptions are adequate. This scaffold sets no universal numerical cutoff, reports no measurements, and does not claim the model describes every physical system.

## Adopted formulation, objects, and units come first

The principal formulation is Newtonian material-point mechanics with fixed positive inertial masses and a chosen force model. Other constrained, stationary-action and Hamiltonian formulations are introduced only after their objects and hypotheses are defined, as conditional branches. “First principles” means making assumptions explicit, not proving nature's laws from mathematics. In an alternative Hamiltonian-first model, the Hamiltonian and symplectic evolution law would instead be primitive model data; the present primitive/derived labels are relative to the chosen Newtonian formulation.

Use SI units with independent dimensions length ℓ, duration τ, mass μ. Coordinates denote numerical components after a chosen metre, second and kilogram, never dimensionless physical equality by default. Angle and rotation matrices are dimensionless (radian convention). The dimension map multiplies under products, divides under quotients, and requires equal dimensions for addition. Generalized coordinates can carry different dimensions; individual generalized momenta have dimension action/[qᵃ], not invariably kg m/s. Every later quantity must inherit or state a dimension. No shared quantities category or units workflow is proposed.

**P1 Newtonian spacetime structure, an adopted physical model.** Spacetime is a four-dimensional affine space $A$ modeled on $V$, with an absolute-time covector $\tau_0:V\to\mathbb R$ that is linear and surjective. Its kernel $K=\ker\tau_0$ is the three-dimensional spatial displacement space, with positive-definite metric $g$ and fixed spatial orientation as defined above. The smooth structure is the affine one. Interpret $\tau_0(w)$ as the elapsed-time component of a displacement $w\in V$, in seconds, and $g(w,w)$ as squared spatial length in square metres for $w\in K$. Events in one time slice differ by $K$; distances are defined within a slice. There is no Lorentzian metric, light cone, universal speed bound or preferred rest frame. Absolute simultaneity and Euclidean spatial geometry are physical assumptions, not conclusions of ODE uniqueness.

**P2 Inertial-frame availability and allowed changes.** Write $SO(3)=\{R\in\mathbb R^{3\times3}:R^TR=I,\det R=1\}$ for orientation-preserving orthogonal matrices in the standard spatial Euclidean product. An inertial frame is the global adapted affine chart determined by an origin $o\in A$, a constant time direction $u\in V$ with $\tau_0(u)=1$, and an oriented $g$-orthonormal basis $e_1,e_2,e_3$ of $K$. Its coordinates are $t=\tau_0(a-o)$ and the spatial components $x\in\mathbb R^3$ in that basis. This coordinate frame is distinct from an observer congruence or a time-varying basis field. The congruence of stationary frame observers is the family $t\mapsto o+tu+\sum_j(x_0)_je_j$ for fixed $x_0\in\mathbb R^3$. The model adopts transformations $t'=t+b$ and $x'=Rx+v_{\rm boost}t+a$, where $b$ is a time shift, $a$ a spatial translation, $v_{\rm boost}$ a boost velocity, and $R\in SO(3)$. Thus $b$ has time dimension, $a$ and $x$ length, $v_{\rm boost}$ length/time, and $R$ is dimensionless. A free particle in this model is one with zero applied interaction; its fixed-mass equation makes its coordinate curve affine. If $w=dx/dt$ and $a_x=d^2x/dt^2$, differentiation gives $w'=Rw+v_{\rm boost}$ and $a_x'=Ra_x$. A force law must also transform appropriately to be covariant; an external apparatus or background potential can select a frame. Kinematic Galilean symmetry alone does not imply momentum conservation of every system.

**P3 Particle idealization and fixed inertial mass.** A particle label $i$ carries a worldline $\gamma_i:I\to A$, where $I\subset\mathbb R$ is an open time interval, $\gamma_i$ is $C^2$ in affine coordinates, and $\tau_0(\gamma_i(t)-o)=t$. This parameter condition replaces relativistic causal constraints. In an inertial chart $\gamma_i(t)=(t,x_i(t))$, with $x_i:I\to\mathbb R^3$; velocity $w_i=dx_i/dt$ has length/time and acceleration $a_i=d^2x_i/dt^2$ has length/time squared. The fixed inertial mass $m_i>0$ is primitive model data with mass dimension. The point-particle idealization retains only the label, position and inertial mass in the translational equation; size, spin and internal degrees of freedom are omitted, so an application must state when those omissions are adequate. An apparatus calibration may estimate a mass parameter under additional assumptions but neither defines nor verifies the dynamical law.

For $N$ labeled particles in the unconstrained branch, the configuration is $q=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)$ in an open set $Q\subset(\mathbb R^3)^N$; a singular-force model removes collision configurations $x_i=x_j$ from $Q$. With $k$ independent holonomic constraints, take a smooth map $G:O\to\mathbb R^k$ on an open ambient set $O\subset(\mathbb R^3)^N$, require $DG(q)$ to have rank $k$ on the nonempty zero set, and set $Q=G^{-1}(0)$. Its dimension is $3N-k$ by the published regular-level theorem. At a fixed time, a time-dependent regular constraint $G(t,q)=0$ defines the same kind of regular level locally. For holonomic systems the configuration degrees of freedom are $\dim Q$; a nonholonomic velocity-map rank is a separate count, and accessibility dimension requires its own definition. In ambient particle coordinates define the positive-definite mass operator $\mathsf M=\operatorname{diag}(m_1I_3,\ldots,m_NI_3)$.

**P4 Newtonian dynamics and force superposition.** For the unconstrained branch, the force law is a map $F:I\times TQ\to(\mathbb R^3)^N$, continuous and locally Lipschitz in $(q,w)$ with a Lipschitz constant locally uniform in time; additional smoothness is imposed when differentiating $F$. Its component $F_i(t,q,w)$ is a spatial vector with force dimension mass·length/time squared. Adopt $m_i\,d^2x_i/dt^2=F_i(t,q,w)$ and additive composition of the stated force contributions. For constrained $Q$, $F$ is restricted or pulled back to the tangent bundle, and the normal reaction is separately specified by P6. Force is primitive interaction data in this formulation, not an extra independent observed quantity inferred from $x_i''$. Local ODE existence and uniqueness is conditional on these hypotheses; it gives neither all-time prediction nor empirical truth. A force bounded at each point does not guarantee continuation on an excluded domain; local M10 gives the compact-continuation criterion. Impacts, dry-friction discontinuities and stochastic forcing need separate laws and solution notions.

**P5 Restricted interaction assumptions.** When used, write $F_i=F_i^{\rm ext}+\sum_{j\ne i}F_{ij}$, with optional pair assumptions $F_{ij}=-F_{ji}$ and, separately, $F_{ij}$ parallel to $x_i-x_j$. In the unconstrained ambient particle branch, a $C^2$ potential model is $U:I\times Q\to\mathbb R$ on the open configuration domain, with joule units, and $F_i=-\nabla_{x_i}U$; gradients use the selected inertial Euclidean product. Only its spatial differential fixes the force, so a time-only additive term leaves force unchanged; the selected normalization is part of any reported energy balance. On a constrained configuration manifold, an ambient potential is restricted to $Q$ and its differential is pulled back to a generalized-force covector; an ambient extension or equivalent intrinsic differential must be specified. Pair potentials $u_{ij}(|x_i-x_j|)$ and external potentials $u_i(t,x_i)$ are examples, not the most general $U$. In coordinates on an open set, a $C^1$ one-form is $\alpha=\sum_j a_j(q)dq^j$; it is closed when $\partial_i a_j=\partial_j a_i$, equivalently $d\alpha=0$, and exact when $\alpha=dU$ for a $C^2$ scalar $U$. The coordinate exterior derivative is the alternating sum of coefficient derivatives. A closed one-form has a local potential on a sufficiently small star-shaped ball by the radial integral in E1, but need not have a global potential on a multiply connected domain. Thus “curl zero” alone on a punctured domain is insufficient. Newtonian gravitation is an optional force model with $U_{ij}=-G_{\rm N}m_i^{g}m_j^{g}/r$, where $r=|x_i-x_j|>0$, $G_{\rm N}$ has dimension length cubed/(mass·time squared), and gravitational masses $m_i^g$ are primitive coupling parameters distinct from inertial masses $m_i$. The identification $m_i^g=m_i$ is an additional empirical assumption. Inverse-square gravitation is not a consequence of Newton's second law.

**P6 Ideal constraint reactions.** A holonomic constraint is a smooth map $G:I\times O\to\mathbb R^k$ with $G(t,q)=0$ and $D_qG$ of full row rank $k$; each component of $G$ has a declared model unit, and its permitted virtual displacements at fixed time are $\delta q\in\ker D_qG$. An ideal reaction is a force covector $R\in T_q^*O$ that annihilates this kernel. In ambient particle coordinates, D'Alembert balance is $(\mathsf M\ddot q-F)\cdot\delta q=0$ for every permitted $\delta q$, with the Euclidean coordinate pairing. Since $D_qG$ is surjective, the finite-dimensional annihilator identity gives $R=D_qG^T\lambda$ for a multiplier $\lambda\in(\mathbb R^k)^*$; its units are energy divided by the corresponding constraint-component units, so $R$ has generalized-force units. This is a representation, not an independent postulate. This ideality is an adopted physical model of the mechanism, not a mathematical consequence of being constrained. A constraint is rheonomic when it depends explicitly on time and scleronomic when it does not; a moving ideal constraint can do work, with $R(\dot q)=-\lambda(\partial_tG)$ when $R=D_qG^T\lambda$. A nonholonomic model specifies a constant-rank velocity map $A(t,q):T_qQ\to\mathbb R^k$ and $b:I\times Q\to\mathbb R^k$, with $A(t,q)\dot q+b(t,q)=0$; each row has a declared output unit and $b$ has that same unit. Its allowed velocities are assumed not locally to be the tangent spaces of positional-constraint submanifolds. D'Alembert and stationary augmented-action (vakonomic) equations are distinct models; E5 proves a concrete discrepancy. Rolling without slipping means that the relative velocity of the contacting material points has zero projection to the common tangent plane; ideal reaction and a friction law capable of sustaining the required tangential force are additional assumptions. Rank loss or changing contact modes requires a separate model.

## Derived quantities and dimensions

A generalized-coordinate chart is a chart of the smooth atlas: a homeomorphism from an open subset of $Q$ to an open subset of $\mathbb R^n$ with smooth coordinate transitions. Write its coordinates $q^1,\ldots,q^n$ and a path's coordinate velocities $v^a=dq^a/dt$. In a variational branch, choose an open set $\mathcal D_{\mathcal L}\subset I\times TQ$ and a $C^2$ scalar map $\mathcal L:\mathcal D_{\mathcal L}\to\mathbb R$; the path graph must lie in this domain. A $C^2$ path $q:[t_0,t_1]\to Q$ there has action $S[q]=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\mathcal L(t,q,\dot q)\,dt$. Define the Legendre map on $\mathcal D_{\mathcal L}$ by $F_{\mathcal L}(t,q,v)=(t,q,p)$ with $p_a=\partial\mathcal L/\partial v^a$; these components transform as a covector under changes of $q$-chart. It is regular at a point where the fiber velocity Hessian $\mathcal L_{vv}$ is invertible, and hyperregular on a specified fiber domain when this map restricts to a global smooth diffeomorphism onto its image. Only on an actual inverse branch define $H(t,q,p)=v^ap_a-\mathcal L(t,q,v)$ with $v=F_{\mathcal L}^{-1}(t,q,p)$; $H$ has energy dimension. A stationary path has zero first variation for every $C^2$ fixed-endpoint variation remaining in $\mathcal D_{\mathcal L}$; it need not minimize the action.

For a rigid body, let $\mathcal B\subset\mathbb R^3$ be a bounded Borel material region with a finite nonnegative Borel mass measure $\nu$ of total mass $m_{\mathcal B}>0$, and let the Borel reference position $\rho:\mathcal B\to\mathbb R^3$ satisfy $\int\rho\,d\nu=0$ and $\int|\rho|^2d\nu<\infty$. Write $dm=d\nu$ and adopt $x(t,\rho)=c(t)+R(t)\rho$, where $c$ is $C^2$ and $R:I\to SO(3)$ is $C^2$. The body angular velocity is the unique $\omega_b$ satisfying $R^T\dot Rz=\omega_b\times z$ for every $z$; the distinct spatial angular velocity satisfies $\dot RR^Tz=\omega_s\times z$ and $\omega_s=R\omega_b$. The inertia tensors and their frame transformations are defined below; no invertibility is assumed.

For one-dimensional linear oscillator models, take $x:I\to\mathbb R$ of length dimension and class $C^2$, fixed $m>0$, constants $k>0,c\ge0$, and a continuous applied force $f:I\to\mathbb R$ in the adopted equation $m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=f(t)$. Define the undamped natural angular frequency $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$ in radians per second and the decay rate $\gamma_{\rm d}=c/(2m)$. Thus $k$ has mass/time squared and $c$ mass/time; the term “frequency” below always means angular frequency unless cycles per second is stated.

On the cotangent phase space $T^*Q$, the canonical one-form is $\lambda_{(q,p)}(z)=p(D\pi(z))$, where $\pi:T^*Q\to Q$ is projection and $z\in T_{(q,p)}T^*Q$. The wedge product is the alternating product of covectors, and $d$ is the coordinate exterior derivative (on $\sum_I a_I dx^I$, take the alternating sum of coefficient derivatives). The canonical symplectic form is $\omega=-d\lambda$; in a $q$-chart, $\lambda=\sum_a p_a\,dq^a$ and $\omega=\sum_a dq^a\wedge dp_a$. For a $C^2$ Hamiltonian $H$ define $X_H$ by $\iota_{X_H}\omega=dH$ and for $C^2$ observables define $\{f,g\}=\omega(X_f,X_g)$; in these coordinates $\dot q^a=\partial H/\partial p_a$ and $\dot p_a=-\partial H/\partial q^a$. A two-form is symplectic exactly when it is closed and nondegenerate.

For a local point generator $\delta t=\varepsilon\tau(t,q)$, $\delta q=\varepsilon\xi(t,q)$, with transformation parameter $\varepsilon$, the components $\tau,\xi$ have units chosen so these variations have time and coordinate units. Define $E_{\mathcal L}=v^ap_a-\mathcal L$. If the exact first-order variation identity in E4 holds with boundary term $\varepsilon B(t,q)$, the Noether charge is $C=p_a\xi^a-E_{\mathcal L}\tau-B$, with $B$ carrying action-per-parameter units. The parameter unit is stated for each model; $C$ has action divided by that unit. Its frame transformation depends on the transformed generator and boundary term. An impulse at $t_*$ is the momentum jump $J_*=p(t_*^+)-p(t_*^-)$ when the one-sided limits exist in the adopted impulse model. For a translational particle, $p=mw$ and $J_*$ has kg·m/s units; a generalized impulse instead inherits the action/$[q^a]$ units of its momentum component. Under a covariant Galilean boost the additive $mv_{\rm boost}$ term cancels in this jump, giving $J_*'=RJ_*$. For a central scattering model with an asymptotic incoming line, the impact parameter $b_{\rm imp}>0$ is the perpendicular distance from that line to the force center. On a smooth locally invertible branch of the azimuthally symmetric deflection map $\theta(b_{\rm imp})$ with $0<\theta<\pi$, the differential-cross-section area density is $d\sigma_{\rm sc}/d\Omega=(b_{\rm imp}/\sin\theta)|db_{\rm imp}/d\theta|$, summed over distinct branches; it is invariant under proper rotations and Galilean changes when the central force and center are transformed together. This is a model-derived outgoing-angle measure under the specified incident-flux convention, not a count of observed events.

For a continuum branch, let $B_{\rm mat}\subset\mathbb R^3$ be a bounded open material region with $C^1$ boundary and let $\chi:I\times\overline B_{\rm mat}\to\mathbb R^3$ be $C^2$, with each $\chi_t$ injective and a local diffeomorphism and $J_\chi=\det D_X\chi>0$. The current region is $\Omega_t=\chi_t(B_{\rm mat})$ and the spatial velocity is $u(t,\chi(t,X))=\partial_t\chi(t,X)$. A mass density is a nonnegative $C^1$ field $\rho_m:I\times\Omega_t\to\mathbb R$ with mass measure $\rho_m dx$. A Cauchy stress is a $C^1$ matrix field $\sigma:I\times\Omega_t\to\mathbb R^{3\times3}$ whose adopted traction law on each oriented unit normal $n$ is $t(n)=\sigma n$; body acceleration is a specified vector field $b:I\times\Omega_t\to\mathbb R^3$. These fields require the stated smoothness and transport/balance assumptions; they are not inferred from a particle limit. An elastic modulus is a specified linear map $\mathbb C:\operatorname{Sym}(3)\to\operatorname{Sym}(3)$ on symmetric strain matrices, with stress units; pressure is a scalar stress field; and in the isotropic Newtonian-fluid branch dynamic viscosity $\eta>0$ is a constant with mass/(length·time) units. Nonisotropic or state-dependent constitutive tensors need their own type and transformation law.

The continuum smoothness statement is understood on a neighborhood of $I\times\overline B_{\rm mat}$: each $\chi_t$ is one-to-one there, $D_X\chi$ is nonsingular, and its determinant is positive. This makes $\chi_t$ a diffeomorphism onto its image and permits the stated change-of-variables formula. The motion $\chi$ is adopted kinematic data, $u$ is derived from it, and $\rho_m$, $\sigma$ and $b$ are specified continuum fields subject to their separately stated balance/constitutive assumptions. The reference mass measure is not identified with $\rho_m dx$ without an additional mass-conservation postulate. The stress convention fixes the outward traction on a material subregion; no symmetry of $\sigma$ or absence of couple stresses follows from this definition alone. Under a proper Galilean change $t'=t+b_0$, $x'=Rx+v_0t+a_0$, their component conventions are $\rho'_m(t',x')=\rho_m(t,x)$, $u'=Ru+v_0$, $\sigma'=R\sigma R^T$, and $b'=Rb$ at corresponding points; this is only a coordinate/tensor rule and does not assert that every constitutive law is covariant. In the balance laws below, $\nabla$ and $\operatorname{div}$ are the Euclidean spatial derivative and row-wise divergence in the selected inertial chart; their use assumes the stated spatial regularity and the local-to-integral hypotheses of E13/E30.

For a rigid body the measure $\nu$ has mass units, $I_b=\int_{\mathcal B}(|\rho|^2I_3-\rho\rho^T)\,d\nu$ is its body-frame inertia operator, and $I_s(t)=R(t)I_bR(t)^T$ is its spatial-frame inertia. Define $\omega_b$ by $R^T\dot R z=\omega_b\times z$ and $\omega_s=R\omega_b$ by $\dot RR^Tz=\omega_s\times z$. Then $T_{\rm rot}=\tfrac12\omega_b^TI_b\omega_b=\tfrac12\omega_s^TI_s\omega_s$; $I_b$ may be singular for a line- or point-supported mass distribution, so a positive-definite inverse is used only when separately assumed. Under a fixed proper rotation $S$, spatial components obey $I_s\mapsto SI_sS^T$ and $\omega_s\mapsto S\omega_s$.

For particle expressions let $M_{\rm tot}=\sum_i m_i$ denote total mass and let $\mathsf M=\operatorname{diag}(m_1I_3,\ldots,m_NI_3)$ denote the positive mass operator; these symbols are distinct. A generalized-coordinate chart is used only on its open chart domain, and a generalized force is a covector $Q_a$ paired with $\dot q^a$; this pairing, rather than a dimensionally ambiguous dot product between unlike coordinate lists, defines generalized power.

## Precise advanced definitions and proof boundaries

In a Lagrangian branch ℒ:I×TQ→R is at least C²; action is the finite-time integral. Stationary means zero derivative for every smooth fixed-endpoint variation. Euler–Lagrange equations are d/dt ℒvᵃ=ℒqᵃ. For ideal regular holonomic constraints the coordinate parametrization eliminates the reaction; nonideal friction must be a specified generalized force. Regular means nonsingular velocity Hessian; hyperregular means a globally smooth invertible Legendre map. Neither stationarity nor Hessian invertibility proves existence of an action minimum. Hamiltonian H may depend on time, and Ḣ=Ht along canonical solutions. Explicitly time-dependent dynamics need time-tagged extended states for the noncrossing uniqueness claim.

A differential k-form assigns at each point a smoothly varying alternating k-linear functional on tangent vectors. A two-form ω is symplectic if dω=0 and ω(z,·)=0 implies z=0. On T*Q, λ(q,p)(z)=p(Dπz), ω=−dλ=Σdqᵃ∧dpₐ; the library convention is ιXHω=dH. Thus q̇=Hp, ṗ=−Hq and {f,g}=ω(Xf,Xg)=Σ(fqᵃgpₐ−fpₐgqᵃ), ḟ={f,H}+ft. A canonical transformation is a local/global diffeomorphism pulling ω back to itself; a generating function represents it only where the chosen projection is nonsingular, with exactness/topology conditions. Symplectic flow preservation and volume preservation are local-in-time claims on the actual flow domain, not completeness. Phase volume is ωⁿ/n! (with chosen orientation). Recurrence needs an invariant finite measure and an appropriate measure-preserving evolution; full rigorous statement/proof is open here.

A first integral on an open phase domain is a real-valued $C^1$ function $f$ constant on solutions, equivalently $\{f,H\}=0$ for autonomous $C^2$ $H$. Involution means pairwise zero brackets. A completely integrable $2n$-dimensional system requires $n$ smooth real-valued first integrals whose differentials are independent on the regular region and which pairwise commute. An action variable has action dimension. Adopt period-one $\theta\in\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ and $I=\oint p\,dq$, giving $\omega=d\theta\wedge dI$ in each action-angle pair and $\dot\theta=\partial H/\partial I$ in cycles per second. Tong’s radian convention uses $\theta_T=2\pi\theta$ and $J_T=I/(2\pi)$; this explicit rescaling preserves the form. Never confuse cyclic frequency with radian angular rate. The published Liouville–Arnold supplier assumes a compact connected regular fibre plus a saturated proper submersion neighborhood with connected fibres, and countable choice; it does not make every energy level a torus or supply global action-angle coordinates. In its local action-coordinate ball, the pulled-back form $\tau=\sigma^*\omega$ is smooth, closed, and of degree two. Apply the published star-shaped-domain result at $k=2$ to obtain the one-form primitive displayed at the start of this scaffold; E21 checks it coefficientwise. This closes the CM14 action-angle use. The original Liouville–Arnold item’s one-form citation mismatch remains outside this scaffold’s edit scope, but is not a prerequisite for this consumer. Hamilton–Jacobi $S$ has action dimension, solves $S_t+H(t,q,S_q)=0$, and a “complete integral” requires an $n$-parameter family with invertible mixed derivative, not just any solution.

Choose positive coordinate scales $s_a$ for length, velocity, momentum and any mixed coordinates and use the norm of the dimensionless components $z_a/s_a$; stability and numerical growth estimates always name these scales. For an autonomous $C^1$ vector field $f$ on an open state domain, a flow $\Phi_t(z)$ solves $\dot z=f(z)$ on its actual maximal domain. An equilibrium $f(z_0)=0$ is Lyapunov stable when for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that every initial state within $\delta$ has a solution for all forward times and satisfies $|\Phi_t(z)-z_0|<\varepsilon$ for all $t\ge0$; asymptotic stability additionally requires convergence for nearby data. The local linearization is $\delta\dot z=Df(\Phi_t(z))\delta z$. Finite-time growth for $t>0$ is $\log(|D\Phi_t(z)\delta z|/|\delta z|)/t$ in a specified norm; an infinite-time Lyapunov exponent requires existence or an explicitly chosen limsup. Coordinate/unit rescaling must be controlled. A Poincare section is a submanifold transverse to $f$ with a well-defined first-return map on its stated domain. “Chaos” must mean a precise property, e.g. a compact invariant set with a conjugacy to a symbolic shift plus specified sensitivity/transitivity; merely drawing irregular trajectories or finding local exponential growth is insufficient. A Hamiltonian elliptic linearization does not by itself prove nonlinear stability. KAM claims require their exact differentiability, nondegeneracy and Diophantine hypotheses (bounds $|k\cdot\omega|\ge\gamma|k|^{-\nu}$ for integer $k\ne0$); E28 supplies a complete analytic nondegenerate KAM theorem with explicit strip/Diophantine/smallness conditions; no general merely-smooth or degenerate variant is inferred.

For a continuum, material labels $X\in B_{\rm mat}\subset\mathbb R^3$ and $C^2$ motion $\chi:I\times\overline B_{\rm mat}\to\mathbb R^3$ define $\Omega_t=\chi_t(B_{\rm mat})$, with $\chi_t$ injective and $J=\det D_X\chi_t>0$. Spatial velocity is $u(t,\chi_t(X))=\partial_t\chi(t,X)$. Density is a nonnegative mass-per-volume function and mass measure $\rho_m dx$. Stress is a matrix-valued field $\sigma$ whose traction on oriented normal $n$ is $\sigma n$; body acceleration $b$ is specified. For the stated smooth field class, local mass balance $\partial_t\rho_m+\operatorname{div}(\rho_m u)=0$ and momentum balance $\rho_m(\partial_tu+(u\cdot\nabla)u)=\operatorname{div}\sigma+\rho_m b$ require the material-integral, transport and localization assumptions proved in E13/E30. They are not inferred from atomic point mechanics without a limit argument. Let $\operatorname{Sym}(3)$ be the real symmetric $3\times3$ matrices with Frobenius inner product. An elastic small-strain model uses a $C^2$ displacement $\xi:B_{\rm mat}\to\mathbb R^3$ of length dimension from the reference material region, strain $\epsilon=(D_X\xi+D_X\xi^T)/2\in\operatorname{Sym}(3)$, and $\sigma=\mathbb C(\epsilon)$, where $\mathbb C:\operatorname{Sym}(3)\to\operatorname{Sym}(3)$ is a specified linear self-adjoint positive-definite map (or the weaker positivity/symmetry assumptions stated by the particular model). Strain is dimensionless and $\mathbb C$ has stress units. Under a proper spatial rotation define $\mathcal R(X)=RXR^T$ on $\operatorname{Sym}(3)$; tensor components obey $\epsilon'=\mathcal R\epsilon$, $\sigma'=\mathcal R\sigma$, and $\mathbb C'=\mathcal R\mathbb C\mathcal R^{-1}$. The isotropic Newtonian-fluid branch specifies $\sigma=-pI_3+\eta(Du+Du^T)$ for a scalar $C^1$ pressure field $p$ in Pa and constant $\eta>0$ in kg/(m·s); $p,\eta$ are rotation-invariant scalars. Pressure, the incompressibility condition $\operatorname{div}u=0$, boundary conditions and regularity are model assumptions. Shock/weak-solution notions and PDE well-posedness beyond the explicit local branches remain outside this core.

## Evidence, review and cross-framework limits

Every postulate gets source/formulation/scope review, no invented Proof section. Every physics theorem and thought experiment needs a complete deduction, with mathematical, physical-assumption and physical-result dependency roles distinguished. A thought experiment assumes a hypothetical apparatus or outcome and supplies no observations. An actual experiment requires retrieval and reading of a primary report, documented preparation, apparatus calibration, measured data, uncertainties, sampling/selection model, systematic-error treatment and qualified comparison model. The four course/text sources here do not close that requirement. No measurement counts, precision, p-values or effect size are invented.

A measurement model may write $Y_k=h(z(t_k),\theta_{\rm apparatus})+\epsilon_k$, where $t_k\in I$, state $z(t_k)$ belongs to the stated ODE state domain, $\theta_{\rm apparatus}$ lies in a specified calibration-parameter set, and $h$ maps to the declared observation space; $Y_k$ and $\epsilon_k$ share the stated observation units. The error is a random variable only after a probability space and law are specified; otherwise it denotes an explicitly bounded or modeled residual. Independence, distributions and moments must be justified or adopted explicitly. Mathematical probability/estimation interfaces need exact suppliers before any quantitative inference. Finite observations constrain specified laws plus apparatus assumptions; they cannot prove Newtonian spacetime, all force laws or all classical mechanics. Reviewer confidence is not a physical uncertainty estimate. Discrepancy may expose model, calibration, preparation or analysis error. Simulated trajectories and numerical fits are labeled computations/proposals.

Author/judge/adjudicator records must separately assess (1) source fidelity, (2) mathematical validity and dependency closure, (3) applicability of the adopted model, and (4) empirical strength. A valid conditional calculation can be inapplicable; a faithful textbook paraphrase can contain an invalid overstatement. Conservation conditions, approximation remainders and empirical qualifications travel downstream.

Relativity is a later bridge requiring Lorentzian spacetime, changed mass/momentum laws and an explicit small-speed/weak-field limit, not a premise of CM01. Electromagnetic force coupling is a later physical assumption requiring specified field maps, charge parameters and a coupling law from an independently developed EM framework; no such coupling is used here, particle mechanical momentum alone need not be conserved, and no field-momentum theorem is imported. Quantum correspondence requires an explicit quantization/semiclassical construction and domain/limit hypotheses; replacing a classical momentum variable by an operator is not a derivation. Thermodynamic/statistical equilibrium and entropy are later bridges requiring ensembles/coarse graining/limits, not consequences of deterministic Hamiltonian evolution. Singular Lagrangians and Dirac constraint theory, general relativity, relativistic gravitation and quantum field theory are deferred beyond this design.
