## Precise framework before physical terminology

Choose SI units and the nonrelativistic Newtonian particle formulation. Let `T` be an oriented affine real line with a fixed time unit, and `E` an oriented Euclidean affine 3-space with translation vector space `V`, inner product `h`, and fixed length unit. Use spacetime `M=T×E` with projection `τ:M→T`; simultaneity means equality of `τ`. Spatial displacement is an element of `V` between simultaneous events. The product's affine structure supplies a flat connection for derivatives. There is no Lorentzian spacetime metric, light cone, proper time, or speed restriction in this model. This absolute-time/Euclidean-space structure is an adopted formulation assumption; it is not a mathematical deduction about nature.

A reference frame here means an adapted coordinate map `Φ:I×R³→τ⁻¹(I)`, `Φ(t,x)=(t,o(t)+R(t)x)`, where `I⊂T` is an open interval, `o∈C²(I,E)` is the origin trajectory and `R∈C²(I,LinearIsom⁺(R³,V))` is an oriented orthonormal spatial basis map. This is a chart specification, not an observer congruence or arbitrary basis on spacetime. Inertial frames have `o(t)=o₀+u(t−t₀)` and constant `R`; selecting their physical applicability belongs to the Newtonian formulation. Their coordinate changes have `t′=t+b`, `x′=Qx+vt+a`, constant `Q∈SO(3)`, `v,a∈R³`. Curvilinear spatial coordinates can be used within one inertial frame; nonlinear coordinate components generally do not obey scalar `m q¨=F_q`. These refinements implement §1.2 pp1–3 and §2.2 pp33–36. The source's boost-only formula p2 is a special case, not the full Euclidean/Galilean class.

A particle trajectory is `γ:I→M`, `γ(t)=(t,r(t))` with `r∈C²(I,E)` and `τ∘γ=id_I`. In a chosen frame its coordinates are `x(t)=R(t)⁻¹(r(t)−o(t))`. Velocity and acceleration in an inertial frame are `ẋ:I→R³` and `ẍ:I→R³`, with units m/s and m/s². A system of `N≥1` labeled particles has fixed parameters `m_i>0` in kg and a configuration `q=(x₁,…,x_N)` in an open region `U⊂(R³)^N`. Collision points must be excluded for singular pair interactions. The state space is `U×(R³)^N`; the force model is a map `F:U×(R³)^N×I→(R³)^N`, continuous in time and locally Lipschitz in state for local unique initial-value evolution. `C¹` in state suffices. A global solution additionally requires continuation bounds and avoidance of singular/boundary escape; determinism is not global existence by fiat (§7.1.1 p143 states local existence without proof).

Newton's equation is the adopted physical postulate `m_i ẍ_i=F_i(q,ẋ,t)` in an inertial frame, supplied with initial `q(t₀),ẋ(t₀)`. Source: §1.2 pp1–2. Initial data and force parameters are inputs, not outputs of the law. The force-free case has affine spatial trajectories. A third-law pair model additionally imposes `F_ij=−F_ji`; a strong central pair model also requires `F_ij` parallel to `x_i−x_j`. These restricted assumptions must not be asserted universally for velocity-dependent interactions or particles exchanging momentum with an omitted field (§1.3 pp12–13).

A generalized configuration space is a smooth finite-dimensional manifold `Q` locally parameterized by independent coordinates `q^a`; a trajectory is a `C²` curve in `Q`. Its velocity lies in the tangent space `T_qQ`, not a coordinate-independent list of equal-unit numbers. For embedded particle constraints, use maps `f:U×I→R^k`, `C²` with spatial derivative rank `k` on `f=0`; this makes each instantaneous configuration set a submanifold. An ideal constraint reaction annihilates all admissible virtual displacements `δq∈ker D_qf` at fixed time. This is the exact content needed for d'Alembert's virtual-work hypothesis, §§1.4–1.5 pp19–25. Time-dependent constraints can do actual work despite zero virtual work. A velocity constraint is `A(q,t)q̇+b(q,t)=0` with constant row rank; its admissible virtual vectors obey `Aδq=0`. The source calls this 'semi-holonomic'. A nonintegrable velocity constraint uses d'Alembert dynamics; unconstrained stationary action with velocity constraints inserted is a different problem in general (p25).

A Lagrangian is a `C²` function `L:TQ×I→R`, units J. The action on a compact time segment is `S[q]=∫L(q,q̇,t)dt`, units J·s. Stationarity means its first derivative at `q` along every smooth endpoint-fixed variation vanishes. Integration by parts and the fundamental lemma give Euler–Lagrange equations; it does **not** imply a minimum (§1.5 pp22–23). For Newtonian natural systems, `L=T−V`, `T=½∑m_i|ẋ_i|²`, with a `C²` potential `V:U×I→R`; `F_i=−∇_iV` uses the spatial metric. Ideal holonomic restriction yields the reduced equations. Nonpotential generalized forces `R_a` enter `d(∂L/∂q̇^a)/dt−∂L/∂q^a=R_a` (p20).

The momentum map of the Lagrangian is the fibre derivative `FL:TQ×I→T*Q×I`, `p_a=∂L/∂q̇^a`. If its velocity Hessian is invertible, it is locally invertible; a global equivalent Hamiltonian needs a global fibre inverse (hyperregularity). Define `H(q,p,t)=p_a v^a−L(q,v,t)` with `v` that inverse. Canonical phase space is the cotangent bundle `T*Q`, locally `(q,p)`, with two-form `ω=∑dq^a∧dp_a`. Hamilton's equations are `q̇^a=∂H/∂p_a`, `ṗ_a=−∂H/∂q^a`. A canonical transformation is a sufficiently smooth diffeomorphism preserving `ω`; time-dependent transformations need the corresponding extended-action condition and transformed Hamiltonian. These definitions make exact the local calculations §§1.2 pp8–10 and 4.1–4.3 pp60–72; no unrestricted equivalence for singular Lagrangians is licensed.

For `C²` functions `f,g` on a phase-space chart, define the Poisson bracket ` {f,g}=∑(∂_q f ∂_p g−∂_p f ∂_q g)`. Along dynamics, `df/dt={f,H}+∂_tf`. For higher bracket identities use adequate differentiability (e.g. `C³`). A complete Hamilton–Jacobi integral is a `C²` function `S(q,α,t)` satisfying `∂_tS+H(q,∂_qS,t)=0` with `det(∂²S/∂q∂α)≠0` locally; then `p=∂_qS`, `β=∂_αS` define the canonical change where the inverse-function theorem applies. It is not a guaranteed global solution across caustics (§4.4 pp73–78).

## Primitive, derived, and model-specific quantities

'Primitive' here means input to the chosen Newtonian formulation, not an SI-base-unit assertion. The Euclidean geometry/time calibration, particles, constant inertial masses, force map and its empirical parameters, initial data, and selected constraints are inputs. Momentum/energy/action are constructed after these. For Lagrangian or Hamiltonian formulations adopted independently, `Q,L` or `(T*Q,ω,H)` may instead be primitive; equivalence requires the preceding hypotheses.

Force laws are additional adopted restricted models. A gravitational example is `V=−∑_{i<j}G m_i^g m_j^g/|x_i−x_j|` on collision-free `U`, with positive gravitational charges `m_i^g`. Identifying `m_i^g=m_i` is an additional physical assumption; Stewart uses that customary identification. For a two-body reduction set `μ=m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂)>0`, relative displacement `r=x₁−x₂`, and `V(r)=−κ/|r|`, `κ>0` in J·m. Hooke's model uses `V=½k(x−x₀)²`, `k>0`; constant near-Earth gravity uses `V=mgz`, with constant `g` (m/s²) as an approximation, not a globally uniform planetary field. See §§1.2–1.3 and 4.5. Linear viscous drag `F=−b v`, `b≥0` in kg/s, and Coulomb sliding drag are distinct models (§1.4 p20). Sliding drag contains `v/|v|`, undefined at zero; static friction needs an inequality/set-valued reaction model and cannot be assigned the smooth ODE theorem without further work. Static, sliding, and rolling friction coefficients are dimensionless phenomenological inputs.

## Source-backed conditional consequences and hypotheses

**Conservation** (§1.3 pp12–15; §4.3 pp69–72). With fixed positive masses and pair antisymmetry, `dP/dt=∑F_ext`; `P` is constant if total external force vanishes. With central antisymmetric internal forces and zero external torque about a fixed inertial origin, `dL_total/dt=0`. About the center of mass the corresponding external torque is required, with the COM correction handled explicitly. `R=∑m_ix_i/M`, `M=∑m_i`, is derived; kinetic energy splits into COM and relative pieces because `∑m_i(x_i−R)=0`.

For a natural autonomous potential and appropriate ideal stationary constraints, `E=T+V` is constant. More generally `dH/dt=∂_tH=−∂_tL` along regular dynamics. `H=E` requires the correct quadratic kinetic/no-velocity-potential structure and frame/coordinate convention; time-dependent coordinates can alter that identification. The moving-cart example pp15–16 illustrates it. A cyclic coordinate has `∂L/∂q^a=0`, hence constant `p_a`; general Noether symmetries need an infinitesimal variation and possibly a total-derivative boundary term, rather than always a globally cyclic coordinate. Hamiltonian phase volume conservation is conditional on a smooth local Hamiltonian flow; differentiable mixed-partial cancellation gives zero divergence, and the Jacobian determinant obeys the flow equation. It does not prove indefinite global flow.

**Central forces and Kepler** (§4.5 pp79–83; §4.6 pp88–91). Let `V(r)=U(|r|)` with `U∈C²((0,∞))`. Torque vanishes, so `ℓ=r×μṙ` is constant. Nonzero `ℓ` confines motion to its perpendicular plane; `ℓ=0` is radial and needs separate treatment. Effective radial potential is `U_eff(s)=U(s)+|ℓ|²/(2μs²)`, `s>0`. Simple turning points and intervals with `E>U_eff` permit quadratures; signs must track inward/outward branches. For inverse-square attraction, conics obey `s⁻¹=(μκ/|ℓ|²)(1+e cos(ψ−ψ₀))`, `e²=1+2E|ℓ|²/(μκ²)`. Noncollision bound `E<0` gives ellipses (including circular case), positive/zero energy gives unbound branches under appropriate initial data. The source does not supply a complete independent derivation of every orbital-element/action integral it records. A potential's singularity prevents universal completeness of trajectories.

**Oscillations and stability** (Ch3 pp52–59; §2.4 p45; §7.3 pp164–172). Around an interior equilibrium `q₀`, take a natural autonomous `C³` Lagrangian with positive definite mass matrix `M(q₀)` and potential Hessian `K=D²V(q₀)`. The quadratic approximation is `½η̇ᵀMη̇−½ηᵀKη`; its modes solve `Ka=ω²Ma`. The spectral theorem applied to `M⁻¹/² K M⁻¹/²` establishes a mass-orthonormal basis. `K≥0` yields nonnegative frequencies squared; zero modes have `a+bt` solutions and are not exhausted by sinusoidal formulas. `K>0` supplies a strict quadratic energy minimum; nonlinear stability can be proved via the conserved energy and suitable local bounds. Linear oscillation alone is not a global nonlinear stability proof.

For an autonomous `C¹` ODE `ż=f(z)` on open `D⊂R^n`, equilibrium means `f(z*)=0`. Lyapunov stability means: for every `ε>0` there is `δ>0` such that initial distance less than `δ` keeps forward distance less than `ε`, for all forward times of a solution. Attraction additionally means convergence to `z*`; asymptotic stability combines them. All eigenvalues of `Df(z*)` with strictly negative real parts imply local asymptotic stability; a positive-real-part eigenvalue implies instability; zero-real-part cases need extra analysis. These implications need separate mathematical suppliers, not the source's plots. Torque-free principal-axis rotation of a rigid body with distinct positive moments has exponentially unstable linear transverse perturbations at the intermediate moment; extreme moments have oscillatory linear perturbations. The source's linear calculation does not alone prove the full nonlinear statement.

**Rigid bodies** (Ch2). Represent a body by a finite positive mass measure `dm` on a reference subset `B⊂R³` with finite second moment. Its motion is `x(t,a)=c(t)+R(t)a`, `R(t)∈SO(3)`, `C²` in time; this fixes shape/orientation component and distances. The body has six local configuration dimensions when its shape has no continuous isotropy; a collinear or point idealization needs separate counting. Define angular velocity by the skew matrix `ṘR⁻¹`, equivalently `(ṘR⁻¹)w=ω×w`. For COM-centered `a`, `I=∫(|a|²Id−a⊗a)dm`, `T_rot=½ω·Iω`, `L_rot=Iω`, with basis transforms stated. `I` is positive semidefinite, strictly definite unless mass is concentrated on a line through the selected origin. The parallel-axis tensor relation is proved algebraically in pp39–40. In body principal axes, `Iω̇+ω×Iω=τ`. A stationary pivot or COM is needed for the simple angular-momentum equation; an arbitrary accelerating origin adds terms. Euler-angle charts are local and singular at polar configurations. The symmetric-top reduction pp45–51 assumes `I₁=I₂`, fixed pivot, constant uniform gravitational torque model, and conserved axial momenta.

**Actions, perturbation, nonlinear dynamics** (Ch4–5,7). For a regular closed one-dimensional energy orbit away from separatrices, `J(E)=∮p dq` satisfies `dJ/dE=period`; hence `dH/dJ=cycles/s`. The source's multidimensional treatment requires separability and local invertibility of the actions; global action-angle coordinates additionally need integrable compact regular invariant tori, not any system with several apparent periodic motions. Perturbation expansions `H=H₀+εH₁` need a stated domain, smoothness, small parameter and time scale; secular `εt` growth limits a truncated approximation. Stewart calculates first-order examples but supplies no universal convergent expansion or quantified remainder.

A limit cycle is an isolated periodic orbit of a flow; attraction is separate. A stroboscopic map is the time-period advance map for a periodically forced system; a general Poincare return map needs a transverse section and actual return domain. A discrete model is a map `f:X→X`, iterates `f^n`, e.g. logistic `f_r(x)=rx(1−x)` on `[0,1]`, `0≤r≤4`. Its local stability criterion is `|f′(x*)|<1`; finite linear growth cannot be asserted to diverge to infinity for a bounded nonlinear map. A Lyapunov exponent, when the limit exists, uses infinitesimal tangent evolution, `lim(1/n)log|D f^n(x)v|` for a map, or `lim(1/t)log||DΦ_t(x)v||` for a flow, not an indefinitely valid exponential law for finite separation. Chaos requires a chosen precise definition and invariant domain; sensitivity, mixing and positive exponents are not interchangeable. Source numerical portraits illustrate behavior; they are not proofs for all parameter values. Feigenbaum universality, Lorenz attractor existence and dimensional equalities require substantial additional hypotheses/proofs.

## Continuum extension and its boundaries

Let `Ω⊂R³` be a spatial domain with suitably regular boundary, `I` a time interval. Define fields `ρ∈C¹(Ω×I,(0,∞))`, `v∈C¹(Ω×I,R³)`, pressure `P∈C¹(Ω×I,R)`; use `C²` spatial velocity for classical viscous equations. A fluid path is `ẋ(t)=v(x(t),t)` where local uniqueness holds; a material flow map `χ(a,t)` is locally a diffeomorphism while its Jacobian stays nonzero. The material derivative is `D_t=∂_t+v·∇`. The continuum description is an extra physical model, not proved by merely letting particle count grow.

Mass balance is `∂_tρ+div(ρv)=0`. For an ideal inviscid fluid the Cauchy stress is `σ_C=−P Id` and momentum balance is `ρD_tv=−∇P+f`, where `f:Ω×I→R³` is force per volume, N/m³. Pressure/EOS/entropy closure are additional input. Source §§6.1–6.2 pp104–114 derives these through material-volume arguments, requiring transport and divergence theorems. It does not derive all constitutive assumptions from particle Newton laws. Entropy `s` here is specific entropy, J/(kg·K), and `D_ts=0` presumes smooth adiabatic inviscid flow without shocks. `div v=0` implies `D_tρ=0`; constant density everywhere requires uniform initial density or another assumption.

For a Newtonian isotropic fluid, viscous stress is `σ_vis=2η(D−(div v)Id/3)+ζ(div v)Id`, `D=(∇v+∇vᵀ)/2`, with `η,ζ≥0`. This linear constitutive law and no-slip wall condition are restricted physical assumptions. Constant coefficients yield the source's Navier-Stokes equation §6.6 pp127–130; coefficient variability changes the expanded formula. Kinetic energy lost to viscous stress becomes internal energy, not disappearance of total energy. Define vorticity `Ω_v=curl v`, units s⁻¹; in smooth constant-density inviscid flow with conservative body force it obeys `D_tΩ_v=(Ω_v·∇)v`, so it need not be constant along a path.

For smooth steady barotropic flow with `P=P(ρ)`, specific enthalpy `h(ρ)=∫P′(u)/u du`, and conservative stationary body force `f=−ρ∇Φ`, Bernoulli `½|v|²+h+Φ` is constant along a connected streamline. For constant density `h=P/ρ`. Global equality between streamlines needs extra conditions, e.g. connected irrotational flow. A global velocity potential needs vanishing circulation or a suitable simply connected domain; curl zero alone is local. Linear sound requires a uniform equilibrium, small perturbations and an EOS derivative `(∂P/∂ρ)_s>0`, giving `c_s²` that derivative. These are conditional approximations, not universal wave-speed laws.

The spring-chain passage pp104–106 holds mass/length `μ=m/a` and stiffness-length `Y=ka` fixed. In that one-dimensional rod convention `Y` has units N (axial rigidity, not 3D Young modulus Pa unless divided by area); the scalar displacement field `η(x,t)` has units m, and `μ η_tt=Y η_xx`. Taylor/Riemann-sum reasoning alone does not prove convergence of discrete solutions. Field variations need compact support or stated endpoint/boundary conditions.

For steady constant-density pipe models with constant viscosity and no-slip walls, direct substitutions validate Poiseuille profiles (§6.7 pp130–134). For an isolated sphere of radius `a>0`, creeping flow assumes Reynolds number `Re=ρua/η≪1`; far-field uniform velocity, no slip, stationary incompressibility and discarded inertia produce Stokes drag magnitude `6πηau` (§6.8 pp135–138). This is conditional on the Stokes approximation, not an exact force at arbitrary Reynolds number. Low Mach and small Reynolds regimes are different limits. The source's wake thresholds and Kolmogorov `E(k)∝ε^(2/3)k^(−5/3)` discussion pp139–141,195 need empirical/phenomenological qualifications and homogeneity/isotropy/inertial-range assumptions; dimensional analysis does not establish measured universality. No general Navier-Stokes global regularity or complete turbulence theorem is supplied.

## Empirical observations versus predictions

The source's force coefficients, material equations, idealizations, and gravitational parameters are physical assumptions/empirical inputs. All deductions above are conditional model predictions. Rulers, timing, inertial-frame approximation, mass/force calibration, temperature/pressure calibration and data analysis are operational prerequisites for a mechanics experiment; they do not logically supply the observed result.

The Pitot/manometer calculation (§6.3 pp119–120) is an apparatus model: under steady incompressible inviscid Bernoulli and hydrostatic assumptions, measured height difference relates to inferred speed. This chapter reports no trial dataset, calibration protocol, uncertainty budget or sampling model, so it cannot furnish a complete reported experiment item. The Foucault example p35 is a prediction/exercise, not an experimental report. The perihelion discussion p101 quotes agreement with Mercury observations but omits the underlying observational source and uncertainty analysis; do not reproduce those numbers as independently audited evidence or claim they prove mechanics/relativity. Its use of 'arcsecond' as a fraction of a time second is a wording error: arcseconds are angular units. The numerical chaos figures are computations/illustrations, not measurements of nature.

A real experimental item must cite an authoritative report, identify apparatus/preparation/calibration, measured dataset or qualified qualitative observations, resolution/systematic errors and any sampling model. Statistical comparison needs explicit alternatives, likelihood/error-rate assumptions and selection effects. Finite agreement supports a specified model with auxiliaries; it does not deductively prove Newtonian mechanics. Prediction and actual measurement must occupy different roles in the dependency graph.
