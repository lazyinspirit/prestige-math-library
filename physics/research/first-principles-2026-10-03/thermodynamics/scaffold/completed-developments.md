## M32. Sequential compactness actually used in finite-dimensional proofs

Every bounded sequence in R^d has a convergent subsequence. For d=0 there is only the empty tuple. For d≥1 place the sequence in a fixed closed cube. Bisect each coordinate successively; at every finite split at least one of the two closed halves contains infinitely many terms, since their union contains all terms in the parent. Choose the left half when it has that property, otherwise the right. Repeating full coordinate cycles gives nested closed boxes of diameters tending to zero, each containing infinitely many sequence terms. The nested-interval property in each coordinate gives a unique common point x. Choose subsequence indices recursively as the least index larger than the preceding index whose term is in the next selected box; infinitely many terms ensures existence. Every chosen term and x lie in that box, so their distances tend to zero. This is a deterministic recursion, not countable choice. A closed bounded subset contains every such limit. A Cauchy sequence is bounded, so has the above subsequence limit; the triangle inequality and its Cauchy condition make the entire sequence converge to that limit. Uniformly Cauchy continuous curves on a compact interval converge pointwise in R^d, and the same uniform bound makes convergence uniform; for continuity of the limit, bound its increment by two uniform approximation errors and the increment of one continuous approximating curve. Thus the closed spaces of curves used in contraction arguments are complete in the sup norm. Therefore the convergent subsequences used in M13–M14 and in the existence/curvature/spectral-gap arguments are supplied by this explicit elementary argument, in addition to the reviewed compactness/extreme-value interfaces.

## M11. Ordered scalable compounds: cancellation and entropy

**Objects.** Fix a nonempty set X. A compound is a finite list of pairs (t,x), t>0, x∈X, modulo permutation and reassociation. Its mass is Σt, a dimensionless bookkeeping number. Scaling multiplies every coefficient. The empty list has mass zero and is an identity. A relation ≺ is defined between equal-mass compounds. Assume reflexivity, transitivity, composition consistency, scaling invariance, splitting (t,x) equivalent to (a,x),(t−a,x) for 0<a<t, and stability: (A,ε_n Z_0)≺(B,ε_n Z_1), for fixed positive compounds Z_i of equal mass and ε_n>0 decreasing to zero, implies A≺B. Assume comparison on every equal-mass pair of compounds, including different decompositions. This is a precise stronger CH interface; no derivation from simple-system geometry is hidden in it.

**Cancellation.** If (A,C)≺(B,C), for each integer n split A into n equal copies and use the scaled given comparison successively to replace each A/n by B/n with the same spectator C/n. Consistency leaves unreplaced and already replaced copies intact. After n replacements (A,C/n)≺(B,C/n). Stability with Z_0=Z_1=C yields A≺B. Conversely consistency supplies the comparison with a spectator. This proof permits compound A,B and uses no topological convergence of their states.

**Signed notation.** A formal expression Σa_i x_i with real coefficients in a comparison means moving each negative coefficient to the opposite side and deleting zeros. Identical terms can be canceled by the preceding theorem; positive identical terms can be recombined by splitting. Consequently addition of the same formal expression to both sides preserves and reflects a comparison. Signed expressions are bookkeeping, never negative physical systems. A stability application involving signed A,B is legal after moving their fixed negative terms across: the resulting bases are fixed positive compounds and only the ε auxiliaries vary.

Suppose x_0≺x_1 and not x_1≺x_0. Write R_λ=(1−λ)x_0+λx_1 in signed notation. If a_0+a_1=b_0+b_1, then a_0x_0+a_1x_1≺b_0x_0+b_1x_1 iff a_1≤b_1: when b_1−a_1≥0 the comparison is the scaled x_0≺x_1 after cancellation; a negative difference would instead imply x_1≺x_0. This proves the assertion also for signed coefficients, not only λ∈[0,1].

For x∈X define L_x={λ∈R:R_λ≺x}. It is nonempty: if no α>0 satisfies (1+α)x_0≺(αx_1,x), comparison gives (αx_1,x)≺(1+α)x_0 for every α; division by α and recombination give (x_1,x/α)≺(x_0,x_0/α), and stability gives the prohibited x_1≺x_0. Thus −α∈L_x for some α. It is bounded above: an unbounded sequence λ_n∈L_x tending to infinity gives (x_0,λ_nx_1)≺(x,λ_nx_0), hence (x_1,x_0/λ_n)≺(x_0,x/λ_n) and again the prohibited reverse comparison by stability. Least upper bounds therefore define S(x)=sup L_x∈R.

The reference ordering makes L_x downward closed. Put s=S(x). For ε_n=1/n, downward closure and the supremum property give R_(s−ε_n)≺x. Adding ε_nx_1 and recombining gives (R_s,ε_nx_0)≺(x,ε_nx_1); stability gives R_s≺x. Since s+ε_n∉L_x, comparison gives x≺R_(s+ε_n); adding ε_nx_0 gives (x,ε_nx_0)≺(R_s,ε_nx_1), so stability gives x≺R_s. Thus x~R_s, and the reference-order lemma proves this value is unique. In particular S(x_0)=0,S(x_1)=1.

Replace each (t_i,x_i) by t_iR_(S(x_i)), use consistency and recombination, and compare the resulting two reference expressions. For equal total mass,

Σt_ix_i≺Σt'_jy_j iff Σt_iS(x_i)≤Σt'_jS(y_j).

This proves additive extensive representation and attainment of the defining supremum. If S* also represents all these comparisons, equivalence x~R_(S(x)) gives S*(x)=S*(x_0)+(S*(x_1)−S*(x_0))S(x); the coefficient is positive because x_0≺≺x_1. If every state is equivalent, comparison/consistency imply all equal-mass compounds equivalent, and any constant represents them; there is no nonzero unit interval to calibrate. Conversely a real additive representation on equal-mass compounds gives these six axioms: stability follows by taking limits of its real inequalities with fixed finite auxiliary values. No simple-state topology or physical law was used.

**Physical use.** Apply only when operational A1–A6 and this product CH hold. If convex recombination (tx,(1−t)y)≺tx+(1−t)y is separately adopted on a convex coordinate domain, the representation proves S(tx+(1−t)y)≥tS(x)+(1−t)S(y); no concavity is inferred merely from a preorder. When types may react, choose a finite list of allowed directed channels a. Let ν_a∈R^r be output minus input type amounts, and let d_a be a finite lower bound on the already calibrated output-minus-input entropy over that channel. An offset vector b∈R^r makes every listed transition nondecreasing whenever b·ν_a+d_a≥0. The exact feasibility criterion is: for all nonnegative vectors λ with Σλ_aν_a=0, Σλ_ad_a≥0. Necessity follows by weighted addition of the inequalities.

For sufficiency the following finite elimination proves the alternative, without citing an unproved separation theorem. A finite system of inequalities c_j·b≥h_j either has a solution or a nonnegative linear combination with zero left side and positive right side. Induct on the number of variables. In zero variables an inconsistent inequality 0≥h_j>0 is itself a certificate. For the last variable, inequalities with positive coefficient give lower bounds; negative coefficients give upper bounds; zero coefficients remain inequalities in the other variables. Existence of the last variable is equivalent to every lower bound being ≤ every upper bound and the retained zero inequalities. Each such reduced inequality is a nonnegative combination of two original inequalities after division by absolute last coefficients. If the reduced system is feasible choose any number between the finitely many lower/upper bounds; when only one side exists choose its maximum/minimum, and when neither exists choose zero. If it is infeasible the induction certificate, substituted into these nonnegative combinations, gives an original certificate. Apply this alternative to c_a=ν_a,h_a=−d_a: the stated criterion excludes the certificate, proving existence of b. Strict increase on every irreversible channel requires strictly stronger inequalities and is not supplied. ## M13. Nonsmooth finite-dimensional duality with a proved separation step

An extended-real function f:R^d→(−∞,+∞] is proper if never −∞ and finite somewhere. Its epigraph E={(x,r):r≥f(x)} is convex when f is convex, and closed when f is lower semicontinuous (all sublevel sets are closed). Define f*(p)=sup_x(p·x−f(x)), and f**(x)=sup_p(p·x−f*(p)). A subgradient at a finite point x is p with f(y)≥f(x)+p·(y−x) for every y. For concave upper-semicontinuous S use f=−S; a supergradient of S is −p for p∈∂f. Relative domains can be handled by identifying their affine hull with R^k and extending by +∞ outside it; derivative charts require relative interior.

**Finite-dimensional separation.** For nonempty closed convex E and z∉E, choose an e_0∈E. A minimizing sequence for distance to z eventually lies in a closed bounded ball, hence has a convergent subsequence; closedness gives a nearest point e*. For every e∈E and 0<t<1, minimality of ‖z−e*‖² compared to ‖z−e*−t(e−e*)‖², followed by t↓0, gives (z−e*)·(e−e*)≤0. Thus the affine functional with normal z−e* separates z strictly from E. Only Heine–Borel, continuity of squared norm and expansion were used.

**Biconjugation.** Always f**≤f by the defining supremum. For t<f(x), separate (x,t) from its closed epigraph in the form a·y+b r≥c>a·x+b t for all (y,r)∈E. The upward ray implies b≥0. If x∈dom f, substituting (x,f(x)) rules out b=0. Then f(y)≥(c−a·y)/b is an affine minorant with value at x strictly exceeding t, so f**(x)>t.

For x outside dom f the separator may have b=0. First obtain one affine minorant ℓ_0(y)=p_0·y+q_0 by the preceding argument at a finite point y_0 and t_0<f(y_0). If b=0, then a·y≥c>a·x for every y∈dom f. The affine functions ℓ_k(y)=ℓ_0(y)+k(c−a·y) are minorants, since their added term is nonpositive on dom f, whereas ℓ_k(x)→+∞. Thus f**(x)=+∞. Combining the finite and infinite cases proves f=f** everywhere. This proof includes the commonly omitted vertical-separator case.

Fenchel equality f(x)+f*(p)=p·x holds iff p∈∂f(x): each statement says that x attains the supremum defining f*(p). Equality characterizes an attained minimum of f(y)−p·y, even if its minimizer is nonunique. No attainment follows merely from closedness. A sufficient condition is lower semicontinuity plus coercivity f(y)−p·y→+∞ as ‖y‖→∞: a minimizing sequence then lies in a compact sublevel and has a convergent subsequence. A finite infimum is needed; an initial finite comparison point provides its upper bound and coercivity/compactness provide its lower bound.

A convex function's subgradient is monotone: add its two supporting inequalities to get (p−q)·(x−y)≥0. If x,y minimize f−p·x, convexity makes every convex combination a minimizer; f is affine on their segment. Conversely any affine segment supported by the same p is a coexistence face. This gives the nonsmooth replacement for differentiating through a cusp.

## M14. Convexification, lever rule and stability boundaries

## M15. Complete regular phase-rule rank calculation and reactions

**Local implicit rank lemma.** Let F:R^n→R^q be C¹ near x*, F(x*)=0 and DF(x*) have row rank q. Here full row rank means a q×q minor is nonsingular; equivalently the q rows are independent. The equivalence follows by successive Gaussian elimination: a nonzero remaining row has a pivot column; eliminate that column in all other rows, recurse on the remaining rows/columns, and independent rows prevent a zero row before q pivots. Pivot columns give a triangular minor with nonzero diagonal after invertible row operations. Select q columns forming an invertible q×q matrix B; write x=(y,z), y∈R^(n−q), z∈R^q. For y near y*, solve z=G_y(z):=z−B^−1F(y,z). Continuity of D_zF makes ‖D_zG_y‖≤1/2 on a sufficiently small closed z-ball. Shrinking the y-ball makes G_y(z*) within half the z-radius, so the map takes that closed ball into itself. Iteration has consecutive distances bounded by 2^−k times the first distance and hence is Cauchy in R^q, converges to a fixed point, and the same bound gives uniqueness. Comparing fixed-point equations gives continuity and a local Lipschitz bound in y. Expanding F(y+h,z+δ)=F(y,z)+F_yh+F_zδ+o(‖(h,δ)‖), and using δ=O(‖h‖), gives Dz(y)=−F_z^−1F_y. The inverse is continuous by the adjugate/determinant formula, with determinant bounded away from zero. Thus the solution is C¹ and locally exactly the graph z(y). This proves the needed dimension n−q instead of citing rank counting alone.

**Nonreacting Gibbs rule.** Let C≥1 components occur with strictly positive mole fractions in each of P≥1 named phases. Each phase's composition is in the open simplex {x_j>0,Σx_j=1}, identified with R^(C−1) coordinates. Assume C¹ chemical potentials μ_j^α(T,p,x^α) in J/mol on a common open domain T>0, with P phase branches simultaneously admitted. Define F_(α,j)=μ_j^α−μ_j^1, α=2,…,P, j=1,…,C. The coordinate count is n=2+P(C−1); the constraint count is q=C(P−1). If DF has row rank q, M15 gives local intensive coexistence dimension n−q=C−P+2. Fractions of phase amounts are not included in this intensive count. Their feasible values have separate extensive conservation constraints. If q>n full row rank is impossible. Missing species, chemical-potential singularities at zero concentration, nontransferable components, externally fixed T or p, and critical coincidences change the model or rank and cannot inherit this count blindly.

**Reactions.** For ν∈R^(C×R) with independent allowed reaction columns and Aν=0 for the specified elemental conservation matrix A, write n=n^0+νξ with n_j≥0. At fixed T,p a C¹ Gibbs potential has derivative dG/dξ=νᵀμ. At an interior minimum it vanishes by both signs of feasible displacement. At a boundary the necessary condition is μ·νh≥0 for every feasible infinitesimal direction h (those with (νh)_j≥0 for each currently zero species). For differentiable convex G this condition is sufficient by its supporting inequality, since every feasible finite difference is in that tangent cone. The relevant number of components in a reacting phase rule is the number of independent conserved combinations, not the species count with reactions ignored. Alternatively start with species coordinates, add the R independent affinity equations νᵀμ^1=0 to the coexistence equations, and require their combined derivative to have row rank q+R; the same proof gives C−R−P+2. Equality of chemical potentials carries the other phases' affinity equations, so counting all phases' identical affinities again would be wrong.

For H₂,O₂,H₂O use A=((2,0,2),(0,2,1)); its rank is two and ker A is spanned by (−2,−1,2), so there is one independent reaction, not two. The calculation follows by solving 2h_1+2h_3=0,2h_2+h_3=0.

## M16. Countable canonical ensembles and diagonal quantum models

Fix a nonempty countable set I, finite energies e_i∈R and weights g_i>0; the counting model has g_i=1, while positive degeneracy/reference weights are explicitly chosen. To justify differentiation, integrate each finite derivative identity on [β_0,β]⊂K; the tail bound lets the integrals converge uniformly. The limiting continuous derivative integrates to the limiting function difference, so Newton–Leibniz yields Z'=−Σg_ie_ie^(−βe_i), Z''=Σg_ie_i²e^(−βe_i). Consequently p_i=g_ie^(−βe_i)/Z is normalized, −∂βlog Z=E and ∂β²log Z=Var(e). These are averages, not energy assignments to each individual outcome.

## M18. Exact thermodynamic limits in two specified models

For independent identically prepared finite subsystems with Ω_0 nonempty finite and energy e_i, Ω_N=Ω_0^N,H_N=Σe_(i_k), Z_N=Z_1^N. Hence the free energy per subsystem f_N=−β^−1N^−1log Z_N is exactly −β^−1log Z_1 at every N; all derivatives commute with this limit since the functions are identical. If one subsystem occupies volume v_0>0, V_N=Nv_0 fixes density N/V_N=1/v_0. Mean energy per subsystem is E_1 and variance of H_N/N is Var(e)/N: expansion of the product sum cancels all cross covariances. For ε>0, P(|H_N/N−E_1|≥ε)≤Var(e)/(Nε²), because the squared deviation is at least ε² on that event. Thus concentration in probability follows, without a universal independent-trial assertion.

A coupled example is the periodic one-dimensional Ising chain s_i∈{−1,1}, N≥2, s_(N+1)=s_1, H_N=−JΣs_is_(i+1)−hΣs_i, finite real J,h. Its eigenvalues are λ_±=e^(βJ)cosh(βh)±sqrt(e^(2βJ)sinh²(βh)+e^(−2βJ)); direct solution of the quadratic verifies this formula. Positive off-diagonal entries imply λ_+>0 and |λ_-|<λ_+: the trace is positive and the square root positive, so the sum and difference have this strict domination. Real symmetric 2×2 matrices diagonalize by explicitly choosing orthogonal eigenvectors (or if repeated, the scalar matrix); here the eigenvalues are distinct. Thus Z_N=λ_+^N(1+(λ_-/λ_+)^N), and N^−1log Z_N→log λ_+. On each compact finite-parameter set with β>0, continuity and strict domination bound |λ_-/λ_+|≤r<1. Differentiating the explicit correction gives polynomial-in-N factors times r^(N−k), divided by denominators bounded away from zero for large N; they tend uniformly to zero for each fixed derivative order. It does not prove the thermodynamic limit for arbitrary stable tempered interactions, infinite-volume Gibbs existence, or all ensemble equivalence.

## M21. Finite Markov entropy production, convergence and Onsager scope

Fix a finite graph with rates k_ij≥0 for i≠j and probability p_i(t)>0 solving p_i'=Σ_(j≠i)(p_jk_ji−p_ik_ij). Each summand is nonnegative by strict monotonicity of log. Equality means r_i=r_j along every positive-rate edge. ## M23. Local Pfaffian entropy route: two dimensions with explicit flow

## M26. General local codimension-one Pfaff–Frobenius theorem

Let ω=Σ_(i=1)^n a_i(x,z)dx_i+b(x,z)dz be a C² one-form near (0,z_0)∈R^(n+1), b(0,z_0)≠0. Suppose ω∧dω=0 throughout a neighborhood. ## M27. Measure-integral prerequisites and dominated differentiation

A measure space (Ω,F,m) means a set, a sigma-algebra (closed under complement and countable union), and a countably additive m:F→[0,∞] with m(∅)=0. For a nonnegative simple function s=Σc_j1_(A_j), with disjoint measurable A_j and c_j≥0, define ∫s=Σc_jm(A_j), with 0·∞=0. Common finite refinement proves representation-independence and additivity. For measurable f≥0 define ∫f=sup{∫s:0≤s≤f,s simple}; measurability means inverse intervals are measurable. Approximants s_n=2^(−n)floor(2^n min(f,n)) are measurable simple, nondecreasing and converge pointwise to f.

**Monotone convergence proved.** If f_n↑f≥0, the integrals increase to L≤∫f. For any simple s≤f and 0<t<1, the sets A_n={f_n≥ts} increase and cover {s>0}; on each level set of s, continuity from below of measure gives ∫s1_(A_n)→∫s. That measure continuity follows directly from decomposing an increasing sequence into disjoint successive differences and countable additivity. Since f_n≥ts1_(A_n), L≥t∫s. Taking t↑1 and then the supremum over s proves L≥∫f, including infinite values. Applying this to approximating simple functions proves additivity of nonnegative integrals: approximants to f,g have simple sums increasing to f+g, and the finite-sum integral identity passes to the limit.

**Fatou and domination proved.** For f_n≥0 put h_k=inf_(n≥k)f_n, a measurable function with h_k↑liminf f_n. Monotonicity and monotone convergence give ∫liminf f_n≤liminf∫f_n. If f_n→f pointwise, |f_n|≤g and ∫g<∞, apply Fatou to g+f_n and g−f_n; additivity for signed integrable functions follows by writing positive/negative parts and their nonnegative additivity. These two applications give both opposite inequalities for liminf/limsup∫f_n, hence ∫f_n→∫f. Applying this to |f_n−f|≤2g gives L¹ convergence as well. Null-set modifications have integral zero by the simple-function definition, so almost-everywhere hypotheses give the same statements after excluding their common countable null set.

For measurable fixed E:Ω→R, define Z(β)=∫e^(−βE)dm. Assume Z>0 and for each compact K inside an open β-interval there is integrable g_K dominating (1+|E|²)e^(−βE) for β∈K. Difference quotients for e^(−βE) are dominated by |E|sup_(β∈K)e^(−βE), by the scalar mean-value theorem. M27's dominated convergence therefore proves Z'=−∫Ee^(−βE), and repeating proves Z''=∫E²e^(−βE), both continuous by the same theorem. The normalized density p=e^(−βE)/Z is measurable and integrates to one. Energy mean/variance and reference-relative entropy −∫p log p are finite under these bounds and obey the identities of M16. This supplies the general classical measurable normalization/differentiation theorem, conditional on a specified reference measure and explicit dominating functions. It does not prove arbitrary interaction partition functions finite or invoke a hidden Lebesgue interchange theorem.

KL positivity for densities p,q with unit integral and q>0 where p>0 follows from −log(q/p)≥1−q/p, giving ∫p log(p/q)≥∫_(p>0)(p−q)≥0 whenever the integral is defined; the negative part is integrable because on p<q its magnitude is bounded by q. Differential entropy depends on the chosen reference measure; rescaling that measure shifts entropy and is not a measurable physical change.

## M30. Local Carathéodory inaccessibility implies Pfaff integrability

For a nonsingular C² one-form as in M26, call a curve horizontal if it is piecewise C¹ and ω(γ')=0 on each smooth piece.
