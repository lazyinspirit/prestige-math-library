## Definition

Let $(\Theta,\mathcal T)$ be a nonempty measurable parameter space and let $\vartheta:(\Theta,\mathcal T)\to(\mathbb R^k,\mathcal B(\mathbb R^k))$ be measurable. Let $X$ be the finite state set of the finite exponential Gibbs family from the A-page Definition, evaluated at canonical parameter $\vartheta(\theta)$; write its mass as $\pi_{\vartheta(\theta)}(x)$. A finite latent Gibbs observation model consists of a prior probability measure $\Pi$ on $(\Theta,\mathcal T)$ and a probability kernel $K$ from $(\Theta\times X,\mathcal T\otimes2^X)$ to a nonempty measurable data space $(\mathsf Y,\mathcal Y)$, where $2^X$ is the full power-set sigma-algebra on $X$ and $\mathcal T\otimes2^X$ is the product sigma-algebra. The kernel $K((\theta,x),B)$ is the observation law for data event $B$ when parameter $\theta$ and latent configuration $x$ are fixed. Define the marginal sampling kernel
$$Q(\theta,B):=\sum_{x\in X}\pi_{\vartheta(\theta)}(x)K((\theta,x),B)\qquad(B\in\mathcal Y).$$
Thus the latent configuration is summed out before the data likelihood is evaluated.

In a dominated observation model, a specified measure $\nu$ on $\mathsf Y$ is sigma-finite, meaning that $\mathsf Y$ is a countable union of measurable sets of finite $\nu$-measure. A nonnegative jointly measurable density $k(y\mid\theta,x)$ then satisfies
$$K((\theta,x),B)=\int_B k(y\mid\theta,x)\,\nu(dy),\qquad \int_{\mathsf Y}k(y\mid\theta,x)\,\nu(dy)=1.$$
The marginal data density, or likelihood density at $y$, is then
$$L(\theta,y):=\sum_{x\in X}\pi_{\vartheta(\theta)}(x)k(y\mid\theta,x).$$
The general kernel model does not require a pointwise likelihood density.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The finite exponential family, measurable parameter and data spaces, the proper prior, and the observation kernel in the Definition.

[F1] [[def-csm-frontier-inverse-finite-exponential]]: $X$ is finite, $u\mapsto\pi_u(x)$ is positive, normalized, and continuous for each $x$; composing with the measurable map $\vartheta$ makes $\theta\mapsto\pi_{\vartheta(\theta)}(x)$ measurable.

[F2] [[def-product-sigma-algebra-and-finite-product-sigma-algebras]]: $\mathcal T\otimes2^X$ is generated by measurable rectangles; for each fixed $x\in X$, the section map $\theta\mapsto(\theta,x)$ is measurable because the preimage of a rectangle $A\times D$ is $A$ if $x\in D$ and empty otherwise.

[F3] [[def-measure-kernel-and-probability-kernel]]: a probability kernel is a pointwise probability measure in its data argument and is measurable in its source argument for each fixed data event.

[F4] [[def-bayesian-model-as-prior-and-sampling-kernel]]: a proper Bayesian model consists of a prior probability measure and a parameter-measurable probability kernel from parameter space to data space.

## Proof

**Given:** The ingredients of the Definition.

1.1 For each fixed $\theta$, every summand in $Q(\theta,B)$ is nonnegative. For pairwise disjoint events $B_j$, countable additivity of each $K((\theta,x),\cdot)$ and the finiteness of $X$ let the finite sum over $x$ commute with the nonnegative series over $j$, so $Q(\theta,\cdot)$ is countably additive. Also $Q(\theta,\mathsf Y)=\sum_x\pi_{\vartheta(\theta)}(x)=1$. [given, F1, F3, algebra]

2.1 For each $B\in\mathcal Y$, $(\theta,x)\mapsto K((\theta,x),B)$ is measurable by [F3]; composing with each measurable section from [F2] makes $\theta\mapsto K((\theta,x),B)$ measurable. Also $\theta\mapsto\pi_{\vartheta(\theta)}(x)$ is measurable by [F1]. Therefore $Q(\theta,B)$ is a finite sum of products of measurable functions and is measurable in $\theta$. Thus $Q$ is a probability kernel and, with the proper prior $\Pi$, is a Bayesian model in the sense of [F4]. [given, F1, F2, F3, F4, step 1.1, algebra]

3.1 In the dominated case, each term $\pi_{\vartheta(\theta)}(x)k(y\mid\theta,x)$ is jointly measurable and nonnegative by the Definition. Its finite sum $L(\theta,y)$ is jointly measurable; integrating it over $B$ gives
$\int_B L(\theta,y)\nu(dy)=\sum_x\pi_{\vartheta(\theta)}(x)K((\theta,x),B)=Q(\theta,B)$.
For $B=\mathsf Y$, this integral is $1$ by step 1.1, so $L$ is exactly a density for the marginal sampling kernel, with latent states already summed out. [given, F1, step 1.1, algebra] ∎

## Remarks

A product of one-site conditional probabilities is a pseudo-likelihood, as defined separately on this page. It is a different inferential input from the joint-data kernel $Q$ above. The B-page finite counterexample shows that treating it as the exact likelihood can give a different posterior; the pseudo-likelihood product is therefore not substituted for $Q$ in this definition.
