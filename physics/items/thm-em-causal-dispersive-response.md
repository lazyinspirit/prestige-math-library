## Statement

Assume Countable Choice ([[def-countable-choice]]) for the smooth Riemann/Lebesgue comparison route below; in clause (c), also assume Dependent Choice ([[def-dependent-choice]]) inherited from the AC supplier. Let $d\ge1$. All kernels below are real $d\times d$ matrix kernels and all histories are $\mathbb R^d$-valued; conditions on matrices are read entrywise, every matrix- or vector-valued integral below is read entrywise as a scalar Lebesgue integral, and $L^1(0,\infty;\mathbb R^{d\times d})$ means entrywise Lebesgue integrability on $(0,\infty)$ with Lebesgue measure.

For a real-variable scalar function $F$ and real $\omega$, the **principal value** is defined by $$\operatorname{PV}\!\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{F(\xi)}{\xi-\omega}\,d\xi:=\lim_{R\to\infty}\lim_{\eta\downarrow0}\Bigl(\int_{\omega-R}^{\omega-\eta}+\int_{\omega+\eta}^{\omega+R}\Bigr)\frac{F(\xi)}{\xi-\omega}\,d\xi,$$ read entrywise when applied to matrix entries; under the bounds proved in (b) and (c) the tails converge absolutely, so this limit exists and the centering at infinity does not affect it.

(a) (Causal convolution.) Let $K\in L^1(0,\infty;\mathbb R^{d\times d})$ and let $E:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^d$ be bounded and continuous. Then for every $t\in\mathbb R$ the integral $P(t):=\int_0^\infty K(s)E(t-s)\,ds$ converges absolutely, read componentwise as $$P_j(t)=\int_0^\infty\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^dK_{jk}(s)E_k(t-s)\Bigr)ds\qquad(j=1,\dots,d),$$ and satisfies $$|P_j(t)|\le\sum_{k=1}^d\Bigl(\sup_{s\in\mathbb R}|E_k(s)|\Bigr)\int_0^\infty|K_{jk}(s)|\,ds\qquad(j=1,\dots,d);$$ the response $P$ is bounded and continuous, the map $E\mapsto P$ is linear, and it is causal: if $E_1,E_2$ are bounded continuous and coincide on $(-\infty,t_0]$, then their responses coincide on $(-\infty,t_0]$.

(b) (Compact branch.) The **support** of a scalar, vector or matrix map is the closure, in its domain, of the set where it is nonzero. If $K\in C_c^\infty((0,\infty);\mathbb R^{d\times d})$ (smooth, with compact support contained in the open half-line), then the transfer function $$\chi(z):=\int_0^\infty K(s)e^{izs}\,ds$$ is entire, satisfies $\chi(-\omega)=\overline{\chi(\omega)}$ for every real $\omega$, obeys $|\chi_{jk}(z)|\le\|K_{jk}''\|_{L^1(0,\infty)}|z|^{-2}$ for every $z\ne0$ with $\operatorname{Im}z\ge0$, and for any $b>0$ with $\operatorname{supp}K\subseteq(0,b)$ the full entire-plane bound is $|\chi_{jk}(z)|\le e^{b\max(0,-\operatorname{Im}z)}\|K_{jk}''\|_1|z|^{-2}$ for every $z\ne0$. Its entries satisfy the principal-value dispersion relations $$\operatorname{Re}\chi_{jk}(\omega)=\frac1\pi\operatorname{PV}\!\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{\operatorname{Im}\chi_{jk}(\xi)}{\xi-\omega}\,d\xi,\qquad \operatorname{Im}\chi_{jk}(\omega)=-\frac1\pi\operatorname{PV}\!\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{\operatorname{Re}\chi_{jk}(\xi)}{\xi-\omega}\,d\xi$$ for every real $\omega$.

(c) (Integrable branch.) If instead $K:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ is locally absolutely continuous entrywise in the sense of [[def-absolutely-continuous-function]] on every compact subinterval of $[0,\infty)$, with $K'$ its almost-everywhere derivative with $K,K'\in L^1(0,\infty;\mathbb R^{d\times d})$ and $s\mapsto sK(s)$ in $L^1(0,\infty;\mathbb R^{d\times d})$, then $\chi(z):=\int_0^\infty K(s)e^{izs}\,ds$ is well defined and holomorphic on the open upper half-plane $\{\operatorname{Im}z>0\}$, continuous on the closed upper half-plane with continuous first derivative on the real boundary, satisfies the same pair of dispersion relations for every real $\omega$, and is bounded on $\{\operatorname{Im}z\ge0\}$ by $|\chi_{jk}(z)|\le|K_{jk}(0)|+\|K_{jk}'\|_{L^1(0,\infty)}$ for $|z|\ge1$; for an instantaneous real response matrix $\chi_\infty\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ define $\chi_{\mathrm{total}}:=\chi_\infty+\chi$ and apply the relations to $\chi_{\mathrm{total}}-\chi_\infty=\chi$.

(d) (Monochromatic work and passivity.) Let $\omega>0$, $e\in\mathbb C^d$, and let $\varepsilon_0>0$ be a real parameter (a response normalization; the equivalence below is independent of its value). Put $E(t):=\operatorname{Re}(e\,e^{-i\omega t})$ and $P(t):=\operatorname{Re}(\varepsilon_0\chi(\omega)e\,e^{-i\omega t})$, real parts taken componentwise and $\chi(\omega)$ acting by matrix multiplication. Then, with $T:=2\pi/\omega$ and the Euclidean dot product on $\mathbb R^d$, $$\frac1T\int_0^T E(t)\cdot P'(t)\,dt=\frac{\varepsilon_0\omega}{2}\operatorname{Im}\bigl(e^*\chi(\omega)e\bigr),\qquad e^*\chi(\omega)e:=\sum_{j,k=1}^d\overline{e_j}\,\chi_{jk}(\omega)\,e_k,$$ where $\chi(\omega)^*$ denotes the conjugate transpose, $(\chi(\omega)^*)_{jk}:=\overline{\chi_{kj}(\omega)}$, a complex matrix $H$ is **Hermitian** when $H^*=H$, and a Hermitian $H$ is **positive semidefinite** when $e^*He\ge0$ for every $e\in\mathbb C^d$. This period-averaged work is nonnegative for every $e\in\mathbb C^d$ if and only if the Hermitian dissipative part $M(\omega):=(\chi(\omega)-\chi(\omega)^*)/(2i)$ satisfies $e^*M(\omega)e\ge0$ for every $e\in\mathbb C^d$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The Axiom of Countable Choice $\mathrm{AC}_\omega$ ([[def-countable-choice]]), and in clause (c) additionally Dependent Choice $\mathrm{DC}$ ([[def-dependent-choice]]); a fixed dimension $d\ge1$; a real $d\times d$ matrix kernel $K=(K_{jk})$ and an $\mathbb R^d$-valued history $E=(E_k)$; all matrix- and vector-valued integrals are read entrywise as scalar Lebesgue integrals on $(0,\infty)$. In clause (b), $K\in C_c^\infty((0,\infty);\mathbb R^{d\times d})$ with $\operatorname{supp}K\subseteq(0,b)$ for some real $b>0$; in clause (c) the kernel satisfies the integrable-branch hypotheses of [F14]; in clause (d), $\omega>0$, $T=2\pi/\omega$, $\varepsilon_0>0$, $e\in\mathbb C^d$, and $a:=\varepsilon_0\chi(\omega)e\in\mathbb C^d$.

[F1] A measurable $h=u+iv:\Omega\to\mathbb C$ is integrable exactly when $\int|h|\,d\mu<+\infty$, and then $\int h\,d\mu=\int u\,d\mu+i\int v\,d\mu$; $L^1(\mu)$ is the class of integrable complex functions. ([[def-integrable-real-and-complex-functions-and-their-integrals]], [[def-l-one-of-a-measure]])

[F2] For integrable functions $|\int f\,d\mu|\le\int|f|\,d\mu$; for measurable $0\le f\le g$ one has $\int f\le\int g$ and $\int cf=c\int f$ for $c\ge0$; the integral over a measurable set is $\int_E f=\int f\chi_E$; and the integral is complex-linear, $\int(\alpha f+\beta g)\,d\mu=\alpha\int f\,d\mu+\beta\int g\,d\mu$. ([[thm-integral-triangle-inequality]], [[prop-order-and-scalar-rules-for-the-nonnegative-integral]], [[def-integral-over-a-measurable-set]], [[thm-linearity-of-the-lebesgue-integral-on-l-one]])

[F3] Every continuous map $\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ is Borel measurable (under $\mathrm{AC}_\omega$), and sums, scalar multiples, absolute values and pointwise products of measurable functions are measurable. ([[cor-continuous-functions-are-borel-measurable]], [[thm-arithmetic-and-lattice-operations-preserve-measurability]])

[F4] Dominated convergence: if $f_n\to f$ almost everywhere and $|f_n|\le g$ almost everywhere for a single integrable $g$, then $\int f_n\to\int f$. ([[thm-dominated-convergence]])

[F5] Continuity under the integral sign: if $x\mapsto f(x,t)$ is integrable for every $t$ in an interval $I$, if $t\mapsto f(x,t)$ is continuous for almost every $x$, and if $|f(x,t)|\le g(x)$ for a single integrable $g$, then $t\mapsto\int f(x,t)\,d\mu(x)$ is continuous on $I$. ([[thm-continuity-under-the-integral-sign]])

[F6] Differentiation under the integral sign in a real parameter: under the hypotheses stated there (integrability for every parameter, almost-everywhere differentiability, measurability of the derivative and a single integrable dominating function for the derivative), $F(t)=\int f(x,t)\,d\mu(x)$ is differentiable with $F'(t)=\int\partial_tf(x,t)\,d\mu(x)$. ([[thm-differentiation-under-the-integral-sign]])

[F7] The complex exponential is defined by $\exp z=\sum_{n\ge0}z^n/n!$, that series converges absolutely for every $z$, $\exp$ is entire with $\exp'=\exp$ and hence continuous, and $|\exp(x+iy)|=e^x$ for real $x,y$; conjugation is a real-field automorphism with $z\overline z=|z|^2$, hence $|\overline z|=|z|$ and conjugation is continuous, while $\operatorname{Re}$, $\operatorname{Im}$ and $\overline{\phantom{z}}$ are the coordinate maps of [[def-complex-conjugate-real-imaginary-part-and-modulus]]. In particular $\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta})=\cos\theta$ and $\operatorname{Im}(e^{i\theta})=\sin\theta$ for real $\theta$, and $\operatorname{Re}(\overline w)=\operatorname{Re}w$. ([[def-complex-exponential]], [[lem-complex-exponential-series-converges-everywhere]], [[thm-complex-exponential-is-entire-with-derivative-itself]], [[cor-complex-differentiability-implies-continuity]], [[cor-complex-exponential-cartesian-form-modulus-and-eulers-identity]], [[lem-complex-conjugation-and-modulus-laws]], [[def-complex-conjugate-real-imaginary-part-and-modulus]])

[F8] If the four first partial derivatives of $u,v$ exist on a neighbourhood of $a$ and are continuous at $a$ with $u_x(a)=v_y(a)$ and $u_y(a)=-v_x(a)$, then $f=u+iv$ is complex differentiable at $a$; holomorphic means complex differentiable on an open set and entire means holomorphic on $\mathbb C$. ([[thm-continuous-partials-and-cauchy-riemann-imply-holomorphic]], [[def-complex-differentiability-holomorphic-and-entire]])

[F9] If $f$ is holomorphic on an open $\Omega\subseteq\mathbb C$ and the closed rectifiable contour $\gamma$ is path-homotopic relative to endpoints to a constant loop in $\Omega$, then $\int_\gamma f\,dz=0$. ([[cor-cauchy-theorem-for-null-homotopic-loops]])

[F10] Integration by parts: if $u,v$ are differentiable at every point of $[a,b]$ with integrable derivatives, then $\int_a^bu\,v'=u(b)v(b)-u(a)v(a)-\int_a^bu'v$; the bounded Riemann integrable case has the same Lebesgue integral; and integrable functions that agree almost everywhere have the same integral. ([[thm-integration-by-parts]], [[thm-bounded-riemann-integrable-functions-are-lebesgue-measurable-and-have-the-same-integral]], [[thm-the-lebesgue-integral-respects-almost-everywhere-equality]], [[def-ck-euclidean-maps-and-diffeomorphisms]])

[F11] The real sine and cosine are differentiable with $(\sin)'=\cos$ and $(\cos)'=-\sin$; the chain rule for total derivatives and the algebra of derivatives give the derivative of every finite combination $t\mapsto c_0+c_1\cos(\omega t)+c_2\sin(\omega t)$. ([[thm-sine-and-cosine-derivatives]], [[thm-chain-rule-for-total-derivatives]], [[thm-algebra-of-derivatives]])

[F12] On $\mathbb R^d$ the Euclidean inner product is $x\cdot y=\sum_{k=1}^d x_ky_k$ ([[def-euclidean-inner-product]]). On $\mathbb C^d$ its complex-bilinear extension is also denoted $x\cdot y=\sum_kx_ky_k$; this bilinear form is not used as a positive inner product. The conjugated pairing used below is $x\cdot\overline y=\sum_kx_k\overline{y_k}$, and $e^*a=\sum_k\overline{e_k}a_k$, with conjugation as in [F7].

[F13] Absolute continuity on a compact interval is the finite disjoint-interval condition of the cited definition; it is the exact hypothesis by which clause (c) reads its kernel, and no differentiability of the kernel beyond the stated almost-everywhere derivative is assumed. ([[def-absolutely-continuous-function]])

[F14] Integrable-branch supplier, consumed verbatim in clause (c): assume $\mathrm{AC}_\omega$ and $\mathrm{DC}$; for a locally absolutely continuous real matrix kernel $K$ with $K,K'\in L^1(0,\infty;\mathbb R^{d\times d})$ and $sK(s)\in L^1(0,\infty;\mathbb R^{d\times d})$, the transform $\chi(z)=\int_0^\infty K(s)e^{izs}ds$ has holomorphic entries on the upper half-plane, continuous and $C^1$ on the real boundary, is bounded on the closed upper half-plane with $|\chi_{jk}(z)|\le|K_{jk}(0)|+\|K_{jk}'\|_1$ for $|z|\ge1$ (indeed $|\chi_{jk}(z)|\le(|K_{jk}(0)|+\|K_{jk}'\|_1)/|z|$ for every nonzero $z$ with $\operatorname{Im}z\ge0$), satisfies the two displayed principal-value formulas for every real $\omega$, and carries the instantaneous-response subtraction for a real matrix $\chi_\infty$; causality alone supplies neither further growth hypotheses nor the passivity quadratic-form condition. ([[lem-em-integrable-causal-dispersion]])

[F15] Period-average supplier, consumed in clause (d) in its scalar form applied componentwise: for $\omega>0$, $T=2\pi/\omega$ and all $a,d\in\mathbb C$, $$\frac1T\int_0^T\operatorname{Re}\bigl(ae^{-i\omega t}\bigr)\operatorname{Re}\bigl(de^{-i\omega t}\bigr)\,dt=\frac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(a\overline d\bigr);$$ the vector form follows by summing this over the coordinate pairs of $a,d\in\mathbb C^d$. ([[lem-em-period-average]])

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct; the steps are ordered in dependency layers, so the four clauses of the Statement are interleaved, and every step cites only earlier steps.

1.1 Fix $t\in\mathbb R$, $j\in\{1,\dots,d\}$ and set $g_j(s):=\sum_{k=1}^dK_{jk}(s)E_k(t-s)$ for $s>0$. Each summand is a product of the measurable function $K_{jk}$ with the continuous, hence Borel measurable, function $s\mapsto E_k(t-s)$; so $g_j$ is measurable. With $M:=\max_k\sup_{\mathbb R}|E_k|<+\infty$ one has $|g_j(s)|\le M\sum_{k=1}^d|K_{jk}(s)|$ for every $s>0$, and $\sum_{k=1}^d|K_{jk}|$ lies in $L^1(0,\infty)$; hence $g_j\in L^1(0,\infty)$ and $P_j(t):=\int_0^\infty g_j(s)\,ds$ converges absolutely. The termwise bound $|g_j(s)|\le\sum_{k=1}^d(\sup_{\mathbb R}|E_k|)|K_{jk}(s)|$ gives the exact stated estimate $|P_j(t)|\le\sum_{k=1}^d(\sup_{\mathbb R}|E_k|)\int_0^\infty|K_{jk}(s)|\,ds\le M\sum_{k=1}^d\int_0^\infty|K_{jk}(s)|\,ds$. [F1, F2, F3]

1.2 Fix $z=x+iy\in\mathbb C$. The map $s\mapsto e^{izs}$ is continuous, since it is the composition of the continuous linear map $s\mapsto izs$ with the continuous exponential, and $K_{jk}$ is measurable; hence $s\mapsto K_{jk}(s)e^{izs}$ is measurable as a product of measurable functions. On $\operatorname{supp}K\subseteq(0,b)$ one has $|e^{izs}|=|e^{ixs}|e^{-ys}=e^{-ys}\le e^{b|y|}$, so $|K_{jk}(s)e^{izs}|\le e^{b|y|}|K_{jk}(s)|$; the right-hand side is integrable, so the integrand is integrable and $\chi_{jk}(z):=\int_0^\infty K_{jk}(s)e^{izs}\,ds$ is well defined with $|\chi_{jk}(z)|\le e^{b|y|}\|K_{jk}\|_1$. [F1, F3, F7]

1.3 Clause (c) is exactly the supplied integrable-branch theorem [F14]: its hypotheses on $K$ are those stated in clause (c) (local absolute continuity entrywise in the sense of [F13], $K,K',sK\in L^1$), and it concludes the well-definedness and holomorphy of $\chi$ on the open upper half-plane, continuity on the closed upper half-plane and continuous first derivative on the real boundary, the entrywise principal-value formulas for every real $\omega$, and the bound $|\chi_{jk}(z)|\le|K_{jk}(0)|+\|K_{jk}'\|_1$ for $|z|\ge1$ (which follows from its sharper $|z|^{-1}$ bound), together with the instantaneous-response subtraction for a real matrix $\chi_\infty$ and the scalar case $d=1$. Continuity on the closed upper half-plane combines the lemma's holomorphy on the open half-plane, hence continuity there, with its continuity at every real boundary point. [F14, F13, F8]

1.4 Write $a_j=\alpha_j+i\beta_j$ and $e_j=p_j+iq_j$ for the components of $a=\varepsilon_0\chi(\omega)e$ and $e$. By [F7], $e^{i\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+i\sin(\omega t)$, and $\operatorname{Re}(w)=\operatorname{Re}(\overline w)$ for every $w\in\mathbb C$; therefore $E_j(t)=\operatorname{Re}(e_je^{-i\omega t})=\operatorname{Re}(\overline{e_j}e^{i\omega t})=p_j\cos(\omega t)+q_j\sin(\omega t)$ and $P_j(t)=\operatorname{Re}(a_je^{-i\omega t})=\operatorname{Re}(\overline{a_j}e^{i\omega t})=\alpha_j\cos(\omega t)+\beta_j\sin(\omega t)$, using the component computation $\operatorname{Re}((u+iv)(\cos+i\sin))=u\cos-v\sin$. [F7, F12]

2.1 The estimate of step 1.1 is uniform in $t$, so $\sup_{t\in\mathbb R}|P_j(t)|\le M\sum_{k=1}^d\|K_{jk}\|_1$ and $P=(P_1,\dots,P_d)$ is bounded. The map $E\mapsto P$ is linear: for $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb R$ and bounded continuous $E,F$ the integrand defining $P_{\alpha E+\beta F}$ equals $\alpha$ times that of $P_E$ plus $\beta$ times that of $P_F$ pointwise, so the linearity of the scalar integral gives $P_{\alpha E+\beta F}=\alpha P_E+\beta P_F$. [step 1.1, F2, F1]

2.2 Fix $j$ and apply [F5] with $X=(0,\infty)$ carrying Lebesgue measure, $I=\mathbb R$ and $f(s,t):=\sum_{k=1}^dK_{jk}(s)E_k(t-s)$: for every $t$ the function $f(\cdot,t)$ is integrable by step 1.1; for every $s$ the map $t\mapsto f(s,t)$ is continuous, being a finite sum of products of constants with the continuous functions $t\mapsto E_k(t-s)$; and $|f(s,t)|\le M\sum_{k=1}^d|K_{jk}(s)|$ for the single integrable function named in step 1.1. Therefore $t\mapsto P_j(t)$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$, and $P$ is continuous componentwise. [step 1.1, F5, F3]

2.3 Causality: if $E_1,E_2$ are bounded continuous and agree on $(-\infty,t_0]$, and if $t\le t_0$, then for every $s>0$ the argument $t-s$ lies in $(-\infty,t_0]$, so the integrands of step 1.1 agree pointwise and hence $P_{E_1}(t)=P_{E_2}(t)$; thus the response at time $t$ depends only on the history up to time $t$. [step 1.1, F1]

2.4 Fix $y$ and apply [F6] in the parameter $x$ to $f(s,x):=K_{jk}(s)e^{i(x+iy)s}$ on a bounded interval of $x$-values: for each $x$ the function $f(\cdot,x)$ is integrable by step 1.2, the map $x\mapsto K_{jk}(s)e^{i(x+iy)s}$ is differentiable for every $s$ with derivative $isK_{jk}(s)e^{i(x+iy)s}$, and on the bounded parameter set this derivative is dominated by $(1+b)e^{b|y|}|K_{jk}(s)|$, an integrable function. Hence $x\mapsto\chi_{jk}(x+iy)$ is differentiable with $\partial_x\chi_{jk}(z)=\int_0^\infty isK_{jk}(s)e^{izs}\,ds$; exchanging the roles of $x$ and $y$ and repeating the same argument gives $\partial_y\chi_{jk}(z)=\int_0^\infty(-s)K_{jk}(s)e^{izs}\,ds$. Both partials are continuous in $(x,y)$: if $(x_n,y_n)\to(x,y)$, the integrands $isK_{jk}(s)e^{i(x_n+iy_n)s}$ converge pointwise to $isK_{jk}(s)e^{izs}$ and are dominated on a bounded parameter neighbourhood by $(1+b)e^{bY}|K_{jk}(s)|$ for a suitable $Y$, so [F4] gives convergence of the integrals. [step 1.2, F6, F4, F3]

2.5 Reality identity: for real $\omega$, conjugating the partial sums of the absolutely convergent series defining $e^{i\omega s}$ term by term gives $\sum_{n\le m}\overline{(i\omega s)^n/n!}=\sum_{n\le m}(-i\omega s)^n/n!$, because conjugation is a continuous real-field automorphism with $\overline i=-i$ and fixes the real numbers $1/n!$; passing to the limit with continuity of conjugation gives $\overline{e^{i\omega s}}=e^{-i\omega s}$. Since $K_{jk}$ is real, $\overline{K_{jk}(s)e^{i\omega s}}=K_{jk}(s)e^{-i\omega s}$. For $h:=K_{jk}(\cdot)e^{i\omega\cdot}\in L^1(0,\infty)$ one has $\overline{\int h}=(\int\operatorname{Re}h)-i(\int\operatorname{Im}h)=\int(\operatorname{Re}h-i\operatorname{Im}h)=\int\overline h$ by [F1] and the complex linearity of the integral; hence $\chi_{jk}(-\omega)=\int_0^\infty K_{jk}(s)e^{-i\omega s}ds=\int\overline h=\overline{\int h}=\overline{\chi_{jk}(\omega)}$. [step 1.2, F1, F2, F7]

2.6 By-parts identity: fix $z\ne0$. On $[0,b]$ the real and imaginary parts of $s\mapsto K_{jk}(s)$ and of $v(s):=e^{izs}/(iz)$ are differentiable with continuous derivatives, and $K_{jk}(0)=K_{jk}(b)=K_{jk}'(0)=K_{jk}'(b)=0$ because $\operatorname{supp}K\subseteq(0,b)$; applying [F10] first with $u=K_{jk}$ and then with $u=K_{jk}'$ gives $\int_0^bK_{jk}(s)e^{izs}ds=-\frac1{iz}\int_0^bK_{jk}'(s)e^{izs}ds=\frac1{(iz)^2}\int_0^bK_{jk}''(s)e^{izs}ds=-z^{-2}\int_0^bK_{jk}''(s)e^{izs}ds$, the boundary terms vanishing at both steps. All three integrands are bounded and Riemann integrable on $[0,b]$, so [F10] transfers the identity to Lebesgue integrals; and since $K_{jk}$, $K_{jk}'$ and $K_{jk}''$ all vanish off $(0,b)$, the a.e.-equality clause of [F10] replaces each integral over $[0,b]$ by the integral over $(0,\infty)$. Hence $\chi_{jk}(z)=-z^{-2}\int_0^\infty K_{jk}''(s)e^{izs}ds$ for every $z\ne0$. [step 1.2, F10, F2, F7]

2.7 Differentiating the expressions of step 1.4 and using [F11] together with the algebra of derivatives gives $P_j'(t)=\omega(-\alpha_j\sin(\omega t)+\beta_j\cos(\omega t))$; on the other hand, expanding $(-i\omega)(\alpha_j+i\beta_j)= \omega\beta_j-i\omega\alpha_j$ and multiplying by $e^{-i\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)-i\sin(\omega t)$ shows that the real part of $(-i\omega)a_je^{-i\omega t}$ equals $\omega\beta_j\cos(\omega t)-\omega\alpha_j\sin(\omega t)$, the same number. Hence $P'(t)=\operatorname{Re}((-i\omega)a\,e^{-i\omega t})=\operatorname{Re}((-i\omega)\varepsilon_0\chi(\omega)e\,e^{-i\omega t})$ componentwise, and $P'$ is the real part of a monochromatic complex expression with vector amplitude $d:=(-i\omega)\varepsilon_0\chi(\omega)e$. 3.1 By step 2.4 and the linearity of the integral, $\partial_y\chi_{jk}(z)=\int_0^\infty(-s)K_{jk}(s)e^{izs}\,ds=\int_0^\infty i\bigl(isK_{jk}(s)e^{izs}\bigr)ds=i\,\partial_x\chi_{jk}(z)$ for every $z$. Writing $\chi_{jk}=u+iv$, this says $u_y+iv_y=i(u_x+iv_x)=-v_x+iu_x$, that is $u_y=-v_x$ and $v_y=u_x$; the four partials exist everywhere and are continuous by step 2.4, so [F8] makes $\chi_{jk}$ complex differentiable at every point of $\mathbb C$, that is, entire. [step 2.4, F2, F8]

3.2 Bounds and boundary continuity: for $z\ne0$ with $\operatorname{Im}z\ge0$, step 2.6 and $|e^{izs}|\le1$ for $s\ge0$ give $|\chi_{jk}(z)|\le|z|^{-2}\|K_{jk}''\|_1$; for arbitrary $z\ne0$ one has $|e^{izs}|=e^{-s\operatorname{Im}z}\le e^{b\max(0,-\operatorname{Im}z)}$ on $\operatorname{supp}K\subseteq(0,b)$, whence $|\chi_{jk}(z)|\le e^{b\max(0,-\operatorname{Im}z)}\|K_{jk}''\|_1|z|^{-2}$. Also $\chi_{jk}$ is continuous on the closed upper half-plane: if $z_n\to z$ with $\operatorname{Im}z_n\ge0$, the integrands of step 1.2 converge pointwise and are dominated by $e^{bY}|K_{jk}|$ once all $\operatorname{Im}z_n$ lie below a bound $Y$, so [F4] gives $\chi_{jk}(z_n)\to\chi_{jk}(z)$. 3.3 Applying the period-average identity of [F15] with amplitude vectors $a=e$ and $d=(-i\omega)\varepsilon_0\chi(\omega)e$ componentwise, and using step 2.7, $\frac1T\int_0^TE(t)\cdot P'(t)\,dt=\sum_{j=1}^d\frac1T\int_0^T\operatorname{Re}(e_je^{-i\omega t})\operatorname{Re}(d_je^{-i\omega t})\,dt=\sum_{j=1}^d\frac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(e_j\overline{d_j}\bigr)=\frac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(e\cdot\overline d\bigr)$. 4.1 Dispersion relations: fix real $\omega$ and $j,k$, and put $\varphi(z):=\chi_{jk}(z)/(z-\omega)$ for $z\ne\omega$. For $R>|\omega|+1$ and $0<\delta<1$ let $\gamma$ be the closed piecewise $C^1$ contour running along $[\omega-R,\omega-\delta]$, then along the clockwise semicircle $z=\omega+\delta e^{i\theta}$, $\pi\ge\theta\ge0$, then along $[\omega+\delta,\omega+R]$, and then back along the counterclockwise large semicircle $z=\omega+Re^{i\theta}$, $0\le\theta\le\pi$. The contour lies in the slit plane $D:=\mathbb C\setminus\{\omega-iv:v\ge0\}$, where $\varphi$ is holomorphic because $\chi_{jk}$ is entire and $z\mapsto(z-\omega)^{-1}$ is holomorphic off $\omega$. This domain is star-shaped about $\omega+iA$ for every $A>0$: along a segment from this centre to a point off the removed ray, either its imaginary part stays positive or its real part differs from $\omega$ except at the centre. The straight contraction to the centre, with the base-point connector and its reverse, therefore gives a null homotopy relative to the base point. Hence [F9] gives $\int_\gamma\varphi\,dz=0$. The large arc contributes at most $\pi\|K_{jk}''\|_1/(R-|\omega|)^2\to0$ by step 3.2; the small arc contributes $-i\pi\chi_{jk}(\omega)+o(1)$ as $\delta\downarrow0$ because $\chi_{jk}(\omega+\delta e^{i\theta})\to\chi_{jk}(\omega)$ uniformly in $\theta$; and the two real segments converge to the principal value of $\int_{\mathbb R}\chi_{jk}(\xi)/(\xi-\omega)d\xi$ as first $\delta\downarrow0$ and then $R\to\infty$: near $\omega$ the term $\chi_{jk}(\omega)/(\xi-\omega)$ cancels between the symmetric punctured intervals while $(\chi_{jk}(\xi)-\chi_{jk}(\omega))/(\xi-\omega)$ stays bounded, and for $|\xi|\ge\max(1,2|\omega|)$ the estimate $|\chi_{jk}(\xi)/(\xi-\omega)|\le2\|K_{jk}''\|_1|\xi|^{-2}$ makes the tails absolutely integrable. Passing to the limits in the identity $0=\int_\gamma\varphi\,dz$ therefore yields $\operatorname{PV}\int_{\mathbb R}\chi_{jk}(\xi)/(\xi-\omega)d\xi=i\pi\chi_{jk}(\omega)$; taking real and imaginary parts gives the two displayed relations, since $-\pi\operatorname{Im}\chi_{jk}(\omega)$ and $\pi\operatorname{Re}\chi_{jk}(\omega)$ are respectively the real and imaginary parts of $i\pi\chi_{jk}(\omega)$. [step 3.1, step 3.2, F9, F4, F2]

5.1 Finally $M(\omega)^*=(\chi(\omega)^*-\chi(\omega))/(-2i)=M(\omega)$, so $M(\omega)$ is Hermitian, and $M(\omega)=(\chi(\omega)-\chi(\omega)^*)/(2i)$ has quadratic form $e^*M(\omega)e=\bigl(e^*\chi(\omega)e-e^*\chi(\omega)^*e\bigr)/(2i)$, and $e^*\chi(\omega)^*e=\sum_{j,k}\overline{e_j}\overline{\chi_{kj}(\omega)}e_k=\overline{e^*\chi(\omega)e}$ because $\chi(\omega)^*$ is the conjugate transpose; hence $e^*M(\omega)e=\operatorname{Im}(e^*\chi(\omega)e)$ for every $e\in\mathbb C^d$. By step 3.3 the period-averaged work equals $\frac{\varepsilon_0\omega}{2}e^*M(\omega)e$ with $\frac{\varepsilon_0\omega}{2}>0$, so it is nonnegative for every $e$ if and only if $e^*M(\omega)e\ge0$ for every $e$; clause (a) establishes causality without assuming this positivity condition.
