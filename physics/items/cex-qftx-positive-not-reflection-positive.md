## Statement refuted

A positive probability measure with a positive-definite ordinary covariance matrix must be reflection positive for the involution exchanging two sites.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Fix a real parameter $r$ with $0<r<1$. On the four-point set $\{(-1,-1),(-1,1),(1,-1),(1,1)\}$ assign probabilities

$$
\mathbb P(X=1,Y=1)=\mathbb P(X=-1,Y=-1)=\frac{1-r}{4},\qquad \mathbb P(X=1,Y=-1)=\mathbb P(X=-1,Y=1)=\frac{1+r}{4}.
$$

On the complex algebra of functions on this four-point space, generated by the coordinate functions $X,Y$ with $X^2=Y^2=1$, define the conjugate-linear map by $\Theta(c1)=\bar c1$, $\Theta X=Y$, $\Theta Y=X$, and $\Theta(ab)=\Theta(b)\Theta(a)$. These rules give an involution because they preserve the relations and square to the identity on scalars and generators. Define the positive-site polynomial algebra to be the unital subalgebra generated by $Y$.

[F1] [[def-qftx-euclidean-limit-data]]: ordinary positivity tests $\mathbb E[\bar F F]\ge0$, while reflection positivity tests $\mathbb E[(\Theta F)F]$ for positive-site polynomials.

## Counterexample

1.1 All four weights are positive and sum to $(1-r)/2+(1+r)/2=1$. Symmetry of the support weights gives $\mathbb E[X]=\mathbb E[Y]=0$, and $X^2=Y^2=1$ at every support point, so the covariance diagonal is $(1,1)$. The equal-sign outcomes have total probability $(1-r)/2$ and product $XY=1$; the opposite-sign outcomes have total probability $(1+r)/2$ and product $XY=-1$. Therefore $\mathbb E[XY]=(1-r)/2-(1+r)/2=-r$. [given, algebra]

2.1 The covariance matrix is $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&-r\\-r&1\end{pmatrix}$. For real $u,v$, $(u,v)C(u,v)^{\mathsf T}=(u-rv)^2+(1-r^2)v^2,$ which is strictly positive unless $(u,v)=(0,0)$ because $0<r<1$. Thus $C$ is positive definite and the probability law is ordinarily positive. Since $Y$ belongs to the declared positive-site algebra, take $F=Y$. Reflection positivity would require $\mathbb E[(\Theta F)F]=\mathbb E[XY]\ge0$, but this is $-r<0$. Hence the statement is false. [F1, given, step 1.1, algebra] ∎
