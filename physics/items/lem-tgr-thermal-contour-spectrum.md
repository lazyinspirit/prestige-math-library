## Definitions and conventions

Use $\sinh w=(e^w-e^{-w})/2$, $\cosh w=(e^w+e^{-w})/2$, and
$\operatorname{csch}w=1/\sinh w$ where defined, as fixed in
[[lem-tgr-exponential-current]]. The same supplier states their derivatives and
that real $\cosh$ is positive. A function on an open subset of $\mathbb C$ is
holomorphic when it is complex differentiable at every point, as defined in
[[def-complex-differentiability-holomorphic-and-entire]]. For the real line,
$\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$ consists of all $\varphi\in C^\infty(\mathbb R;\mathbb C)$
with $p_{m,n}(\varphi)=\sup_s|s^m\varphi^{(n)}(s)|<\infty$ for every
$m,n\in\mathbb N_0$; its topology is generated by these seminorms. A tempered
distribution is a continuous complex-linear functional on this topological
space, with bilinear test pairing, as defined in
[[def-schwartz-space-and-its-seminorms]],
[[def-schwartz-topology-and-convergence]], and
[[def-tempered-distribution]].

“Chiral current” refers to the current of [[lem-tgr-exponential-current]],
whose one-particle space has exactly the positive-frequency sector $k>0$.
In the phrase “analytic kernel,” analytic means locally represented by a
convergent complex power series on the set where $\sinh(bz)\ne0$. Indeed,
$\sinh(bz)$ is analytic by its exponential series; near any such $z_0$, put
$a=\sinh(bz_0)$ and $q(z)=(\sinh(bz)-a)/a$. On a sufficiently small disk
$|q(z)|<1$, and
$$\frac1{\sinh^2(bz)}=a^{-2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty(-1)^n(n+1)q(z)^n,$$
which is a convergent local power series.

For the final scope sentence, a norm-continuous C*-algebra KMS system at
$\theta>0$ means a complex Banach $*$-algebra $\mathcal A$ satisfying
$\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2$, a group $t\mapsto\alpha_t$ of bijective
$*$-homomorphisms for which
$t\mapsto\alpha_t(a)$ is norm-continuous for each $a\in\mathcal A$, and a
state $\rho$ (a positive linear functional of norm one, with $\rho(1)=1$
when $\mathcal A$ is unital) such that for every $a,b\in\mathcal A$ there is a bounded function
$F_{a,b}$, continuous on $0\le\operatorname{Im}z\le\theta$ and holomorphic on
the interior, with $F_{a,b}(t)=\rho(a\alpha_t(b))$ and
$F_{a,b}(t+i\theta)=\rho(\alpha_t(b)a)$. A detector transition probability
would additionally require a specified detector, coupled initial state and
measurement outcome. Neither structure is part of the present two-point
Fourier calculation.

## Statement

Assume Countable Choice. Let $A,b>0$, put $\beta=\pi/b$ and $L=A/b^2$, and define the analytic kernel
$$C(z)=-A\operatorname{csch}^2(bz),\qquad \operatorname{csch}w=1/\sinh w.$$
Use the angular-frequency convention
$$\widehat\varphi(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb R}\varphi(s)e^{-i\omega s}\,ds,\qquad \varphi(s)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\widehat\varphi(\omega)e^{i\omega s}\,d\omega$$
for Schwartz functions $\varphi\in\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$, where Schwartz space has seminorms $\sup_s|s^m\varphi^{(n)}(s)|$ and a tempered distribution is a continuous complex-linear functional on that space. Define its Fourier transform by the bilinear pairing $\langle\widehat T,\varphi\rangle=\langle T,\widehat\varphi\rangle$; test functions are not conjugated.

For $0<\epsilon<\beta$, $C_\epsilon(s)=C(s-i\epsilon)$ is a smooth $L^1$ function. These regularizations have a limit $C_{A,b}\in\mathcal S'(\mathbb R)$ as $\epsilon\downarrow0$, denoted $-A\operatorname{csch}^2(b(s-i0))$. The kernel is holomorphic on $-\beta<\operatorname{Im}z<0$, has period $i\beta$, and its tested boundary values obey
$$C(s-i\beta+i0)=C(-s-i0).$$
The Fourier transform of $C_{A,b}$ is the continuous nonnegative function
$$S_{A,b}(\omega)=\frac{2\pi(A/b^2)\omega}{e^{\pi\omega/b}-1},\qquad S_{A,b}(0)=\frac{2\pi L}{\beta}=\frac{2A}{b}.$$
In particular $S_{A,b}(-\omega)=e^{\beta\omega}S_{A,b}(\omega)$ for $\omega>0$. The zero-frequency value is the removable limit, and the boundary and Fourier assertions are distributional, not pointwise integrals at a double pole.

If the coordinate has a specified unit, $s,z,\beta,\epsilon$ have that unit and $b,\omega$ its reciprocal. If $A$ has covariance units, $L$ has those units times the square of the coordinate unit and $S_{A,b}$ has those units times the coordinate unit.

In particular, for $\kappa>0$ in inverse length, take $A=\kappa^2/(16\pi)$ and $b=\kappa/2$. Then $L=1/(4\pi)$, $\beta=2\pi/\kappa$, and $C_{A,b}$ is precisely the tested chiral covariance $C_\kappa$ of [[lem-tgr-exponential-current]]. Its spectrum and detailed balance are
$$S_\kappa(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{2(e^{2\pi\omega/\kappa}-1)},\qquad S_\kappa(0)=\frac{\kappa}{4\pi},\qquad S_\kappa(-\omega)=e^{2\pi\omega/\kappa}S_\kappa(\omega)\quad(\omega>0).$$
Here $C_\kappa$ has units of inverse length squared and $S_\kappa$ inverse length. This is a two-point strip and detailed-balance statement, including the specified chiral current as a specialization. It does not assert a norm-continuous $C^*$-algebra KMS dynamics or an exact detector transition probability.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Countable Choice; $A,b>0$; $\beta=\pi/b$ and $L=A/b^2$; and the Fourier conventions in the Statement. For the chiral specialization, also use $\kappa>0$ and the current covariance from [[lem-tgr-exponential-current]].

[A1] The Countable Choice assumption used by the test, Fock, and Fourier suppliers is retained. [[def-countable-choice]]

[F1] [[lem-tgr-exponential-current]] gives the tested covariance $C_\kappa(s)$ as the lower boundary of $C(z)=-(\kappa^2/16\pi)\operatorname{csch}^2(\kappa z/2)$, including the test-coordinate convention for $i0$.

[[thm-fourier-transform-maps-schwartz-space-continuously-to-itself]] preserves that test space. [[thm-fourier-inversion-on-schwartz-space]] gives inversion with cyclic frequency $e^{-2\pi i s\xi}$; setting $\omega=2\pi\xi$ gives the angular convention in the Statement and determines tempered distributions by their Fourier transforms.

[F3] [[cor-cauchy-theorem-for-null-homotopic-loops]]: a holomorphic function has zero line integral around a closed rectifiable contour path-homotopic to a constant loop in its open domain.

[F4] [[def-complex-line-integral-over-a-rectifiable-path]] defines the contour integral and [[thm-existence-of-complex-line-integrals-on-rectifiable-paths]] proves its existence for continuous integrands. [[thm-fundamental-theorem-for-complex-line-integrals]] gives the endpoint increment for a function with a continuous primitive derivative.

[F5] The complex exponential is entire with derivative itself; it obeys addition, Euler's identity $e^{i\pi}=-1$, and $e^z=e^w$ exactly when $z-w\in2\pi i\mathbb Z$. [[def-complex-exponential]] [[thm-complex-exponential-is-entire-with-derivative-itself]] [[thm-complex-exponential-addition-and-real-extension]] [[cor-complex-exponential-cartesian-form-modulus-and-eulers-identity]] [[thm-kernel-and-fibres-of-complex-exponential]]

[F6] [[thm-sine-cosine-zero-sets-and-fundamental-period]] gives the real period and zero sets used in the exponential kernel characterization; [[cor-exponential-reciprocal-and-positivity]] gives the reciprocal identity for real exponentials.

[F7] [[def-complex-differentiability-holomorphic-and-entire]] defines complex differentiability by the difference quotient. If the quotient has a finite limit at $z$, then it is bounded near $z$, and $f(z+h)-f(z)=h(f(z+h)-f(z))/h\to 0$; thus complex differentiability implies continuity at $z$. The power-series theorem [[thm-termwise-differentiation-of-complex-power-series]] gives complex differentiability of the displayed series for $q$, and [[thm-chain-rule-for-total-derivatives]] gives derivatives of compositions.

[F8] [[thm-dominated-convergence]] applies to the test-paired damped Fourier integrals. [[thm-integration-by-parts]] supplies rapid decay of Fourier transforms of compact smooth tests when applied to real and imaginary parts. [F9] [[thm-fubini-theorem-for-l-one-on-sigma-finite-product-spaces]] permits changing integration order when pairing an $L^1$ damped kernel with an $L^1$ Schwartz Fourier transform.

## Proof

1.1 Write $\sinh w=(e^w-e^{-w})/2$ and $\operatorname{csch}w=1/\sinh w$ where the denominator is nonzero. From [F5]–[F6], $\sinh(w+i\pi)=-\sinh w$, $\sinh(-w)=-\sinh(w)$, and $\sinh w=0$ exactly when $w\in i\pi\mathbb Z$. Thus $C(z)$ has poles only at $z=in\beta$ ($n\in\mathbb Z$). It is holomorphic on the stated strip: $\sinh$ is holomorphic by [F5] and the chain rule; where it is nonzero, its reciprocal is complex differentiable because
$$\frac{1/\sinh(w+h)-1/\sinh(w)}{h}=-\frac{\sinh(w+h)-\sinh(w)}{h\sinh(w+h)\sinh(w)}$$
converges as $h\to0$, using continuity from [F7]. The exponential addition law gives period $i\beta$, and oddness of $\sinh$ gives evenness of $C$. These are identities of the general analytic kernel; their distributional boundary interpretation follows once the boundary has been constructed. [A1, F5, F6, F7, given, algebra]

1.2 Set $z_0=-i\beta$ and $\delta=z-z_0$. Since $bz_0=-i\pi$, $\sinh(bz)=-b\delta q(\delta)$, where $q(\delta)=\sinh(b\delta)/(b\delta)$ for $\delta\ne0$ and $q(0)=1$. Its power series $q(\delta)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(b\delta)^{2m}/(2m+1)!$ converges for all $\delta$ as a subseries of the complex exponential series. The termwise-differentiation theorem in [F7] makes $q$ holomorphic and its displayed series makes it even. Since $q(0)=1$, continuity from [F7] makes $q$ nonzero on a small disk; the reciprocal difference quotient using [F7] makes $q^{-2}$ holomorphic there, and its even series is $1+O(\delta^2)$. Therefore
$$C(z)=-\frac{L}{(z-z_0)^2}+H(z),\qquad L=\frac{A}{b^2},$$
where $H(z)$ is holomorphic at $z_0$, with no simple-pole term. [F5, F7, algebra]

2.1 Fix $\omega\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$ and $0<\epsilon<\beta$. Integrate $e^{-i\omega z}C(z)$ around the clockwise rectangle with top from $-R-i\epsilon$ to $R-i\epsilon$ and bottom at imaginary part $-(\beta+\epsilon)$. Its interior lies in $-2\beta<\operatorname{Im}z<0$ and contains only $z_0$. The holomorphic part $e^{-i\omega z}H(z)$ integrates to zero by [F3] on a slightly enlarged convex rectangle: its closure contains the contour, and convexity contracts the closed contour to a constant loop inside that open domain. For the double-pole term, the function $(e^{-i\omega z}-e^{-i\omega z_0})/(z-z_0)$ extends holomorphically at $z_0$, so [F3] gives zero for its contour integral. To evaluate the remaining simple pole, write $a=\beta-\epsilon>0$ and $c=\epsilon>0$. Relative to $z_0$, the rectangle's vertices are $(-R,a),(R,a),(R,-c),(-R,-c)$. With $\theta_a=\arctan(a/R)$ and $\theta_c=\arctan(c/R)$, the continuous argument changes along its four clockwise sides by $-\pi+2\theta_a$, $-\theta_a-\theta_c$, $-\pi+2\theta_c$, and $-\theta_a-\theta_c$, respectively; their sum is $-2\pi$. The net change of $\log|z-z_0|$ is zero, so $\oint dz/(z-z_0)=-2\pi i$ and $\oint e^{-i\omega z}(z-z_0)^{-1}dz=-2\pi i e^{-i\omega z_0}$. The function $e^{-i\omega z}/(z-z_0)$ is single-valued and has continuous derivative on a neighborhood of the contour, which avoids $z_0$; by [F4] its derivative integrates to zero. Since
$$d\!\left(\frac{e^{-i\omega z}}{z-z_0}\right)=\left(-i\omega\frac{e^{-i\omega z}}{z-z_0}-\frac{e^{-i\omega z}}{(z-z_0)^2}\right)dz,$$
we obtain $\oint e^{-i\omega z}(z-z_0)^{-2}dz=-2\pi\omega e^{-i\omega z_0}$. [F3, F4, F5, F7, step 1.2, algebra]

3.1 The vertical-side integrals tend to zero as $R\to\infty$: for $\operatorname{Re}z=R$, $\operatorname{csch}^2(bz)=4e^{-2bz}(1-e^{-2bz})^{-2}=O(e^{-2bR})$ uniformly on the finite vertical interval, and the analogous formula with $e^{2bz}$ gives the same bound on $\operatorname{Re}z=-R$. The factor $e^{-i\omega z}$ is bounded there for fixed $\omega,\epsilon$. The bottom line is the top line translated by $-i\beta$; periodicity of $C$ and the Fourier phase multiply its oriented integral by $-e^{-\beta\omega}$. Combining this with step 2.1 gives
$$ (1-e^{-\beta\omega})I_\epsilon(\omega)=2\pi L\omega e^{-\beta\omega},$$
where $I_\epsilon(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-i\omega(s-i\epsilon)}C(s-i\epsilon)\,ds$. [step 1.2, step 2.1, F5, algebra]

4.1 For each fixed $0<\epsilon<\beta$, $s\mapsto C(s-i\epsilon)$ has no real pole, is bounded on compact intervals, and decays exponentially at both ends, so it is in $L^1(\mathbb R)$. If $\omega_n\to\omega$, the integrands converge pointwise and are bounded by $|C(s-i\epsilon)|\in L^1$; [F8] therefore makes its Fourier transform $J_\epsilon(\omega)=\int e^{-i\omega s}C(s-i\epsilon)ds$ continuous. Since $I_\epsilon=e^{-\omega\epsilon}J_\epsilon$, step 3.1 yields, for $\omega\ne0$,
$$J_\epsilon(\omega)=2\pi L e^{\omega\epsilon}\frac{\omega}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}.$$
Continuity forces the value at $\omega=0$ to be $2\pi L/\beta$; no point value at zero is left arbitrary. [step 3.1, F8, algebra]

5.1 Define $S(\omega)=2\pi L\omega/(e^{\beta\omega}-1)$ with its continuous value $2\pi L/\beta$ at zero. Continuity bounds $S$ on $[-1,1]$. For $\omega\ge1$, $|S(\omega)|\le C\omega e^{-\beta\omega}$, while for $\omega\le-1$, $|S(\omega)|\le C|\omega|$ because $1-e^{\beta\omega}\ge1-e^{-\beta}$. Thus $|S(\omega)|\le C(1+|\omega|)$ globally. For any Schwartz test $\varphi$, with seminorms $p_{m,n}(\varphi)=\sup_\omega|\omega^m\varphi^{(n)}(\omega)|$, split at $|\omega|=1$: the integral of $(1+|\omega|)|\varphi(\omega)|$ is bounded by a constant times $p_{0,0}(\varphi)+p_{3,0}(\varphi)$, since the tail is bounded by $2p_{3,0}|\omega|^{-2}$. Hence $S$ defines a tempered distribution. For $0<\epsilon\le\beta/2$, $e^{\omega\epsilon}S(\omega)$ is bounded by $C(1+|\omega|)$ for $\omega\le0$ and by $C(1+\omega)e^{-\beta\omega/2}$ for $\omega\ge0$. These bounds make the Schwartz-test product integrable and give an integrable majorant independent of $\epsilon$; [F8] yields $J_\epsilon\to S$ in the tempered-distribution sense. To construct the boundary without assuming a chiral specialization, apply Fourier inversion [F2] to a Schwartz test $\varphi$ and use $C_\epsilon\in L^1$ and $\widehat\varphi\in L^1$ to justify Fubini [F9]:
$$\langle C_\epsilon,\varphi\rangle=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}J_\epsilon(\omega)\widehat\varphi(-\omega)\,d\omega.$$
The same majorant and [F8] give the limit
$$\langle C_{A,b},\varphi\rangle:=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}S(\omega)\widehat\varphi(-\omega)\,d\omega.$$
This is continuous in the Schwartz topology by the seminorm bound above and continuity of the Schwartz Fourier map in [F2]; hence it defines a tempered distribution and $C_\epsilon\to C_{A,b}$ on every Schwartz test. Angular Fourier inversion gives $\widehat{\widehat\varphi}(-\omega)=2\pi\varphi(\omega)$, so the displayed formula applied to $\widehat\varphi$ yields $\langle\widehat C_{A,b},\varphi\rangle=\int S(\omega)\varphi(\omega)d\omega$. Thus the general boundary has exactly the stated spectrum. [A1, F2, F8, F9, step 4.1, algebra]

6.1 For $\omega>0$, the numerator and denominator of $S_{A,b}(\omega)$ are positive; for $\omega<0$ both are negative, and its zero limit $2\pi L/\beta=2A/b$ is positive. Direct algebra gives $S_{A,b}(-\omega)=e^{\beta\omega}S_{A,b}(\omega)$. The analytic period and reflection from step 1.1 give $C(s-i\beta+i\epsilon)=C(s+i\epsilon)=C(-s-i\epsilon)$; reflection preserves Schwartz tests, so step 5.1 gives the stated tested boundary relation. For the chiral specialization $A=\kappa^2/(16\pi)$, $b=\kappa/2$, [F1] identifies the same analytic regularizations with the compact-test boundary $C_\kappa$. Uniqueness of their distributional limit makes it the restriction of $C_{A,b}$, giving its tempered extension. Substituting $L=1/(4\pi)$ and $\beta=2\pi/\kappa$ yields the original chiral spectrum, zero value and ratio. The units follow because the phase $\omega s$ is dimensionless and Fourier transformation integrates once in the coordinate. These are two-point mathematical deductions only; neither the contour proof nor the strip identity establishes a full $C^*$-algebra KMS state or an exact detector transition probability. [F1, step 1.1, step 5.1, given, algebra] ∎

## Remarks

The contour calculation uses a single explicit double pole and the null-homotopic Cauchy theorem; it does not use the general residue theorem. The cited Wald, Crispino–Higuchi–Matsas, and Jacobson treatments describe broader curved-spacetime or accelerated-detector settings and provide context, not the local distributional or contour proofs above.
