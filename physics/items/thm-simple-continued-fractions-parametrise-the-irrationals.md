## Statement

The continued-fraction coding determined by [[def-simple-continued-fraction-coding]] gives a bijection from the sequences $(a_0,a_1,\ldots)$ with $a_0\in\mathbb Z$ and $a_n\ge1$ for $n\ge1$ onto $\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$. Both the coding map and its inverse are continuous for the cylinder and subspace topologies.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The objects, hypotheses, and choice principles stated above.

[F1] Define a bijection $z:\mathbb N\to\mathbb Z$ by $z(2k)=k$ and $z(2k+1)=-(k+1)$; the division algorithm makes these two cases exhaustive (thm-division-algorithm-in-z). For $x\in\mathbb N^{\mathbb N}$ put $a_0=z(x_0)$ and $a_n=x_n+1$ for $n\ge1$. Its finite simple continued fractions $[a_0;\ldots,a_n]$ are defined by recursion on the length, evaluated in $\mathbb Q$ (def-rationals, def-rat-operations): $[a_n]:=a_n$ and $[a_k;a_{k+1},\ldots,a_n]:=a_k+1/[a_{k+1};\ldots,a_n]$ for $k<n$. The recursion never divides by zero, because $a_j\ge1$ for $j\ge1$ makes every tail value $[a_{k+1};\ldots,a_n]$ at least $1$. A finite prefix determines the cylinder of all codes extending it. Infinite continued-fraction values are established, rather than assumed, in this item. ([[def-simple-continued-fraction-coding]]).

[F2] Let $a_0\in\mathbb Z$ and $a_n\in\mathbb Z$ with $a_n\ge1$ for $n\ge1$. With the initial values $p_{-2}=0$, $p_{-1}=1$, $q_{-2}=1$, $q_{-1}=0$ and the recurrences $p_n=a_np_{n-1}+p_{n-2}$, $q_n=a_nq_{n-1}+q_{n-2}$ for $n\ge0$: $p_0=a_0$ and $q_0=1$; the $q_n$ are positive for $n\ge0$ and strictly increasing for $n\ge1$; and $p_nq_{n-1}-p_{n-1}q_n=(-1)^{n-1}$ for $n\ge0$. For a finite prefix $(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$, $C(a_0,\ldots,a_n)\subseteq\mathbb N^{\mathbb N}$ is the **code cylinder** of all codes extending that prefix, and $J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ is the closed real interval with endpoints $p_n/q_n$ and $(p_n+p_{n-1})/(q_n+q_{n-1})$. The intervals $J$ are nested as the prefix is extended, and $\operatorname{diam}J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)=1/(q_n(q_n+q_{n-1}))$, which tends to $0$. A code cylinder and a real interval are different objects and the two are not identified. Both endpoints of $J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ are rational, being ratios of integers; whether an infinite code's value can equal such an endpoint is not settled there. ([[lem-simple-continued-fraction-convergents-and-cylinders]]).

[F3] Identify $\mathbb Z$ with its canonical copy inside $\mathbb R$. Then for every real $x$ there is **exactly one** integer $m$ with $$m\le x<m+1 ,$$ written $\lfloor x\rfloor$ and called the **integer part**, or **floor**, of $x$. Existence is the Archimedean property (thm-of-archimedean) together with the well-ordering of $\mathbb N$ (thm-well-ordering-principle); uniqueness is the discreteness of $\mathbb Z$, no integer lying strictly between $m$ and $m+1$. ([[lem-integer-part]]).

[F4] The map $q\mapsto\hat q$ (def-real-numbers) is an embedding of ordered fields. Every real is approximated by rationals: for $x\in\mathbb R$ and rational $\varepsilon>0$ there is $q\in\mathbb Q$ with $|x-\hat q|<\hat\varepsilon$. Consequently, strictly between any two reals lies a rational. ([[lem-rat-embeds-dense]]).

[F5] For each $k\in\mathbb N$ let $I_k=[c_k,d_k]$ be a closed bounded interval with $c_k\le d_k$ (def-interval), and suppose the family is **nested**: $I_{k+1}\subseteq I_k$ for every $k\in\mathbb N$. Write $\ell_k=d_k-c_k\ge0$ for the length of $I_k$. Then: 1. $\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb N}I_k$ is **nonempty**; more precisely, with $c=\sup\{c_k:k\in\mathbb N\}$ and $d=\inf\{d_k:k\in\mathbb N\}$, both of which exist, one has $c\le d$ and $\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb N}I_k=[c,d]$. 2. $\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb N}I_k$ is a **single point** if and only if $\ell_k\to0$. ([[thm-nested-interval-property]]).

[F6] The **Baire sequence space** is $\mathcal N:=\mathbb N^{\mathbb N}$, the set of functions from $\mathbb N$ to itself, with the product topology obtained by giving each copy of $\mathbb N$ the discrete topology (def-product-topology, def-standard-topologies). For a finite sequence $s=(s_0,\ldots,s_{k-1})$, its cylinder is $N_s:=\{x\in\mathcal N:x_i=s_i\text{ for }i<k\}$. The empty sequence has cylinder $\mathcal N$, and these cylinders form a basis. ([[def-baire-sequence-space]]).

[F7] For $S\subseteq X$ the **subspace topology** on $S$ is $\mathcal T_S:=\{\,U\cap S:U\in\mathcal T\,\}$, the family of traces on $S$ of the open sets of $X$; a subset of $S$ lying in $\mathcal T_S$ is said to be *open in $S$*. ([[def-subspace-topology-top]]).

[F8] For $d_{\mathbb R}(x,y):=|x-y|$: $d_{\mathbb R}$ is a metric on $\mathbb R$, the open ball is the bounded open interval $B(x,r)=(x-r,x+r)$, and consequently $U\subseteq\mathbb R$ is open in the metric topology of $d_{\mathbb R}$ exactly when for every $x\in U$ there is $r>0$ with $(x-r,x+r)\subseteq U$; this topology is called the **usual topology** of $\mathbb R$. ([[lem-real-line-is-a-metric-space]], claims 1 to 3).

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 Write $\mathcal A$ for the set of sequences $a=(a_0,a_1,\ldots)$ with $a_0\in\mathbb Z$ and $a_n\ge1$ for $n\ge1$, and for a finite prefix put $C(a_0,\ldots,a_n):=\{\,b\in\mathcal A:b_k=a_k\text{ for }k\le n\,\}$; by [F1] the assignment $x\mapsto a$ with $a_0=z(x_0)$ and $a_n=x_n+1$ for $n\ge1$ is a bijection of $\mathcal N=\mathbb N^{\mathbb N}$ onto $\mathcal A$, since $z$ is a bijection of $\mathbb N$ onto $\mathbb Z$ and $m\mapsto m+1$ is a bijection of $\mathbb N$ onto the integers that are at least $1$, and it acts coordinatewise, so it carries the cylinder $N_s$ of [F6] onto the cylinder $C(t_0,\ldots,t_{k-1})$ of the decoded prefix $t_0=z(s_0)$ and $t_i=s_i+1$ for $1\le i<k$, and every $C(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ arises from exactly one $N_s$ in this way; give $\mathcal A$ the topology transported along the bijection, so that the bijection is a homeomorphism and, the $N_s$ being a basis of $\mathcal N$ by [F6] and their images being exactly the sets $C(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$, those sets are a basis of $\mathcal A$, which is the cylinder topology of the Statement, while $\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$ carries the subspace topology of [F7] inherited from $\mathbb R$. [given, F1, F6, F7]

1.2 For $a\in\mathcal A$ let $p_n$ and $q_n$ be as in [F2] and, for $n\ge-1$, put $M_n(t):=(p_nt+p_{n-1})/(q_nt+q_{n-1})$; the initial values of [F2] give $M_{-1}(t)=(1\cdot t+0)/(0\cdot t+1)=t$ for every real $t$, and for $n\ge0$ and every real $t\ge1$ the denominator satisfies $q_nt+q_{n-1}>0$, since $q_0t+q_{-1}=t>0$ and, for $n\ge1$, $q_n>0$ and $q_{n-1}>0$ by [F2], so $M_n$ is defined at every real $t\ge1$ when $n\ge0$ and at every real $t$ when $n=-1$. [given, F2, algebra]

1.3 For $a\in\mathcal A$ the intervals $J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ are closed and bounded, are nested as $n$ increases, and satisfy $\operatorname{diam}J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)=1/(q_n(q_n+q_{n-1}))\to0$, all by [F2], so by claims 1 and 2 of [F5] their intersection is a single real, written $V(a)$, and $V(a)\in J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ for every $n\in\mathbb N$; moreover $V(a)$ is irrational, for suppose $V(a)=u/v$ with $u,v$ integers and $v\ge1$, and note first that the $q_n$ are positive for $n\ge0$ and strictly increasing for $n\ge1$ by [F2] and are integers, so $q_1\ge1$ and $q_{n+1}\ge q_n+1$ give $q_n\ge n$ for $n\ge1$; for $n\ge1$ both $V(a)$ and the endpoint $p_n/q_n$ lie in $J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$, so $|V(a)-p_n/q_n|\le1/(q_n(q_n+q_{n-1}))<1/q_n^2$ because $q_{n-1}>0$, and hence $|uq_n-vp_n|=vq_n\,|V(a)-p_n/q_n|<v/q_n$; taking $n>v$ makes $|uq_n-vp_n|<1$, and it is a nonnegative integer, hence $0$, so $V(a)=p_n/q_n$ for every $n>v$, which is impossible because the determinant identity of [F2] gives $p_{n+1}/q_{n+1}-p_n/q_n=(p_{n+1}q_n-p_nq_{n+1})/(q_nq_{n+1})=(-1)^n/(q_nq_{n+1})\ne0$. [given, F2, F5]

2.1 For $n\ge-1$ and reals $s,t$ at which $M_n$ is defined, clearing denominators gives $M_n(s)-M_n(t)=(s-t)(p_nq_{n-1}-p_{n-1}q_n)/\big((q_ns+q_{n-1})(q_nt+q_{n-1})\big)$, and by [F2] the determinant $p_nq_{n-1}-p_{n-1}q_n$ equals $(-1)^{n-1}$ for $n\ge0$ while for $n=-1$ the initial values give $p_{-1}q_{-2}-p_{-2}q_{-1}=1\cdot1-0\cdot0=1$; in every case it is $1$ or $-1$, and the denominators are positive by step 1.2, so $M_n$ is strictly monotone on its domain, strictly increasing when that determinant is $1$ and strictly decreasing when it is $-1$. [step 1.2, F2, algebra]

2.2 Fix $a\in\mathcal A$ and $n\ge0$; the recurrences of [F2] give $M_{n-1}(a_n)=(p_{n-1}a_n+p_{n-2})/(q_{n-1}a_n+q_{n-2})=p_n/q_n$ and $M_{n-1}(a_n+1)=(p_{n-1}(a_n+1)+p_{n-2})/(q_{n-1}(a_n+1)+q_{n-2})=(p_n+p_{n-1})/(q_n+q_{n-1})$, both arguments lying in the domain found in step 1.2 because $a_n\ge1$ when $n\ge1$ while $M_{-1}$ is defined on all of $\mathbb R$, so by [F2] the interval $J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ is the closed interval with endpoints $M_{n-1}(a_n)$ and $M_{n-1}(a_n+1)$, both of which are rational by [F2]; the interval is nondegenerate because $\operatorname{diam}J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)=1/(q_n(q_n+q_{n-1}))>0$, and $M_{n-1}$ depends only on $a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1}$, since $p_{n-1},p_{n-2},q_{n-1},q_{n-2}$ do. [step 1.2, F2, algebra]

2.3 Let $x\in\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$ and define $x_0:=x$, $a_n:=\lfloor x_n\rfloor$ and $x_{n+1}:=1/(x_n-a_n)$; by induction on $n$ every $x_n$ is irrational and $x_n>1$ for $n\ge1$, since given $x_n$ irrational [F3] supplies the unique integer $a_n$ with $a_n\le x_n<a_n+1$, so that $0\le x_n-a_n<1$ with $x_n-a_n\ne0$ because $x_n$ is irrational while $a_n$ is an integer, whence $0<x_n-a_n<1$ and $x_{n+1}=1/(x_n-a_n)>1$, and $x_{n+1}$ is irrational because a rational nonzero $x_{n+1}$ would make $x_n=a_n+1/x_{n+1}$ rational; consequently $a_n=\lfloor x_n\rfloor\ge1$ for $n\ge1$, the recursion never divides by zero, and $\Phi(x):=(a_0,a_1,\ldots)$ is a member of the set $\mathcal A$ of step 1.1. [step 1.1, F3, algebra]

2.4 Let $S$ be open in $\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$, so $S=T\cap(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q)$ for some $T$ open in $\mathbb R$ by [F7], and let $a\in\mathcal A$ satisfy $V(a)\in S$; claim 3 of [F8] gives a real $r>0$ with $(V(a)-r,V(a)+r)\subseteq T$, and the diameters $\operatorname{diam}J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ tend to $0$ by [F2], so some $n$ has $\operatorname{diam}J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)<r$; for every $b\in C(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ the interval $J(b_0,\ldots,b_n)$ equals $J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$, because [F2] computes it from the prefix alone, and both $V(b)$ and $V(a)$ lie in it by step 1.3, so $|V(b)-V(a)|<r$ and $V(b)\in T\cap(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q)=S$; hence every point of the preimage lies in a cylinder contained in it, so that preimage is the union of the cylinders it contains and is open by step 1.1, and the coding map is continuous. [step 1.1, step 1.3, F2, F7, F8]

3.1 Fix $a\in\mathcal A$ and $n\ge0$; subtracting and using the determinant identity of [F2] gives $M_n(t)-p_n/q_n=\big(q_n(p_nt+p_{n-1})-p_n(q_nt+q_{n-1})\big)/\big(q_n(q_nt+q_{n-1})\big)=-(p_nq_{n-1}-p_{n-1}q_n)/\big(q_n(q_nt+q_{n-1})\big)=(-1)^n/\big(q_n(q_nt+q_{n-1})\big)$ for every real $t\ge1$, and at $t=1$ the value $M_n(1)=(p_n+p_{n-1})/(q_n+q_{n-1})$ is the endpoint of $J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ other than $p_n/q_n$ identified in step 2.2; for $t>1$ the two differences $M_n(t)-p_n/q_n$ and $M_n(1)-p_n/q_n$ therefore have the same sign $(-1)^n$ and satisfy $|M_n(t)-p_n/q_n|<|M_n(1)-p_n/q_n|$, because $q_nt+q_{n-1}>q_n+q_{n-1}>0$, so $M_n(t)$ lies strictly between the two endpoints of $J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ and is neither of them. [step 1.2, step 2.2, F2, algebra]

3.2 Let $x\in\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$, let $(a_n)$ and $(x_n)$ be as in step 2.3, and let $p_n$ and $q_n$ be the convergent numerators and denominators of the code $\Phi(x)$; then $x=M_n(x_{n+1})$ for every $n\in\mathbb N$, by induction on $n$: at $n=0$ the values $p_0=a_0$, $q_0=1$, $p_{-1}=1$ and $q_{-1}=0$ of [F2] give $M_0(x_1)=(a_0x_1+1)/x_1=a_0+1/x_1=a_0+(x_0-a_0)=x$ because $x_1=1/(x_0-a_0)$, and for the inductive step $x_{n+1}=a_{n+1}+1/x_{n+2}$ with $x_{n+2}>1$, so multiplying numerator and denominator by $x_{n+2}$ gives $M_n(x_{n+1})=\big((a_{n+1}p_n+p_{n-1})x_{n+2}+p_n\big)/\big((a_{n+1}q_n+q_{n-1})x_{n+2}+q_n\big)=(p_{n+1}x_{n+2}+p_n)/(q_{n+1}x_{n+2}+q_n)=M_{n+1}(x_{n+2})$ by the recurrences of [F2]; every denominator here is nonzero, since $x_{n+1}>1$ and $x_{n+2}>1$ lie in the domains found in step 1.2. [step 1.2, step 2.3, F2, algebra]

3.3 Let $a,b\in\mathcal A$ with $a\ne b$ and let $m$ be the least index with $a_m\ne b_m$; the quantities $p_{m-1},p_{m-2},q_{m-1},q_{m-2}$ are computed from the common initial segment $a_0,\ldots,a_{m-1}$ alone, which is empty when $m=0$, the four quantities then being the initial values of [F2], so by step 2.2 the codes $a$ and $b$ determine the same function $M:=M_{m-1}$, and $J(a_0,\ldots,a_m)$ is the closed interval with endpoints $M(a_m)$ and $M(a_m+1)$ while $J(b_0,\ldots,b_m)$ is the closed interval with endpoints $M(b_m)$ and $M(b_m+1)$; assume $a_m<b_m$, which costs nothing by symmetry, so that $a_m+1\le b_m$ as these are integers, and all four arguments lie in the domain of $M$; if $M$ is strictly increasing there, which is one of the two cases of step 2.1, then $J(a_0,\ldots,a_m)=[M(a_m),M(a_m+1)]$ and $J(b_0,\ldots,b_m)=[M(b_m),M(b_m+1)]$ with $M(a_m+1)\le M(b_m)$, so the two meet in $\varnothing$ when $M(a_m+1)<M(b_m)$ and in $\{M(b_m)\}$ when $M(a_m+1)=M(b_m)$, while if $M$ is strictly decreasing the same computation with the endpoints exchanged gives $J(a_0,\ldots,a_m)=[M(a_m+1),M(a_m)]$ and $J(b_0,\ldots,b_m)=[M(b_m+1),M(b_m)]$ with $M(b_m)\le M(a_m+1)$, so the two meet in at most the single point $M(a_m+1)$; in both cases $J(a_0,\ldots,a_m)\cap J(b_0,\ldots,b_m)$ has at most one point, and any such point is an endpoint of both intervals and so is rational by step 2.2. [step 2.1, step 2.2]

4.1 Let $x\in\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$ and let $a:=\Phi(x)$ be the code produced in step 2.3; for every $n\in\mathbb N$ the tail identity of step 3.2 gives $x=M_n(x_{n+1})$ with $x_{n+1}>1$, so step 3.1 places $x$ strictly between the two endpoints of $J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ and in particular inside it, whence $x\in\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb N}J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$, which by step 1.3 is the single point $V(a)$; therefore $V(\Phi(x))=x$, and $V$ maps $\mathcal A$ onto $\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$. [step 1.3, step 2.3, step 3.1, step 3.2]

4.2 Let $a,b\in\mathcal A$ with $a\ne b$ and let $m$ be least with $a_m\ne b_m$; by step 1.3 the values $V(a)$ and $V(b)$ are irrational with $V(a)\in J(a_0,\ldots,a_m)$ and $V(b)\in J(b_0,\ldots,b_m)$, so if $V(a)=V(b)$ then that common value would lie in $J(a_0,\ldots,a_m)\cap J(b_0,\ldots,b_m)$ and hence be rational by step 3.3, a contradiction; therefore $V(a)\ne V(b)$ and $V$ is injective. [step 1.3, step 3.3]

5.1 By step 1.3 the map $V$ sends every member of $\mathcal A$ to an irrational real, it is surjective onto $\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$ by step 4.1 and injective by step 4.2, so $V:\mathcal A\to\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$ is a bijection, its inverse sends an irrational $x$ to the code $\Phi(x)$ of step 2.3, and composing with the coordinatewise bijection of step 1.1 presents it as a bijection defined on $\mathbb N^{\mathbb N}$. [step 1.1, step 1.3, step 4.1, step 4.2]

6.1 Let $C(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ be a cylinder and let $x\in V[C(a_0,\ldots,a_n)]$, say $x=V(c)$ with $c\in C(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$, so that $J(c_0,\ldots,c_n)=J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ is a closed interval $[\alpha,\beta]$ with $\alpha<\beta$ rational by step 2.2 and $x\in[\alpha,\beta]$ irrational by step 1.3, giving $\alpha<x<\beta$, and by [F4] there are rationals $u,v$ with $\alpha<u<x<v<\beta$; if $x'$ is irrational with $u<x'<v$ then $x'=V(b)$ for exactly one $b\in\mathcal A$ by step 5.1 and $x'\in J(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$, and were $b_k\ne a_k$ for some $k\le n$ then, taking $m$ to be the least such index, so that $m\le n$, the nesting of [F2] would put $x'$ in $J(a_0,\ldots,a_m)\cap J(b_0,\ldots,b_m)$, which by step 3.3 has at most one point and that point is rational, contradicting irrationality of $x'$, so $b\in C(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ and $x'\in V[C(a_0,\ldots,a_n)]$; consequently the set $T:=\bigcup\{\,(s,t):s<t\text{ rational and }(s,t)\cap(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q)\subseteq V[C(a_0,\ldots,a_n)]\,\}$ is a union of open intervals and so is open in $\mathbb R$ by [F8], and $T\cap(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q)=V[C(a_0,\ldots,a_n)]$, the inclusion from left to right being the defining condition of the union and the reverse holding because the interval $(u,v)$ just produced for the arbitrary point $x$ of the image is one of the united intervals; hence that image is open in $\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$ by [F7], and since every open subset of $\mathcal A$ is a union of cylinders by step 1.1 and the image of a union is the union of the images, $V$ maps open sets to open sets, which for the bijection of step 5.1 says exactly that its inverse is continuous. [step 1.1, step 1.3, step 2.2, step 3.3, step 5.1, F2, F4, F7, F8]

7.1 The coding map is a bijection onto $\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$ by step 5.1, it is continuous by step 2.4, and its inverse is continuous by step 6.1, which is the assertion. [step 2.4, step 5.1, step 6.1] ∎

## Remarks

- **The tail identity is what makes the algorithm invert the coding.** Running floors and reciprocals on an irrational $x$ produces a code, but nothing in that recipe by itself says the code's value is $x$ again. The identity $x=M_n(x_{n+1})$ of step 3.2 says it: the whole of $x$, not merely an approximation to it, is recovered from the first $n+1$ partial quotients together with the exact remainder $x_{n+1}$, and since $x_{n+1}>1$ the value sits strictly inside the $n$-th prefix interval. The intersection of those intervals is a single point, so it is $x$.

- **Irrationality is used twice, and for different purposes.** It is what makes the algorithm run forever, since a remainder equal to its own integer part would stop it; and it is what makes prefixes separate, since two prefix intervals of the same length belonging to different codes can share only a rational endpoint. The second use is what gives injectivity and the continuity of the inverse at once.

- **Where the parametrisation fails for rationals.** Nothing above extends to a rational target: the algorithm terminates, and the coding map is onto the irrationals only. That is the reason the companion identification is with $\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$ and not with $\mathbb R$, and the reason $\mathbb Q$ cannot be homeomorphic to $\mathbb N^{\mathbb N}$ by this route.
