## Statement

Let $G$ be a finite connected loopless multigraph with vertices $1,\ldots,r$ and edge multiplicities $b_{ij}=b_{ji}\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ for $i\ne j$. Define the symmetric matrix $M$ by $M_{ii}=-2$ and $M_{ij}=b_{ij}$. Suppose $M$ is negative semidefinite, its kernel is one-dimensional, and it contains a vector with every coordinate strictly positive. Then $G$ is one of the affine Dynkin graphs $\widetilde A_n$ ($n\ge1$), $\widetilde D_n$ ($n\ge4$), $\widetilde E_6$, $\widetilde E_7$, or $\widetilde E_8$. Here $\widetilde A_1$ is two vertices joined by two edges; $\widetilde A_n$ for $n\ge2$ is a cycle of $n+1$ vertices; $\widetilde D_4$ is the four-legged star; $\widetilde D_n$ for $n\ge5$ has two trivalent vertices joined by a path of $n-4$ edges, with two terminal leaves at each end; and the three affine exceptional graphs have one trivalent vertex with arm lengths $(2,2,2)$, $(1,3,3)$, and $(1,2,5)$, respectively. Primitive means that the greatest common divisor of the integer coordinates is $1$. In each case the primitive positive integral kernel vector is unique.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** $G$, its edge multiplicities, the associated matrix $M$, and the three matrix hypotheses in the Statement.

[F1] Negative semidefinite means $x^{\mathsf T}Mx\le0$ for every $x\in\mathbb R^r$; a real symmetric matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis, so $x^{\mathsf T}Mx=0$ for a negative-semidefinite matrix implies $Mx=0$ ([[cor-real-spectral-theorem-for-self-adjoint-endomorphisms]]).

## Proof

**Given:** $G$, its edge multiplicities, the associated matrix $M$, and the three matrix hypotheses in the Statement.

[F1] Negative semidefinite means $x^{\mathsf T}Mx\le0$ for every $x\in\mathbb R^r$; a real symmetric matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis, so $x^{\mathsf T}Mx=0$ for a negative-semidefinite matrix implies $Mx=0$ ([[cor-real-spectral-theorem-for-self-adjoint-endomorphisms]]).



**Proof technique:** classify proper null subgraphs, then use the Schur complement at the unique trivalent vertex.

1.1 A proper induced subgraph cannot have a nonzero null vector. Its principal matrix is negative semidefinite by [F1]. Extending a null vector by zero gives a vector $x$ with $x^{\mathsf T}Mx=0$, hence $Mx=0$. Since $\ker M$ is generated by a strictly positive vector, this extended vector cannot vanish at any vertex; therefore the subgraph was all of $G$. [F1, given, algebra]

2.1 No edge has multiplicity at least three: on its two endpoints the principal matrix $\begin{pmatrix}-2&b\\b&-2\end{pmatrix}$ has positive eigenvalue $b-2$ when $b\ge3$. If an edge has multiplicity two, $(1,1)$ is a nonzero null vector of its principal matrix, so [step 1.1] forces that two-vertex graph to be all of $G$; this is $\widetilde A_1$. Henceforth every edge is simple. [F1, step 1.1, algebra]

2.2 If a vertex has degree $d\ge5$, the principal star matrix has positive quadratic value at the vector with center $\sqrt d$ and each leaf $1$: the value is $-4d+2d\sqrt d>0$, contradicting [F1]. If a vertex has degree four, the star vector with center $2$ and leaves $1$ is null, so [step 1.1] forces the entire graph to be that star, $\widetilde D_4$. Otherwise the maximum degree is at most three. [F1, step 1.1, algebra]

3.1 By steps 2.1 and 2.2 the graph is simple with maximum degree at most three. If it contains a cycle, choose a shortest simple cycle; it has no chord, since any chord would cut it into a shorter cycle. The vector equal to one on this induced cycle and zero elsewhere has quadratic value zero: each cycle vertex has exactly two selected incident edges and the cycle has no chords. By [step 1.1] its vertex set is all of $G$, hence $G=\widetilde A_n$ for its number of vertices $n+1$. If $G$ is a tree with no vertex of degree three, it is a path; for a path on $r\ge1$ vertices, $-x^{\mathsf T}Mx=x_1^2+x_r^2+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}(x_i-x_{i+1})^2>0$ for every nonzero $x$, so it has no kernel. [step 1.1, step 2.1, step 2.2, algebra]

4.1 If the tree has at least two trivalent vertices, take two consecutive trivalent vertices and the path between them, together with one adjacent off-path vertex at each of the two unused branches at both ends. This subgraph is an affine $\widetilde D_n$ graph. Its positive null vector has value $2$ on the path and the two trivalent vertices and value $1$ on the four leaves; the row equations give $M_Hm=0$, and the edge-square identity $x^{\mathsf T}M_Hx=-\sum_{i<j}b_{ij}(\sqrt{m_j/m_i}x_i-\sqrt{m_i/m_j}x_j)^2$ shows it is negative semidefinite with one-dimensional kernel. Thus [step 1.1] forces $G=H$, giving $\widetilde D_n$. [step 1.1, step 2.1, step 2.2, step 3.1, algebra, construct]

5.1 By steps 2.1–2.2, 3.1, and 4.1, the only remaining case is a tree with exactly one trivalent vertex. Let its three arm lengths, counted in vertices beyond the central vertex, be $1\le p\le q\le s$. For $A=-M$, each arm block is the positive definite path matrix with diagonal $2$ and adjacent entries $-1$. Solving the path equations gives the endpoint entry of its inverse as $p/(p+1)$ (the solution of $A_pu=e_1$ is $u_j=(p+1-j)/(p+1)$). Eliminating the three arm blocks leaves central Schur complement $2-p/(p+1)-q/(q+1)-s/(s+1)$. Since $A$ is positive semidefinite of corank one, this complement is zero, so $1/(p+1)+1/(q+1)+1/(s+1)=1$. [given, step 2.1, step 2.2, step 3.1, step 4.1, algebra]

6.1 If $p\ge2$, each reciprocal is at most $1/3$; equality of their sum with $1$ forces $(p,q,s)=(2,2,2)$, the affine $E_6$ graph. If $p=1$, then $1/(q+1)+1/(s+1)=1/2$. With $q\le s$, $q=2$ gives $s=5$ and $q=3$ gives $s=3$; $q=1$ would require $1/(s+1)=0$, while $q\ge4$ makes the sum at most $2/5$. These are exactly the affine $E_8$ and $E_7$ graphs. This exhausts the single-trivalent-vertex case. [step 5.1, algebra]

7.1 The integer matrix $M$ has a one-dimensional real kernel, so its rational nullspace is also one-dimensional because rank is unchanged on extending scalars from $\mathbb Q$ to $\mathbb R$. It therefore has a nonzero rational kernel vector; clearing denominators and choosing its sign gives an integral vector proportional to the strictly positive vector in the hypothesis. The one-dimensional kernel makes this positive integral vector unique up to positive integer scale, and dividing its coordinates by their greatest common divisor gives the unique primitive positive integral kernel vector. The classification in steps 3.1–6.1 includes the double-edge $\widetilde A_1$, the four-legged $\widetilde D_4$, and all three exceptional arm triples. [given, step 2.1, step 2.2, step 3.1, step 4.1, step 6.1, algebra] ∎
