## Example

Let $H\subset\mathbb R^2$ be the regular hexagon of side length $1$ with vertices $v_0,\dots,v_5$ in cyclic order, regarded as a single compact convex $2$-cell of an isometric polyhedral gluing, with one maximal cell, its six sides and its six vertices (three shapes). This is the $A_2$ Coxeter cell for an equidistant generic point: step 3.1 verifies the reflection presentation $\langle s,t\mid s^2=t^2=(st)^3=1\rangle$, computes its six-point orbit, and identifies $H$ with the orbit hull (Davis, Definition 7.3.1 and Examples 7.3.2(ii)). Explicitly, take
$$H:=\{(x_1,x_2)\in\mathbb R^2:\ |x_2|\le\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ |\sqrt3x_1+x_2|\le\sqrt3,\ |\sqrt3x_1-x_2|\le\sqrt3\}$$
and let
$$v_0=(1,0),\ v_1=(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}),\ v_2=(-\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}),\ v_3=(-1,0),\ v_4=(-\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}),\ v_5=(\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2});$$
the inequalities written out are the six affine inequalities $\pm x_2\le\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, $\pm(\sqrt3x_1+x_2)\le\sqrt3$, $\pm(\sqrt3x_1-x_2)\le\sqrt3$. They also give $|x_1|\le1$, since $2\sqrt3|x_1|\le|\sqrt3x_1+x_2|+|\sqrt3x_1-x_2|\le2\sqrt3$. Thus $H$ is nonempty (it contains $0$), bounded and defined by finitely many closed affine inequalities, hence is a compact convex polyhedral cell; the verification below identifies its vertices and sides. Let $d$ be the chain metric of the gluing. Then:

**(i)** for a single convex cell the chain metric is the Euclidean metric: $d(x,y)=|x-y|_2$ for all $x,y\in H$ ([[lem-metrics-on-rn]]);

**(ii)** $(H,d)$ is a complete geodesic space; the straight segment $[x,y]\subseteq H$ realizes $d$, by completeness and convexity of $H$ as verified here;

**(iii)** the barycentric triangulation of $H$ (the order complex of the face poset of the single cell) has vertices the six polygon vertices, the six edge midpoints and the centre $o$, and twelve congruent right triangles $v\,m\,o$, one for every incident vertex-edge pair $v<e$ with midpoint $m$; each has legs $|vm|=1/2$ and $|mo|=\sqrt3/2$ and hypotenuse $|vo|=1$, so its three barycentric coordinates have slopes $2$, $2/\sqrt3$ and $4/\sqrt3$; hence $L=4/\sqrt3$ and the uniform star radius of the star lemma is $\delta=1/(2L(D+1))=\sqrt3/24$ for $D=2$;

**(iv)** let $d_{\mathrm{gr}}$ be the graph distance of the hexagonal $1$-skeleton $C_6$ (the Cayley graph of the Coxeter group of type $A_2$, which is the dihedral group $D_3$ of order $6$, for its two standard generators, Davis Proposition 7.3.4). For a pair of vertices at graph distance $k\in\{1,2,3\}$ one has
$$d(v_0,v_k)=1,\ \sqrt3,\ 2\qquad(k=1,2,3),\qquad d_{\mathrm{gr}}(v_0,v_k)=1,\ 2,\ 3.$$
Thus $d$ and $d_{\mathrm{gr}}$ already disagree on vertices: $\sqrt3<2$ (the short diagonal is shorter than the two-edge boundary route) and $2<3$ (opposite vertices are joined by the straight segment through the centre, while the boundary arc has length $3$). The graph metric is therefore not the metric induced by the cell, and neither is the boundary arc length.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The single-cell gluing $X=\iota_H(H)$ of the hexagon $H$ with the chain metric $d$ of [[def-cg-abstract-isometric-polyhedral-gluing-and-chain-metric]]; the Euclidean plane with its inner product and norm $\lVert\cdot\rVert_2$ and the metric $d_2(x,y)=\lVert x-y\rVert_2$, so $|x-y|_2=d_2(x,y)$ ([[lem-metrics-on-rn]], [[def-euclidean-inner-product]], [[def-inner-product-norm]]).

[F1] A compact convex polyhedral cell is a nonempty bounded set given by finitely many affine inequalities $\ell_i(x)\ge0$, and every nonempty face arises by turning some of the defining inequalities into equalities; a $0$-dimensional face is a singleton and is a vertex. ([[def-finite-convex-cell-complex-and-linear-subdivision]])

[F2] For an isometric polyhedral gluing with (H1)-(H3) and maximal cell dimension $D$, the order complex of the poset of nonempty faces carries a compatible barycentric triangulation whose carrier simplices cover $X$ with disjoint relative interiors; the hat coordinates $\lambda_v$ are affine on each simplex, satisfy $\sum_v\lambda_v=1$, and admit a uniform Lipschitz constant $L<\infty$ which may be taken to be the maximum of $1$ and of the slopes $1/h$ over the finitely many positive-dimensional model simplices, $h$ being the distance from a simplex vertex to the affine hull of the opposite face of that simplex; for every $x$ some $\lambda_v(x)\ge1/(D+1)$ and $B(x,\delta)$ lies in the open star of $v$ for $\delta=1/(2L(D+1))$. ([[lem-cg-polyhedral-face-coherence-and-uniform-star-radius]])

[F3] Under (H1)-(H3) the chain metric candidate is a metric inducing the weak topology and $(X,d)$ is proper and complete; the weak topology declares $U$ open exactly when $U\cap\iota_p(C_p)$ is relatively open in $\iota_p(C_p)$ for every cell. ([[thm-cg-polyhedral-chain-metric-topology-and-properness]], [[def-cg-abstract-isometric-polyhedral-gluing-and-chain-metric]])

[F4] $d_2$ is a metric on $\mathbb R^2$, so its triangle inequality and symmetry hold, and it restricts to the Euclidean distance on $H$. ([[lem-metrics-on-rn]], [[def-metric-space]])

[F5] A subset of $\mathbb R^2$ is convex when it contains the segment between any two of its points ([[def-convex-subset-of-euclidean-space]]); a convex set is path-connected via its straight segments, and a path-connected space is connected ([[def-path-connected]], [[thm-path-connected-implies-connected]]); an $M$-Lipschitz map between metric spaces is continuous ([[thm-metric-regularity-hierarchy]], [[def-metric-continuity]]).

[F6] A triangle $T(A,B,C)$ is Jordan measurable with content $\tfrac12|\det[B-A\ C-A]|$, and for $A\ne B$ this content equals $\tfrac12\lVert B-A\rVert_2\,d(C-A,\mathbb R(B-A))$, where $d(C-A,\mathbb R(B-A))$ is the perpendicular height of [[def-base-and-height-for-plane-figures]]; equivalently $\lVert v\rVert_2\,d(w,\mathbb Rv)=|\det[v\ w]|$ for $v\ne0$. ([[thm-triangle-content-and-base-height-formula]], [[lem-determinant-base-height-identity-in-r2]])

[F7] Square roots: every $c\ge0$ has a unique nonnegative square root $\sqrt c$ with $\sqrt c^2=c$, and $\sqrt 3>0$ with $(\sqrt3)^2=3$; squaring is strictly increasing on the nonnegatives, so $\sqrt3<2$ because $3<4$. ([[thm-of-square-roots]], [[lem-of-square-monotone]])

[F8] The path metric of a connected simple graph assigns to two vertices the least number of edges of a path joining them and is a metric on the vertex set. ([[def-graph-path-metric]], [[thm-the-path-metric-of-a-connected-simple-graph-is-a-metric]])

[F9] The Euclidean inner product is bilinear and symmetric, $\lVert v\rVert_2^2=\langle v,v\rangle$, so $\lVert u-w\rVert_2^2=\lVert u\rVert_2^2-2\langle u,w\rangle+\lVert w\rVert_2^2$; orthogonal vectors satisfy $\lVert a+b\rVert_2^2=\lVert a\rVert_2^2+\lVert b\rVert_2^2$. ([[def-euclidean-inner-product]], [[def-inner-product-space]], [[def-inner-product-norm]], [[prop-pythagorean-parallelogram-and-polarisation-identities]])

## Verification

1.1 (The cell $H$ and its face poset.) The six points $v_0,\dots,v_5$ lie in $H$: for $v_1=(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})$ one has $|x_2|=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ and $\sqrt3x_1+x_2=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}=\sqrt3$ with $\sqrt3x_1-x_2=0$, and the remaining five cases follow by the same substitution, the values used being $(\sqrt3)^2=3$ and $\tfrac12\sqrt3+\tfrac12\sqrt3=\sqrt3$ [F7]. The six equalities among the defining inequalities are the three pairs of parallel lines $x_2=\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, $\sqrt3x_1+x_2=\pm\sqrt3$, $\sqrt3x_1-x_2=\pm\sqrt3$; each of the twelve pairs of equalities from different pairs determines a unique point, namely $v_1$ for $x_2=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ with $\sqrt3x_1+x_2=\sqrt3$, $v_2$ for $x_2=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ with $-\sqrt3x_1+x_2=\sqrt3$, $v_0$ for $\sqrt3x_1+x_2=\sqrt3$ with $\sqrt3x_1-x_2=\sqrt3$, and symmetrically $v_3,v_4,v_5$ for the three remaining admissible cases, while in the other six cases one of the remaining inequalities fails (for instance $x_2=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ with $\sqrt3x_1+x_2=-\sqrt3$ forces $x_1=-\tfrac32$, and then $\sqrt3x_1-x_2=-2\sqrt3<-\sqrt3$ violates the inequality). Intersections of three or more equalities are contained in one of these, so by [F1] the nonempty faces of $H$ are exactly the cell $H$ itself, the six sides
$$[v_0,v_1],\ [v_1,v_2],\ [v_2,v_3],\ [v_3,v_4],\ [v_4,v_5],\ [v_5,v_0],$$
and the six vertices $v_0,\dots,v_5$, and $H$ is $2$-dimensional because it contains the non-collinear points $v_0,v_1,v_3$. Consecutive vertices satisfy $\lVert v_k-v_{k+1}\rVert_2^2=1$ in each of the six cases (for $(v_0,v_1)$ and $(v_4,v_5)$ it is $\tfrac14+\tfrac34=1$, for $(v_1,v_2)$ it is $1$, and for $(v_2,v_3)$, $(v_3,v_4)$ and $(v_5,v_0)$ it is $\tfrac14+\tfrac34=1$ $($the first coordinate step $\tfrac12$ and the second $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, with $(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})^2=\tfrac34))$, so the sides have length $1$; also $\lVert v_k\rVert_2^2=1$ for all six $k$. The centre $o:=(0,0)$ is the barycentre $\tfrac16(v_0+\dots+v_5)$ of $H$: the first coordinates $1,\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,-1,-\tfrac12,\tfrac12$ sum to $0$ and so do the second coordinates $0,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},0,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, and the midpoint of the side with endpoints $u,w$ is $m=\tfrac12(u+w)$. [F1, F7]

2.1 (The gluing, (H1)-(H3) and clauses (i), (ii).) The shape $P$ is the full face poset of $H$, including the empty face; its cells are $C_p=p$ for every nonempty face, with identity inclusions as face isometries. Each principal down-set is the face poset of its face, meets are intersections of faces, and the cocycle condition holds for inclusions. The quotient identifies each face copy with its subset in $H$, so the intersection condition holds and the map $\iota_H\colon H\to X$ is a bijection; a subset of $X$ is open exactly when its trace on $\iota_H(H)=X$ is relatively open, so $\iota_H$ is a homeomorphism. $X$ is path-connected: for $x,y\in H$ and $t\in[0,1]$ the point $(1-t)x+ty$ lies in $H$ because the six inequalities are affine, $(1-t)\ell(x)+t\ell(y)\le\max\{\ell(x),\ell(y)\}\le c$ for each defining inequality $\ell\le c$ [F5]; the segment path $t\mapsto(1-t)x+ty$ is $\lVert x-y\rVert_2$-Lipschitz for $d_2$, since $\lVert((1-t)x+ty)-((1-s)x+sy)\rVert_2=|t-s|\,\lVert x-y\rVert_2$ [F4], hence continuous [F5]; composing with $\iota_H$ gives a path in $X$, so $X$ is path-connected and therefore connected [F5]. There are thirteen nonempty faces and three shapes (points, unit intervals and $H$), so (H2) local finiteness and (H3) finite shapes hold, and $D=\dim H=2$; by [F3] the chain metric $d$ is a metric inducing the weak topology and $(X,d)$ is proper and complete. Clause (i): for $x,y\in H$ the one-step chain shows $d(x,y)\le\lVert x-y\rVert_2$ [F3], while every chain has steps inside $H$ and length the sum of Euclidean distances of its steps, which is at least $\lVert x-y\rVert_2$ by the triangle inequality for $d_2$ [F4]; taking the infimum, $d(x,y)=\lVert x-y\rVert_2$ for all $x,y\in H$. Clause (ii): hence $d$ is the Euclidean metric, the straight segment $[x,y]\subseteq H$ (which lies in $H$ by convexity, verified in the first sentence) has the unit-speed parametrization $\gamma(t)=x+(t/R)(y-x)$ on $[0,R]$ when $R=\lVert x-y\rVert_2>0$, satisfying $d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=|s-t|$; for $x=y$ use the constant map on $[0,0]$. Thus it is a geodesic segment from $x$ to $y$ ([[def-geodesic-and-geodesic-metric-space]]), and $(H,d)$ is complete by [F3]. [F1, F3, F4, F5, step 1.1]

2.2 (Clause (iii): the barycentric triangulation.) By [F2] the order complex $K$ of the face poset of $H$ gives a compatible barycentric triangulation of $H$ whose maximal simplices are the maximal chains $v<e<H$, twelve in number, one for every incident vertex-edge pair; the associated triangle has vertices $b_v=v$, $b_e=m$ (the midpoint of the side $e$) and $b_H=o$, where $m$ and $o$ are as in step 1.1. Let $e=[u,w]$ be a side, $v$ one of its endpoints and $m=\tfrac12(u+w)$ its midpoint. Then $\lVert u\rVert_2=\lVert w\rVert_2=1$ and $\lVert u-w\rVert_2=1$ by step 1.1, so $\langle u,w\rangle=\tfrac12(\lVert u\rVert_2^2+\lVert w\rVert_2^2-\lVert u-w\rVert_2^2)=\tfrac12$ by [F9], and with $v-m=\pm\tfrac12(u-w)$ and $o-m=-\tfrac12(u+w)$ one gets $\langle v-m,o-m\rangle=\mp\tfrac14(\lVert u\rVert_2^2-\lVert w\rVert_2^2)=0$: the triangle has a right angle at $m$, its legs are $\lVert v-m\rVert_2=\tfrac12\lVert u-w\rVert_2=\tfrac12$ and $\lVert o-m\rVert_2=\tfrac12\lVert u+w\rVert_2=\tfrac12\sqrt{2+2\langle u,w\rangle}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, and its hypotenuse is $\lVert v-o\rVert_2=\lVert v\rVert_2=1$, consistently with $\tfrac14+\tfrac34=1$ [F7]. Since $m$ lies on the line $vm$ and, for every $p$ on that line, $o-p=(o-m)+(m-p)$ is an orthogonal decomposition [F9], $m$ is the point of the line closest to $o$ and the perpendicular height over the base $vm$ is $\lVert o-m\rVert_2=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, so the triangle content is $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{8}$ by [F6]. The three barycentric-coordinate slopes of this triangle are the reciprocals of the distances from $v$, $m$, $o$ to the opposite sidelines; by the base-height form of [F6] these distances are $\tfrac{2\operatorname{cont}}{\lVert m-o\rVert_2}=\tfrac12$, $\tfrac{2\operatorname{cont}}{\lVert v-o\rVert_2}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}$ and $\tfrac{2\operatorname{cont}}{\lVert v-m\rVert_2}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ respectively, so the slopes are $2$, $\tfrac{4}{\sqrt3}$ and $\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}$. Every one of the twelve triangles arises this way, from a side and one of its endpoints, so all twelve have these three slopes; the lower-dimensional simplices of $K$ are the chains $v<H$, $e<H$, $v<e$ with barycentres $v,o$; $m,o$ and $v,m$ and slopes $1$, $\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}$ and $2$. Singleton simplices have constant coordinates with slope $0$. Hence the maximum slope over all simplices is $\tfrac{4}{\sqrt3}$, and $L=\max\{1,\tfrac{4}{\sqrt3}\}=\tfrac{4}{\sqrt3}$ because $\sqrt3<2$ gives $\tfrac{4}{\sqrt3}>2>1$ [F7]. [F2, F6, F7, F9, step 1.1]

3.1 (The $A_2$ orbit and its Cayley graph.) Put $c:=\sqrt3/2$ and define linear maps $s(x,y):=(x/2+cy,cx-y/2)$ and $t(x,y):=(x/2-cy,-cx-y/2)$. Direct multiplication gives $s^2=t^2=1$, $r:=st$ with $r(x,y)=(-x/2-cy,cx-y/2)$, and $r^3=1$; their matrices are orthogonal by [F9]. The fixed lines of $s,t$ are respectively $y=x/\sqrt3$ and $y=-x/\sqrt3$, so they are reflections in the two walls of the sector $x\ge0$, $|y|\le x/\sqrt3$. The point $v_0=(1,0)$ is interior to this sector and at distance $1/2$ from each wall, by the perpendicular-distance formula [F6]. In the group presentation $\langle s,t\mid s^2=t^2=(st)^3=1\rangle$, any word first reduces to an alternating word; the relation gives $stst=ts$ and $tsts=st$ and then $sts=tst$, leaving at most the six words $1,s,t,st,ts,sts$. The six matrices represented by these words send $v_0$ respectively to $v_0,v_1,v_5,v_2,v_4,v_3$, all distinct. Thus the reflection group has exactly six elements and exactly this presentation, the rank-two Coxeter presentation of type $A_2$. Its generic orbit is precisely the six vertices. By step 2.2 the triangles cover $H$, and every triangle vertex is a polygon vertex, a midpoint of two such vertices or their average $o$; hence $H$ is their convex hull. This verifies directly the orbit-hull definition of its Coxeter cell. Label a group element $w$ by the vertex $wv_0$. Its right-generator neighbors $wsv_0=wv_1$ and $wtv_0=wv_5$ are the two neighbors of $wv_0$ in the hexagon: orthogonal maps in this group permute the six vertices and preserve their distances, and the only vertices at Euclidean distance $1$ from $v_0$ are $v_1,v_5$ by the displayed coordinates, hence the same holds at $wv_0$. This proves that the Cayley graph for $s,t$ is exactly the hexagonal $1$-skeleton. [F6, F7, F9, step 1.1, step 2.2, algebra]

3.2 (Clause (iv): the vertex distances.) By step 2.1 clause (i), $d(v_0,v_k)=\lVert v_0-v_k\rVert_2$. For $k=1$: $\lVert v_0-v_1\rVert_2=\lVert(\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})\rVert_2=\sqrt{\tfrac14+\tfrac34}=1$ [F7]. For $k=2$: $v_0-v_2=(\tfrac32,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})$, so $\lVert v_0-v_2\rVert_2^2=\tfrac94+\tfrac34=3$ and $\lVert v_0-v_2\rVert_2=\sqrt3$. For $k=3$: $v_0-v_3=(2,0)$, so $\lVert v_0-v_3\rVert_2=2$ [F7]. [F7, step 2.1]

3.3 (Clause (iii): the star radius.) Substituting $D=2$ and $L=\tfrac{4}{\sqrt3}$ into [F2] gives $$\delta=\frac{1}{2L(D+1)}=\frac{1}{2\cdot\tfrac{4}{\sqrt3}\cdot 3}=\frac{\sqrt3}{24},$$ using $(\sqrt3)^2=3$ and $\tfrac{1}{24/\sqrt3}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{24}$ [F7]. [F2, F7, step 2.2]

4.1 (Clause (iv): the graph distance and the comparison.) The hexagonal $1$-skeleton is the cycle $v_0v_1v_2v_3v_4v_5v_0$ on six vertices, a connected simple graph, so it carries the graph path metric $d_{\mathrm{gr}}$ [F8]; for $k\in\{1,2,3\}$ the boundary path $v_0,v_1,\dots,v_k$ has length $k$, so $d_{\mathrm{gr}}(v_0,v_k)\le k$, while a path of $r$ edges consists of index increments $+1$ or $-1$ modulo $6$, whose integer sum $j$ satisfies $j\equiv k\pmod6$ and $|j|\le r$. The minimum of $|j|$ among integers congruent to $k$ is $\min(k,6-k)=k$ for $k=1,2,3$, hence $r\ge k$; thus $d_{\mathrm{gr}}(v_0,v_k)=k$. Comparing with step 3.2: $\sqrt3\ne 2$ and $2\ne 3$ [F7], so the chain metric and the graph metric already differ at the vertex pairs $(v_0,v_2)$ and $(v_0,v_3)$, and in particular the graph metric of the $1$-skeleton is not the restriction of the cell metric; the boundary path has length $3$ between $v_0$ and $v_3$, strictly more than $d(v_0,v_3)=2$. [F8, step 3.1, step 3.2] ∎
