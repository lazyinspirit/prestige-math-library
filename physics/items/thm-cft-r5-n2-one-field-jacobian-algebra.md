## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Let $k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$, and set $W(X)=\lambda X^{k+2}/(k+2)$. Use the formal derivative and Jacobian quotient of [[def-cft-r5-n2-one-field-jacobian-algebra]].

[F1] [[def-cft-r5-n2-one-field-jacobian-algebra]]: $\operatorname{Jac}(W)=\mathbb C[X]/(W'(X))$, with the formal derivative defined by $(X^n)'=nX^{n-1}$ and the ideal $(W')$ defined in the commutative ring $\mathbb C[X]$.

[F2] [[def-complex-numbers-and-arithmetic]] and [[thm-complex-numbers-form-a-field]]: $\mathbb C$ is a field containing the embedded real field, so every positive integer such as $k+2$ is a nonzero complex scalar and every nonzero complex number is invertible.

[F3] [[def-polynomial-ring-over-a-commutative-ring]]: $\mathbb C[X]$ consists of finitely supported coefficient sequences with coefficientwise addition and convolution multiplication.

[F4] [[lem-field-is-a-commutative-ring]] and [[thm-polynomial-ring-is-a-commutative-ring]]: $\mathbb C$ is a commutative ring and these operations make $\mathbb C[X]$ a commutative ring.

[F5] [[def-generated-and-principal-ideals]] and [[thm-generated-ideal-description-in-a-commutative-ring]]: in a commutative ring the ideal generated by a single element $a$ is exactly $Ra=\{ra:r\in R\}$.

[F6] [[def-vector-space]]: a complex vector space has an abelian-group addition and a scalar map satisfying the vector-space axioms.

[F7] [[def-quotient-ring]], [[def-quotient-vector-space-and-canonical-projection]], and [[prop-quotient-vector-space-operations-and-projection]]: for a linear subspace $I$ of a complex vector space $V$, the same cosets $V/I$ carry the quotient vector-space operations $[p]+[q]=[p+q]$ and $a[p]=[ap]$; for an ideal $I$ in a ring, the quotient-ring elements are those same additive cosets.

[F8] [[def-linear-subspace]]: a subset containing zero and closed under addition and complex scalar multiplication is a linear subspace.

[F9] [[def-linear-independence]], [[def-linear-combination-and-span]], [[def-linear-basis]], and [[def-dimension]]: independence is the finite linear-combination criterion, a basis is an independent spanning subset, and the dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space is the cardinality of a finite basis.

## Statement

For $W(X)=\lambda X^{k+2}/(k+2)$ with $k\ge1$ and $\lambda\ne0$, the Jacobian algebra is
$
\operatorname{Jac}(W)\cong\mathbb C[X]/(X^{k+1}),
$
with basis $[1],[X],\ldots,[X^k]$ and complex dimension $k+1$.

## Proof

1.1 By the derivative definition in [F1], $W'(X)=\lambda X^{k+1}$. Since $\lambda\ne0$ is a unit by [F2], the ideals $(W')$ and $(X^{k+1})$ are equal: each generator is a unit multiple of the other. Thus the quotient in [F1] is $\mathbb C[X]/(X^{k+1})$. [given, F1, F2, algebra]

1.2 Define scalar multiplication on $\mathbb C[X]$ by $a\cdot\sum_j b_jX^j=\sum_j(ab_j)X^j$. Its addition is coefficientwise; the additive-group laws follow coefficientwise from the ring structure [F4], and the scalar distributivity, associativity, and identity axioms follow coefficientwise from the field laws [F2] and the polynomial operations [F3]. Hence this makes $\mathbb C[X]$ a complex vector space in the sense of [F6]. [F2, F3, F4, F6, algebra]

1.3 Let $I=(X^{k+1})$. By [F5], $I=\{qX^{k+1}:q\in\mathbb C[X]\}$. It contains zero; if $qX^{k+1},q'X^{k+1}\in I$, their sum is $(q+q')X^{k+1}\in I$; and for $a\in\mathbb C$, $a\cdot(qX^{k+1})=(a\cdot q)X^{k+1}\in I$. Thus $I$ is a linear subspace of $\mathbb C[X]$ by [F8]. [F3, F4, F5, F8, algebra]

2.1 By [F7] and step 1.3, the additive cosets $\mathbb C[X]/I$ in the quotient-ring construction of [F1] carry the quotient vector-space operations $[p]+[q]=[p+q]$ and $a[p]=[a\cdot p]$. Therefore the quotient ring in step 1.1 has this specified complex vector-space structure. [F1, F6, F7, step 1.2, step 1.3]

3.1 For a finitely supported polynomial $p(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}a_jX^j$, extend its coefficient sequence by $a_j=0$ for every $j>N$, and write $p=qX^{k+1}+r$, where $q=\sum_{j=k+1}^{N}a_jX^{j-k-1}$ (the zero polynomial if $N\le k$) and $r=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(N,k)}a_jX^j$. Then $[p]=[r]=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_j[X^j]$ in the quotient vector space, so every quotient vector is a finite linear combination of $B=\{[1],[X],\ldots,[X^k]\}$. The span of $B$ is a linear subspace containing $B$ by the span definition in [F9], so it contains every such combination; hence $B$ spans the quotient. [given, F3, F9, step 1.1, step 2.1, algebra]

3.2 Suppose $\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_j[X^j]=0$. By quotient-coset equality this means $r=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_jX^j\in I$. By [F5], $r=qX^{k+1}$ for some polynomial $q$. Convolution multiplication in [F3] gives zero coefficients in degrees $0,\ldots,k$ for $qX^{k+1}$, whereas those coefficients of $r$ are $a_0,\ldots,a_k$; hence each $a_j=0$. The finite-list criterion [F9] gives linear independence of the displayed classes. [given, F3, F5, F7, F9, step 2.1, algebra]

4.1 Let $B=\{[1],[X],\ldots,[X^k]\}$. The map $j\mapsto[X^j]$ from $\{0,1,\ldots,k\}$ onto $B$ is injective: if $[X^i]=[X^j]$ for $i\ne j$, their difference gives a nontrivial vanishing linear combination, contradicting step 3.2. To check independence of the subset $B$, take any injective finite list from $B$ and assign its coefficients to the distinct exponent indices of its entries, with zero coefficients at the unused indices; step 3.2 forces all coefficients to vanish. Thus $B$ is linearly independent as a subset. Together with spanning from step 3.1, [F9] makes $B$ a basis with $k+1$ elements, so the dimension definition in [F9] gives $\dim_{\mathbb C}\operatorname{Jac}(W)=k+1$. This is an algebraic quotient calculation only; no infrared-CFT identification is part of this theorem. [F2, F9, step 3.1, step 3.2] ∎
