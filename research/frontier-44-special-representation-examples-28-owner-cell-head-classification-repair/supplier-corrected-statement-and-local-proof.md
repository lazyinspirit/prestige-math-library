# Nonzero cell heads classify the simple modules of a cellular algebra

## Statement

Let $F$ be a field and $A$ a finite-dimensional unital cellular $F$-algebra with cellular basis $(C,\Lambda)$ over the finite poset $\Lambda$ ([[def-cellular-algebra-basis-and-cell-module]]). For $\lambda\in\Lambda$ put $D^{\lambda}:=C^{\lambda}/\mathrm{rad}\,C^{\lambda}$, where $\mathrm{rad}\,C^{\lambda}$ is the radical of the cell form ([[lem-cell-form-is-associative-and-its-radical-is-a-submodule]]). Then:
1. if $D^{\lambda}\ne0$ then $D^{\lambda}$ is an absolutely irreducible (absolutely simple) $A$-module: it is simple over $F$ and remains simple after every extension of scalars to a larger field;
2. $\{D^{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda,\ D^{\lambda}\ne0\}$ is a complete set of pairwise non-isomorphic simple $A$-modules ([[def-simple-module]], [[thm-jordan-holder-theorem-for-modules]]);
3. $D^{\lambda}\ne0$ if and only if the cell form $\langle\ ,\ \rangle_\lambda$ is not identically zero, if and only if $\mathrm{rad}\,C^{\lambda}\ne C^{\lambda}$; and, when these equivalent conditions hold, $\mathrm{rad}\,C^{\lambda}$ is the unique maximal proper submodule and the Jacobson radical of $C^{\lambda}$, so $D^{\lambda}$ is its simple head. If the form is identically zero, the cell-form quotient $D^{\lambda}$ is zero; no assertion about the Jacobson radical or the actual head of $C^{\lambda}$ is made.
No semisimplicity of $A$ is assumed; the theorem is the Graham-Lehrer classification and applies in particular to $A=H_{F,q}(S_n)$ for every field $F$ and every $q\in F^{\times}$.

## Local proof strategy

Use the right-cell action identity $xC^{\lambda}_{uv}=\langle x,c^{\lambda}_u\rangle_\lambda c^{\lambda}_v$, obtained directly by comparing the cellular product modulo $A^{\rhd\lambda}$. The form radical is a submodule by [[lem-cell-form-is-associative-and-its-radical-is-a-submodule]]. If $x$ lies outside it, choose $y=\sum_u a_uc^{\lambda}_u$ with $\langle x,y\rangle_\lambda=1$ and put $b_v=\sum_u a_uC^{\lambda}_{uv}$. Then $xb_v=c^{\lambda}_v$ for every $v$. Thus every vector outside the form radical generates the entire cell module; every proper submodule lies in that radical. When the form is not identically zero, the radical is therefore the unique maximal proper submodule, the quotient is simple, and the radical equals the module Jacobson radical. The same rank-one computation after any field extension, together with base change of the kernel of a finite Gram matrix, proves absolute simplicity. A form is not identically zero exactly when its radical is proper, exactly when this quotient is nonzero; nondegeneracy of the original form instead means its radical is zero.
For distinctness, let $A^{\unrhd\nu}$ be the span of cells with labels at least $\nu$. These are two-sided ideals by the cellular axioms and the anti-involution. A product of a $\lambda$-cell basis element and a $\nu$-cell basis element lies in both upward-cell ideals. If $\lambda$ does not dominate $\nu$, all labels in their intersection are strictly above $\lambda$, so the $\nu$-cell basis elements act as zero on $C^{\lambda}$. In contrast the $\lambda$-cell elements act nontrivially on $D^{\lambda}$ when its form is nonzero, by the rank-one identity. If $D^{\lambda}\cong D^{\mu}\ne0$, comparison of these actions forces both $\lambda\unrhd\mu$ and $\mu\unrhd\lambda$, hence $\lambda=\mu$.
For completeness choose a linear extension listing larger cells first and let $0=I_0\subset I_1\subset\cdots\subset I_N=A$ be the corresponding upward-closed cellular ideals. If $\lambda$ is the newly added label at stage $j$, the right $A$-module $I_j/I_{j-1}$ is the direct sum of $|T(\lambda)|$ copies of $C^{\lambda}$, with the first basis index fixed in each copy. For any simple right module $S$, take the least $j$ with $SI_j\ne0$. Then $SI_j=S$ and $SI_{j-1}=0$. For some $s\in S$ the map $I_j/I_{j-1}\to S$, $a+I_{j-1}\mapsto sa$, is nonzero, so its restriction to one cell-module copy surjects onto $S$. If the $\lambda$-form were zero, the cellular multiplication rule would give $I_j^2\subset I_{j-1}$, contradicting $SI_j^2=(SI_j)I_j=S$. Its form is therefore nonzero and the already proved uniqueness of the maximal proper submodule forces $S\cong D^{\lambda}$. This proves exhaustion without an unsupported radical-factor induction. All sets and linear extensions are finite and no choice principle is used. Mathas Proposition 2.9 and Theorem 2.13 supply the rank-one and filtration arguments; the sentence after Theorem 2.13 in the fetched book uses “non-degenerate” where “not identically zero” is mathematically required and is corrected here.
