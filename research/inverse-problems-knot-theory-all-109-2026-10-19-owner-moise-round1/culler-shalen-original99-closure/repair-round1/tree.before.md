## Statement

Assume full AC. Let $N$ be a compact connected orientable smooth three-manifold whose boundary is a torus with supplied smooth meridian-longitude coordinates. Suppose $N$ is irreducible and its boundary is incompressible. Let $F/\mathbb C$ be a finitely generated field of transcendence degree one, equipped with the discrete valuation $v:F^*\to\mathbb Z$ at a point of a supplied smooth curve model, and let $\rho:\Gamma=\pi_1(N)\to SL_2(F)$ be a representation. Suppose its two commuting peripheral matrices have a common eigenline over $F$, with eigenvalues $L,M\in F^*$, and
$$\bigl(v(L),v(M)\bigr)\ne(0,0).$$
Then $N$ contains a compact connected neatly properly embedded orientable smooth surface $S$ with nonempty boundary, no compressing disk, no boundary-compressing disk, and no boundary-parallel component. Every boundary circle represents the unique primitive unoriented class $a[\lambda]+b[\mu]$ satisfying
$$a\,v(L)+b\,v(M)=0.$$
The surface is obtained from an actual equivariant map of the universal cover into the lattice tree by finite component deletions and compression homotopies. Each lifted surface component maps to one edge midpoint, and each complementary lifted component maps into the corresponding midpoint-complement component.

If $\rho$ is absolutely irreducible, $S$ is not a fiber of any fibration $N\to S^1$ with fiber $S$. No ideal point, Newton edge, or existence of this representation is inferred by this theorem; those are separate algebraic inputs.

## Proof

1.1 Write $\mathcal O=\{x:v(x)\ge0\}$ and choose a uniformizer $\pi$. Form the graph with vertices the homothety classes of free rank-two $\mathcal O$ lattices, and edges $\pi\Lambda\subsetneq\Lambda'\subsetneq\Lambda$ with one-dimensional residue quotient. Here is the actual tree verification. The finite rank-two elementary-divisor reduction works over $\mathcal O$: choose a nonzero matrix entry of least valuation, pivot it by invertible row and column operations, subtract its multiples to clear its row and column, and repeat on the remaining entry. Divisibility is exactly valuation comparison. Relative to a fixed root lattice $\Lambda_0$, every vertex therefore has a representative $\Lambda=\mathcal O e_1+\mathcal O\pi^d e_2\subset\Lambda_0$, primitive in $\Lambda_0$, where $e_1,e_2$ are a root basis and $d\ge0$. For $d>0$ there is exactly one neighbor of level $d-1$, represented by $\Lambda+\pi^{d-1}\Lambda_0$; all other neighbors have level $d+1$. Indeed the lines of $\Lambda/\pi\Lambda$ are the line of $\pi^d e_2$, giving that parent after homothety, and the lines of $e_1+c\pi^d e_2$, giving $\mathcal O(e_1+c\pi^d e_2)+\mathcal O\pi^{d+1}e_2$. Thus every vertex reaches the root, and a cycle would have a maximum-level vertex with two parents, impossible. This proves that the graph is a tree $T$, even if the residue field is infinite. The determinant valuation of a lattice basis modulo2 is independent of homothety; $SL_2(F)$ preserves it and adjacent vertices have opposite parity. The action is therefore without edge inversions. A determinant-one matrix fixing a vertex preserves its actual lattice, since a homothety multiplier has twice its valuation equal to zero. In its lattice basis it is in $SL_2(\mathcal O)$. Conversely a matrix with integral trace fixes a vertex: for a nonscalar matrix $A$, choose a cyclic vector $w$ and use $(w,Aw)$, in which Cayley–Hamilton gives the integral companion matrix with determinant1; a scalar matrix is $\pm I$. [A1, given, construct, algebra]

2.1 The peripheral action has a translation line and no fixed vertex. At least one peripheral matrix has an eigenvalue of nonzero valuation, and so cannot preserve a lattice, by its integral characteristic polynomial. That matrix has two distinct eigenvalues, both in $F$; its eigenspaces are preserved by the commuting other matrix. In their common eigenbasis the peripheral action on the lattice vertices $[\mathcal O e_1+\mathcal O\pi^j e_2]$, $j\in\mathbb Z$, is translation by $2v(L)$ and $2v(M)$, up to one common orientation sign. This is an embedded line in $T$ by step 1.1. Use the universal cover of [F7]. On a lifted marked boundary torus, whose universal parameter is $\mathbb R^2$, map $(x,y)$ linearly to coordinate $2xv(L)+2yv(M)+c$ on that line, with $c$ chosen off the finitely many vertex coincidences of a fundamental cell. It is peripheral-equivariant. If the boundary fundamental group has a kernel in $\Gamma$, that kernel acts trivially on the tree and its translation number is zero; the linear map is therefore invariant under it and descends to the actual boundary lift. Translate this map to all boundary lifts. No boundary-group injection has been assumed in constructing it. Its midpoint inverse image descends to finitely many smooth parallel essential circles. Extend the map over a small boundary collar by the product, retaining these smooth surface collars. Triangulate the marked flat boundary torus by its finite line sections and the ordinary periodic grid, so the boundary map is piecewise affine and the prescribed level circles have their smooth product collars. Product this finite cell triangulation with a short inward collar; its smooth differential fan is the actual product fan. By the protected relative construction in [F1], extend this to the whole manifold retaining a smaller such collar. On the universal cover of $N$, choose finitely many representative lifts of the vertices of this finite relative triangulation and assign tree vertices to them, extending equivariantly. Extend over edges by tree paths, then over faces and tetrahedra: their finite boundary images lie in finite subtrees, which contract by successively removing end edges, so coning gives actual finite PL extensions. Keep the given boundary collar fixed. Common finite subdivisions make these formulas piecewise linear away from that collar; ensure no domain vertex outside the protected product collar maps to a midpoint by a small relative subdivision and choice of its image off that discrete set. The inverse image of all edge midpoints is a compact finite locally flat PL surface with the fixed smooth boundary collars. Away from those already regular product collars, in the interior of a target edge, a nonconstant affine simplex section is a local product: its least incident domain simplex has a nonzero affine direction, as the level misses its vertices. There are finitely many such sections modulo $\Gamma$. No inversion means the stabilizer of an edge fixes its two ends; hence these local transverse directions have no monodromy along a component. The surface is two-sided and therefore orientable. [A1, F1, F7, step 1.1, construct]

3.1 Make this surface smooth using [F2], but also modify the actual equivariant map. This modification is essential: abstractly replacing a surface alone would not preserve its dual-map witness. The supplier's modifications are graph replacements in edge boxes and disk replacements in finitely many vertex balls, fixed on their attaching collars. In a sufficiently small such block the old map meets only the one target midpoint of the sheet being changed. Each replacement is a two-sided disk or strip with the same boundary separation as the original. For a disk in a ball that separation follows from [F3]; for a graph strip it follows directly from its signed graph coordinate. Give a small smooth collar of the replacement its signed normal coordinate mapping to the two adjacent halves of that tree edge, with zero at the midpoint. On either remaining side of the block the old prescribed boundary values lie in the same component $C$ of $T$ minus its midpoints as the new collar values. This $C$ is a convex subtree and is contractible to its unique old tree vertex. Extend the map on that side by this contraction, matching the old values on the outside block boundary. Concretely triangulate the finite boundary data and extend cells by coning to that vertex within $C$; relative collars give the matching continuous seams. Thus the replacement creates exactly the new smooth midpoint sheet and no other midpoint preimages. Lift each little ball or half-box, perform the construction there, and translate it to the disjoint deck translates. The modifications are equivariant. Their geodesic homotopy in the tree gives an actual equivariant homotopy, with the boundary collar fixed. We now have a map whose midpoint surface is smooth and neat and whose local map at each surface point is its smooth normal coordinate. Further maps considered below retain this regularity near their midpoint surfaces; away from them continuity and equivariance suffice. [F1, F2, F3, step 2.1, construct]

4.1 We record two local operations on such maps. Ordinary smooth disk witnesses may be tangent at their attaching faces, so first regularize the witness using [F6]. For compression, push its boundary into the interior of the surface by the finite inward flow tangent to the neat surface, cut along that surface, and apply angular neatification to the disk in its incident cut side, fixing its boundary. For boundary compression, make the open face arcs transverse by adding a positive small inward-source coordinate to their nonnegative face coordinate, and use [F6]’s explicit quadrant endpoint patches and graph-collar matches. These retain the essential arc class, give a corner disk with the usual neat product neighborhoods, and do not replace a broad nonneat premise by an assumption. A compressing disk has a small ball neighborhood intersecting the old surface in its boundary-circle band; a boundary-compressing disk has the corresponding half-ball neighborhood with its other face in $\partial N$. In a lift the disk interior misses all midpoint sheets, so its image is in one convex component $C$ of the midpoint complement, while its surface arc or circle maps to the one adjacent midpoint $m$. Compactness lets the neighborhood miss all other midpoints. Replace the old band by the two disk faces in an interior compression, or by the two arc shores in a boundary compression, rounding their finite product corners. Explicitly, take the small disk prism on the $C$ side: its lateral annular face is the old surface band, and its two disk faces lie inside that side. Reassign the prism to the opposite color. Its lateral face then disappears from the color frontier and its two disk faces become the new frontier, which is exactly compression. For boundary compression use the half-prism: its face on the ambient boundary is free to change color and the same frontier calculation is the arc-band surgery. Round the prism corners inside its product charts. This assigns the new complementary regions the same colors as on the unchanged outside boundary. Set the map on collars of the new sheets to their smooth normal coordinate, and extend on each color region by coning within its convex midpoint-complement component, exactly as in step 3.1. This is possible regardless of the topology of that region, since the target component is contractible. The formulas match the old map outside the disk neighborhood; on the half-ball they are allowed to change its boundary face, so the representation action, not an artificial boundary map, is kept fixed. All lifted disk neighborhoods are disjoint and their translates give the equivariant operation. The new surface is exactly the compressed oriented surface; the pointwise unique tree-path interpolation is an equivariant homotopy from the old map to the new one. This supplies both compression operations rather than assuming a homotopy from Hempel. [F3, F6, step 3.1, construct]

4.2 A component that bounds a ball, or cobounds a product with a boundary subsurface, can be erased together with all the surface components on that side. Enlarge the region slightly across its sheet so that its only interface with the unchanged interior lies on the adjacent midpoint-free side. For a ball its deck stabilizer is trivial. For a product region $A\times I$ its fundamental group is carried by its surface face $A$, and therefore fixes that face's midpoint, both edge endpoints and the adjacent vertex. Each lifted interface is mapped into the convex midpoint-complement component of that vertex. Extend those interface values over the whole region by the vertex contraction. For equivariance, use the same fixed vertex for its stabilizer and translate to the other lifted regions; this is well defined even if the product group has a kernel in $\Gamma$. The boundary portion is free to change. There are no midpoint preimages left in the region, and none is added outside it. Every other connected surface component is either wholly inside this region or outside, since it cannot cross its surface interface. The same tree-path interpolation supplies the homotopy. A sphere component bounds an interior ball by irreducibility. A disk component has inessential boundary by boundary-incompressibility of $N$; join it to its disk in the boundary torus, round slightly inward, and use irreducibility to get the ball between them. An inward collar of that boundary disk and the ball have the marked disk-window model, so undoing the rounding gives the required boundary product ball. Thus the operation erases all sphere and disk components by finitely many deletions. It also erases any boundary-parallel component, with all components in its chosen product region. [given, F3, step 3.1, construct]

5.1 Among regular equivariant maps reachable from the map of step 3.1 by FINITELY MANY of the operations in steps 4.1 and 4.2, with smooth neat midpoint surfaces having no sphere or disk components, minimize lexicographically the triple $$\left(\sum_Q\max(0,-\chi(Q)),\ \sum_Qg(Q),\ \#\{Q:Q\text{ is an annulus}\}\right).$$ The class is nonempty by steps 3.1 and 4.2 and is closed under appending another compression and its finite deletions; every member comes with its finite history. Choose the least occurring nonnegative integer at each of the three entries. It is not necessary to choose an infinite compression sequence. The midpoint surface cannot be empty: a connected universal cover would then map entirely into one midpoint-complement component; equivariance would make its unique old vertex fixed by all of $\Gamma$, contradicting step 2.1. Nor can its boundary be empty: the same argument on a connected lift of the boundary torus would give a peripheral fixed vertex. Every boundary-parallel component is an orientable surface, and, once spheres and disks are excluded, contributes positively to at least one entry of this triple by [F4]. Erasing its product region by step 4.2 deletes whole components and strictly decreases the displayed triple: a negative-Euler component decreases the first entry, a torus decreases the second, and an annulus decreases the third if the earlier entries did not decrease. This contradicts minimality. Thus the selected surface has no boundary-parallel component. [F4, step 2.1, step 3.1, step 4.2, construct, algebra]

6.1 There is no compressing or boundary-compressing disk for this surface. If one existed, perform step 4.1 and erase any resulting disks and spheres by step 4.2. On a component of negative Euler characteristic, interior compression increases $\chi$ by two, and boundary compression increases it by one. Essentiality excludes an inessential disk cut off by the surgery. A boundary-parallel essential loop may produce a disk, but deleting that disk still leaves an increase of one on the other component. Thus the sum of negative Euler characteristics strictly decreases in every such case. The only remaining orientable types are tori and annuli by [F4]. A torus compression produces a sphere, lowering total genus with first entry zero. An annulus compression or essential boundary compression produces disks, lowering the annulus count without increasing the earlier entries. Other components only disappear in the deletions, so cannot increase any entry. Each case contradicts the minimum. Notice that boundary compressions were permitted to change the boundary map; the action stayed the SAME, and the nonelliptic peripheral action in step 5.1 still forces a boundary component afterward. Thus no assertion that arbitrary boundary compressions preserve individual boundary circles is needed. [F4, step 4.1, step 4.2, step 5.1, construct, algebra]

7.1 Every boundary circle is essential on the torus. If not, choose one innermost there, bounding a torus disk whose interior misses the boundary surface curves. Push that disk just inside the boundary collar and attach the small annulus in the surface collar. This is a disk with boundary a parallel interior circle of the surface and interior disjoint from the surface. Its boundary is essential unless the surface component is a disk, already excluded. It would be a compressing disk, contrary to step 6.1. An essential simple circle on a torus represents a nonzero primitive class; lifting to the plane, or cutting along it and using its annulus complement, proves primitivity. A lift of a surviving boundary circle maps constantly to one midpoint, so its deck element fixes that midpoint. By [F5] it satisfies $a v(L)+b v(M)=0$. The nonzero valuation pair has a rank-one primitive integral kernel, so all these essential circles have precisely the same unoriented primitive class. Choose any connected component with boundary. Steps 5.1 and 6.1 give its incompressibility, boundary-incompressibility and nonparallelism, and its orientation and smooth neatness were retained by all operations. All requested assertions except the optional nonfiber clause are proved. [F5, step 2.1, step 5.1, step 6.1, construct, algebra]

8.1 Suppose now that $\rho$ is absolutely irreducible and that this connected surface is a fiber. The bundle projection gives an exact sequence $1\to H\to\Gamma\to\mathbb Z\to1$, with $H$ the image of its fundamental group: lift the base to $\mathbb R$, trivialize over successive intervals, and identify the resulting cover with the fiber times $\mathbb R$. Its fundamental group is $H$, and one loop over the base generator gives $t$ with $\Gamma=\langle H,t\rangle$. The lifted surface maps to one midpoint, so $H$ fixes its edge and a vertex $q$. Its fixed-vertex set is a nonempty convex subtree $C$, invariant under $t$ by normality. If $t$ fixed a vertex $w$, project $w$ to the nearest vertex of $C$. The nearest vertex exists by the least nonnegative integral distance and is unique by the tree path property. Projection is preserved by every tree automorphism preserving $C$, so that vertex would be fixed by $t$ and by $H$, hence by $\Gamma$. Step 2.1 forbids this. Therefore $t$ fixes no vertex; step 1.1 gives $v(\operatorname{tr}\rho(t))<0$. Its two eigenvalues have distinct opposite nonzero valuations in a finite extension of $F$ with an extended discrete valuation. The extension exists by the actual valuation-extension proof in [F5]; diagonalize $\rho(t)$ there as $\operatorname{diag}(z,z^{-1})$, $v(z)\ne0$. [A1, F5, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 7.1, construct, algebra]

9.1 Every $h\in H$ preserves the lattice $q$. Normality says that $t^{-n}ht^n$ does so for EVERY $n\in\mathbb Z$. In a basis of $q$ its matrix entries are therefore all integral. Changing to the fixed eigenbasis of $\rho(t)$ gives a single finite lower valuation bound, independent of $n$, on every entry. If $\rho(h)$ in that basis is $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$, the off-diagonal entries of $\rho(t)^{-n}\rho(h)\rho(t)^n$ are $z^{-2n}b$ and $z^{2n}c$. Their valuations can be bounded below for all positive and negative $n$ only if $b=c=0$. Thus every $\rho(h)$ is diagonal in this eigenbasis, as is $\rho(t)$. Since $\Gamma=\langle H,t\rangle$, the whole representation preserves its two eigenlines over that finite extension. This contradicts absolute irreducibility. The chosen surface is not a fiber. The construction gave an actual dual-map witness throughout, including both kinds of compression and the component deletions, and all slope and strictness claims follow from that witness and the explicit matrix argument. [step 8.1, algebra] ∎
