
 IV.2  Fibonacci Anyons: a Simple Example
which is Universal for Quantum Computation 

 
 One of the simplest models of non-Abelian statistics is known as the
Fibonacci anyon model, or “Golden
theory”  [ 118 ,  262 ,  38 ,  160 ] . In this
model, there are only two fields, the identity ( 𝟏 \bf 1 ) as well as
single nontrivial field usually called  τ \tau  which represents the
non-Abelian quasiparticle. (Note there is no field representing the
underlying electron in this simplified theory). There is a single
nontrivial fusion rule in this model 
 

 
  
 τ × τ = 𝟏 + τ \tau\times\tau={\bf 1}+\tau 
  
 (161) 
 
 which results in the Bratteli diagram given in
Fig.  9 b. This model is particularly simple in that
any cluster of quasiparticles can fuse only to  𝟏 \bf 1  or  τ \tau . 
 
 
 The  j = 0 j=0  and  j = 1 j=1  quasiparticles in SU(2) 3  satisfy the fusion
rules of Fibonacci anyons. Therefore, if we simply omit the  j = 1 / 2 j=1/2 
and  j = 3 / 2 j=3/2  quasiparticles from SU(2) 3 , we will have FIbonacci
anyons. This is perfectly consistent since half-integral  j j  will
never arise from the fusions of integral  j j s; the model with only
integer spins can be called SO(3) 2  or, sometimes, ‘the even part
of SU(2) 3 ’. As a result of the connection to SU(2) 3 , sometimes
 𝟏 \bf 1  is called q-spin “0” and  τ \tau  is called q-spin “1” (see
 [ 160 ] ).  ℤ 3 \mathbb{Z}_{3}  parafermions are equivalent to a
coset theory SU(2) 3 /U(1). This can be realized with an SU(2) 3 
WZW model in which the U(1) subgroup is coupled to a gauge field
 [ 129 ,  177 ] . Consequently,  ℤ 3 \mathbb{Z}_{3} 
parafermions have essentially the same fusion rules as SU(2) 3 ;
there are some phase differences between the two theories which show
up in the  R R  and  F F -matrices. In the  ℤ 3 \mathbb{Z}_{3}  parafermion
theory, the field  ϵ \epsilon  which results from fusing  σ 1 \sigma_{1} 
with  ψ 1 \psi_{1}  satisfies the Fibonacci fusion rule Eq.
 161 , i.e.,  ϵ × ϵ = 𝟏 + ϵ \epsilon\times\epsilon={\bf 1}+\epsilon . 
 
 
 As with the  ℤ 3 \mathbb{Z}_{3}  parafermion model described above, the
dimension of the Hilbert space with  n n  quasiparticles (i.e., the
number of paths through the Bratteli diagram  9 b
terminating at  𝟏 \bf 1 ) is given by the Fibonacci number
 Fib ​ ( n − 1 ) \mbox{Fib}(n-1) , hence the name Fibonacci anyons. And similarly
the number terminating at  τ \tau  is  Fib ​ ( n ) \mbox{Fib}(n) . Therefore, the
quantum dimension of the  τ \tau  particle is the golden mean,
 d τ = ϕ ≡ ( 1 + 5 ) / 2 {d_{\tau}}=\phi\equiv(1+\sqrt{5})/2  (from which the theory receives
the name “golden” theory). The Fibonacci model is the simplest
known non-Abelian model that is capable of universal quantum
computation  [ 118 ] . (In the next section, the proof will
be described for SU(2) 3 , but the Fibonacci theory, which is its
even part, is also universal.) It is thus useful to study this model
in some detail. Many of the principles that are described here will
generalize to other non-Abelian models. We note that a detailed
discussion of computing with the Fibonacci model is also given in
 160 . 
 
 
 (a) Structure of the Hilbert Space:  
An important feature of non-Abelian systems is the detailed structure
of the Hilbert space. A given state in the space will be described
by a “fusion path”, or “fusion tree” (See appendix
 A ). For example, using the fusion rule
( 161 ), or examining the Bratteli diagram we see
that when two  τ \tau  particles are present, they may fuse into two
possible orthogonal degenerate states – one in which they fuse to
form  𝟏 \bf 1  and one in which they fuse to form  τ \tau . A convenient
notation  [ 38 ]  for these two states is  | ( ∙ , ∙ ) 𝟏 ⟩ |(\bullet,\bullet)_{\bf 1}\rangle  and  | ( ∙ , ∙ ) τ ⟩ |(\bullet,\bullet)_{\tau}\rangle .
Here, each  ∙ \bullet  represents a particle. From the fusion rule,
when a third is added to two particles already in the  𝟏 \bf 1  state
(i.e., in  | ( ∙ , ∙ ) 𝟏 ⟩ |(\bullet,\bullet)_{\bf 1}\rangle ) it must fuse to form
 τ \tau . We denote the resulting state as  | ( ( ∙ , ∙ ) 𝟏 , ∙ ) τ ⟩ ≡ | 0 ⟩ |((\bullet,\bullet)_{\bf 1},\bullet)_{\tau}\rangle\equiv|0\rangle . But if the third is
added to two in the  τ \tau  state, it may fuse to form either  τ \tau 
or  𝟏 \bf 1 , giving the two states  | ( ( ∙ , ∙ ) τ , ∙ ) τ ⟩ ≡ | 1 ⟩ |((\bullet,\bullet)_{\tau},\bullet)_{\tau}\rangle\equiv|1\rangle  and  | ( ( ∙ , ∙ ) τ , ∙ ) 𝟏 ⟩ ≡ | N ⟩ |((\bullet,\bullet)_{\tau},\bullet)_{\bf 1}\rangle\equiv|N\rangle 
respectively. (The notations  | 0 ⟩ , | 1 ⟩ |0\rangle,|1\rangle  and
 | N ⟩ |N\rangle  will be discussed further below). Thus we have a three
dimensional Hilbert space for three particles shown using several
notations in Fig.  14 .

 (b) Braiding Fibonacci Anyons:  As discussed in the
introduction, for non-Abelian systems, adiabatically braiding
particles around each other results in a unitary operation on the
degenerate Hilbert space. Here we attempt to determine which unitary
operation results from which braid. We start by considering what
happens to two Fibonacci particles when they are braided around each
other. It is known  [ 126 ]  that the topological spin  Θ τ \Theta_{\tau}  of a
Fibonacci field  τ \tau  is  Θ τ ≡ e 2 ​ π ​ i ​ Δ τ = e 4 ​ π ​ i / 5 \Theta_{\tau}\equiv e^{2\pi i\Delta_{\tau}}=e^{4\pi i/5} . (Note that  Δ τ \Delta_{\tau}  is also the dimension of the  ϵ \epsilon 
field of the  ℤ 3 \mathbb{Z}_{3}  theory, see Appendix  A .) With
this information, we can use the OPE
(see Appendix  A )as in section  III.4 
above, to determine the phase accumulated when two particles wrap
around each other. If the two  τ \tau  fields fuse together to form
 𝟏 \bf 1 , then taking the two fields around each other clockwise
results in a phase  − 8 π / 5 = 2 π ( − 2 Δ τ ) -8\pi/5=2\pi(-2\Delta_{\tau})  whereas if
the two fields fuse to form  τ \tau , taking the two fields around
each other results in a phase  − 4 π / 5 = 2 π ( − Δ τ ) -4\pi/5=2\pi(-\Delta_{\tau}) .
Note that a Fibonacci theory with the opposite chirality can exist
too (an “antiholomorphic theory”), in which case one accumulates
the opposite phase. A particularly interesting non-chiral (or
“achiral”) theory also exists which is equivalent to a combination
of two chiral Fibonacci theories with opposite
chiralities. In section  III.7 , we discussed
lattice spin models  [ 212 ]  which give rise to
a non-chiral (or “achiral”) theory which is equivalent to a combination
of two chiral Fibonacci theories with opposite
chiralities. We will not discuss these theories further here.
