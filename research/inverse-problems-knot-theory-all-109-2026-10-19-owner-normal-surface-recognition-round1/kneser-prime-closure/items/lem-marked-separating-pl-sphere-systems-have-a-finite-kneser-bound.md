## Statement

Let $N$ be a compact connected orientable PL three-manifold with a supplied finite simplicial triangulation $T$, and suppose every embedded PL two-sphere in its interior separates. Let $f$ be the number of triangular faces of $T$, counting boundary faces once, and let $b_2=\dim_{\mathbb F_2}H_2(N;\mathbb F_2)$. Let $\mathcal S$ be a finite disjoint family of $m$ embedded PL two-spheres in $\operatorname{Int}N$. Cut along them using disjoint finite PL product collars. Every part of the original boundary $\partial N$ is marked.

A cut component is an **unmarked punctured PL three-sphere** if it contains no original boundary point and admits a finite PL parameter as standard $S^3$ with finitely many disjoint interiors of parametrized PL balls removed. Suppose no cut component is such a manifold. Then
$$m+1\le 4f+b_2.$$

For this purpose the system can be replaced by a system of the same cardinality satisfying the same marked condition, such that every tetrahedral intersection is a disk and every face intersection consists only of normal arcs. The replacements use isotopies and surgery on a sphere followed by retention of one of its two children. They need not preserve the individual sphere isotopy classes or produce triangle/quadrilateral disk types. Original boundary components, including sphere components, remain marked and unchanged.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The finite PL manifold, the separating-sphere hypothesis, the marked boundary, and the finite sphere system of the Statement.

[F1] A finite PL sphere in standard PL three-space or three-sphere bounds parametrized PL balls, with the asserted complementary-side identifications. [[thm-finite-pl-schoenflies-in-dimension-three]]

[F2] Parametrized PL balls glue across prescribed boundary-disk windows to give a parametrized ball; boundary-disk maps extend across them, and a ball attached at a standard boundary window can be removed leaving a ball. [[lem-parametrized-pl-balls-absorb-standard-boundary-windows]]

[F3] Compact PL boundary subpolyhedra have finite PL collars. Compact PL embeddings and finite convex cell decompositions admit compatible finite subdivisions. [[lem-compact-locally-collared-pl-subpolyhedron-has-pl-collar]] [[lem-pl-embedded-polyhedron-admits-compatible-subcomplex-triangulation]] [[lem-finite-convex-cell-complexes-admit-compatible-triangulations]]

[F4] A connected finite closed PL surface has Euler characteristic at most two; characteristic two gives a sphere and characteristic one gives a projective plane. [[lem-positive-euler-finite-pl-surfaces-are-disks-spheres-or-projective-planes]]

[F5] Disjoint connected closed locally flat surfaces in a connected boundaryless three-manifold, with connected total complement, give independent mod-two homology classes, including one-sided surfaces. [[lem-connected-complement-surfaces-have-independent-mod-two-homology-classes]]

[F6] Finite simplicial homology computes singular homology. A boundary collar makes the inclusion of the interior a homotopy equivalence, by pushing the collar a positive distance inward. [[thm-simplicial-and-singular-homology-agree-for-simplicial-complexes]] [[thm-homotopy-equivalences-induce-isomorphisms-on-singular-homology]]

## Proof

1.1 We record the finite ball operations being used. A proper PL disk $D$ in a parametrized ball $B$ cuts $B$ into two parametrized balls. Its boundary cuts $\partial B$ into two parametrized disks by the polygonal disk construction in [F2]. Cap $D$ by either disk, using a small corner collar. Apply [F1] in the double of $B$; the side on the inside of $B$ is its parametrized ball. Its contact with $\partial B$ has the standard quadrant window supplied by the transverse disk collar. Removing it leaves a parametrized ball by [F2]. For a disjoint finite disk system repeat this argument in the balls already obtained. Thus every complementary region of a finite proper-disk system is a parametrized ball. Boundary maps can be prescribed: extend maps across the complementary boundary disks and then cone the resulting sphere map between convex ball models. The same argument shows that disjoint spheres in a punctured standard PL sphere cut it into punctured standard PL spheres: fill its finitely many holes, apply [F1] successively, then remove the hole balls again. No assertion about spheres in an arbitrary ambient manifold is used. [F1, F2, F3, construct]

2.1 Suppose a sphere $S_i$ is surgered on an embedded disk whose interior misses the whole system. The two children and $S_i$, moved slightly apart, bound a parametrized three-punctured sphere $P$: it is the collar $S_i\times I$ with a thickened disk attached on its outer side. Equivalently, cap its inner sphere by a ball; the disk neighborhood is a standard boundary window, and [F2] identifies the result as a ball with two interior balls removed. Since every sphere separates, the two children cut the old component on that side into $B',B''$; call the component on the other side $A$. If both $B',B''$ were unmarked punctured spheres, gluing them to $P$ would make the old component one, contrary to the hypothesis. Choose a child adjacent to a $B'$ that is not one. The other new component is $A\cup P\cup B''$. If this were an unmarked punctured sphere, its embedded sphere separating off $A$ and the disk-cut assertion of step 1.1 would make $A$ an unmarked punctured sphere too. This is impossible. Retain this child and discard the other. The system still has $m$ spheres and no forbidden component. The argument retains all original-boundary marks: a union with an original boundary point cannot become unmarked. [F1, F2, step 1.1, given, construct]

3.1 Put the system in finite PL general position with respect to $T$, missing vertices and transverse to edges and faces. A finite compatible subdivision and successive small generic vertex displacements do this; excluded displacements lie in finitely many proper affine planes. Let $w$ count original-edge intersections. Among all systems obtainable by the allowed operations and still satisfying the marked condition, choose least $w$, then least $\lambda=\sum_P(b(P)-1)$, where $P$ ranges over the components inside tetrahedra and $b(P)$ is its number of boundary circles. Each $P$ is planar because it is a subsurface of a sphere. No closed $P$ occurs: an entire sphere inside one tetrahedron bounds a ball by [F1]; an innermost such ball gives an unmarked punctured-sphere cut component. Thus $b(P)\ge1$ and $\lambda\ge0$. Both minima are minima of nonempty subsets of natural numbers. [F1, F3, step 2.1, given, construct]

4.1 If a tetrahedral piece is not a disk, select a boundary circle $c$ innermost among boundary circles of the non-disk pieces on that tetrahedral boundary sphere. The disk region bounded by $c$ there contains only boundaries of disk pieces. Prepare these finitely many disks in innermost order near that boundary region. The proper-disk cutting parameters of step 1.1 make each preparation an isotopy in its ball, relative its attaching boundary; remaining pieces cannot enter that ball without a boundary circle there or crossing its proper disk. Closed pieces have already been excluded. A parallel copy of the resulting boundary cap, joined to the inward collar of $c$ on its non-disk piece, gives a surgery disk wholly in the open tetrahedron and missing every other piece. Its boundary is a small inward parallel of $c$ and misses the original edges. Surgery splits that planar piece into a disk and a piece with one fewer boundary circle; all other old pieces are merely distributed among the two sphere children. For either child the total $\lambda$ decreases by at least one, unless original edge intersections were discarded, which already decreases $w$. Select the child guaranteed by step 2.1. It contradicts the chosen minima. Hence every tetrahedral piece is a disk. The preparations have not introduced any edge intersection. [F1, F2, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.1, construct]

5.1 There is no circle in a face intersection. An innermost such circle bounds disk pieces in the two incident tetrahedra. Their union is an entire sphere of the system. The face disk and the two proper disks cut off a parametrized ball by step 1.1; no other sphere can be wholly enclosed without either an interior tetrahedral sphere or another face circle inside the chosen face disk. An innermost enclosed sphere, if any, would in any case give a forbidden cut component. Thus the marked condition is contradicted. Boundary faces have no intersection since the spheres lie in the interior. If a face arc has both endpoints on one edge, choose an edgemost one. It and the edge interval bound a face bigon whose interior misses the system. In the finite product chart of the edge star, pushing the surface strip across this bigon removes two original-edge intersections and introduces none. This is an isotopy of the sphere system and preserves the marked condition. Reapply the disk preparation if necessary; it never increases $w$. Minimality of $w$ excludes this return. All face arcs therefore join different edges. [F1, F3, step 1.1, step 3.1, step 4.1, construct]

6.1 In any triangular face a disjoint normal-arc family has at most four nonrectangular complementary regions: at most one containing each of its three vertices and at most one central region. Indeed arcs cutting off each given vertex are nested; consecutive arcs of that type bound rectangles, and deleting these rectangular strips leaves the three outer corner regions and the possible central region. Empty families give just the whole triangular region. Charge every cut component meeting a nonrectangular face region to its first such region. Different components cannot use the same region. There are consequently at most $4f$ such components, including any component meeting the original boundary. [step 5.1, given, construct]

7.1 Consider a remaining cut component $R$. Its intersection with each face consists only of rectangles. Give their two sides on original edges compatible interval parameters and extend across the rectangles, making their other two sides horizontal. On the boundary sphere of a tetrahedron these rectangles form finite cycles: each has two edge-side ends, each glued to just one next rectangle across that edge. Each cycle is an annulus, not a Möbius strip since it embeds in the oriented tetrahedral boundary sphere. Its horizontal boundary consists of two disk-piece boundary circles. Step 1.1 cuts the tetrahedron into parametrized balls. For one such ball region, its boundary is connected; each annular portion of its old tetrahedral boundary, capped by its two disk faces, is already a closed sphere, so it must be its entire boundary. Thus this ball region has exactly one such annulus and two horizontal disks. Prescribe its annulus parameter and extend the induced circle maps over these disks, then cone the whole boundary map over the ball. This gives a parametrized $D^2\times I$ agreeing with the prescribed rectangular face data. [F1, F2, F3, step 1.1, step 6.1, construct]

8.1 These products paste to an actual PL interval bundle on $R$. To make the face data coherent first parametrize each interval gap on each original edge once, and extend the corresponding ordered intervals across every rectangular face region. Along a rectangle cycle the interval closing map preserves order; it can be absorbed into its last strip by a finite fibre-preserving PL map. Explicitly take a common breakpoint subdivision for an increasing interval map and the identity, triangulate each product rectangle by a diagonal, keep the first coordinate, and linearly interpolate the second-coordinate vertex values. Each triangle has positive derivative in the interval direction, so each fibre map is increasing and invertible. Step 7.1 extends this prescribed annulus data through each ball; identified tetrahedra now have identical face fibres. The quotient base has disk charts from the product balls, half-disk seams across faces and cyclic vertex links around each original edge gap, so is a closed connected finite PL surface $B$. The horizontal boundary $\partial R$ is its associated two-sheeted endpoint cover. There is no vertical boundary because $R$ has no exceptional face region and hence misses $\partial N$. [F2, F3, step 7.1, construct]

9.1 Every component of $\partial R$ is an entire sphere of the cut system. If the endpoint cover is disconnected, each component maps homeomorphically to $B$, so $B=S^2$ and the endpoint labels trivialize the interval bundle. Increasing transition maps can be straightened by the same finite interpolation as step 8.1, giving $R=S^2\times I$, an unmarked twice-punctured sphere, forbidden. If that cover is connected it is a sphere; its Euler characteristic is twice that of $B$, by lifting a finite cell structure, so $\chi(B)=1$. By [F4], $B$ is $\mathbb {RP}^2$. An interval bundle is determined by this endpoint cover: after labeling endpoints on each trivializing cell, increasing transition maps are removed by finite interpolation, leaving only the endpoint permutations. For the standard disk model of $\mathbb {RP}^2$, its one-cell circle is the sole generator and the two-cell attaches twice around it. A connected two-sheeted cover must swap the endpoint labels on that generator; the disk interior has trivial endpoint monodromy. Cutting along that circle therefore gives the two standard disks glued with the antipodal boundary pairing. This identifies the endpoint cover with $S^2\to\mathbb {RP}^2$. Removing the increasing parts of the transition maps as just described identifies the interval bundle itself with its mapping cylinder, the twisted interval bundle over $\mathbb {RP}^2$. This is standard PL $\mathbb {RP}^3$ minus a ball, by the antipodal-boundary ball model and its boundary collar. In particular $R$ contains a closed locally flat one-sided projective-plane core, and the complement of that core in $R$ is connected. [F3, F4, step 8.1, construct, algebra]

10.1 Take the one-sided cores in all these remaining components. They are disjoint. Their total complement in $\operatorname{Int}N$ is connected: a finite polygonal path may be cut at its entrances into these regions and rerouted inside the connected complement of each core, with the same entrances. The boundary sphere of a twisted interval bundle is connected, so the rerouting can also be made on a small inner collar of it. Only finitely many crossings are involved after PL general position. By [F5], the cores give independent classes in $H_2(\operatorname{Int}N;\mathbb F_2)$. A finite boundary collar and [F6] identify this group with $H_2(N;\mathbb F_2)$, of finite dimension $b_2$ because it is computed by finite matrices. There are at most $b_2$ remaining components. [F3, F5, F6, step 9.1, construct]

11.1 Each separating sphere increases the number of cut components by one; equivalently the dual graph is a tree. Thus there are exactly $m+1$ components. Steps 6.1 and 10.1 bound their number by $4f+b_2$. Steps 2.1–5.1 justified the replacement without changing $m$ or losing the marked condition. All selections were finite or natural minima, the products were constructed with actual prescribed face maps, and the only Schoenflies applications had standard PL ambient parameters. This proves every clause. [step 2.1, step 5.1, step 6.1, step 10.1, algebra] ∎
