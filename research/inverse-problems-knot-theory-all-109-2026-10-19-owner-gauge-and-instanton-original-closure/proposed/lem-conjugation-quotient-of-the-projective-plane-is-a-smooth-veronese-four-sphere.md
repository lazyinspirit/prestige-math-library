## Statement

Let $\tau$ be complex conjugation on $\mathbb{CP}^2$. Give its quotient the smooth branched structure which, along the real locus, replaces the two real normal coordinates $(y_2,y_3)$ by the real and imaginary parts of $(y_2+iy_3)^2$. There is an explicit $SO(3)$-equivariant diffeomorphism of this quotient onto the unit sphere in the five-dimensional space of trace-free real symmetric $3$-by-$3$ matrices. The branch surface maps onto
$$B=\{(3uu^T-I)/\sqrt6:u\in S^2\}.$$
Thus the underlying smooth quotient is a four-sphere with this specified Veronese projective plane. Relative to the quotient orientation induced by the complex orientation of $\mathbb{CP}^2$, the branch surface has normal Euler number $-2$; reversing that ambient orientation gives $+2$.

For sufficiently small $\epsilon>0$, the part of $B$ represented by $|u_3|\le\epsilon$ is an inward push of the explicit unknotted half-twisted band $[(\theta,t)]\mapsto(\sqrt{R^2-t^2}e^{2i\theta},te^{i\theta})$ in a level three-sphere, with $(\theta+\pi,-t)\sim(\theta,t)$. The complementary disk in the other four-ball is smoothly boundary-parallel.

This is an explicit quotient identification. It does not identify an arbitrary embedded projective plane from its normal Euler number, or assert that a particular saddle movie yields this embedding.

## Proof

**Given:** The standard complex projective plane, its conjugation involution and the displayed real matrix space with its trace inner product.

[F1] [[thm-euclidean-inverse-function-theorem]]: A $C^1$ map with invertible derivative has a $C^1$ local inverse, with derivative the inverse derivative matrix. Iterating that formula for smooth coefficients makes the inverse smooth.



1.1 Write a projective point as $[x+iy]$ with $x,y\in\mathbb R^3$. A phase rotation diagonalizes their real two-by-two Gram matrix. After normalization and ordering its eigenvalues the representative is $$R(\cos r\,e_1+i\sin r\,e_2),\qquad R\in SO(3),\quad0\le r\le\pi/4.$$ For $0<r<\pi/4$ the ordered perpendicular axes are unique up to their signs. Projective sign identifies the simultaneous changes of $e_1,e_2$, and conjugation identifies the change of $e_2,e_3$. Consequently the orbit in the quotient is $SO(3)/V_4$, where $V_4$ consists of diagonal sign matrices of determinant one. At $r=0$ only the unoriented first axis matters, giving a real projective plane; at $r=\pi/4$ only the unoriented third axis matters, also giving a real projective plane. These statements follow directly from equality of the two ordered Gram eigenvalues or vanishing of the second one, rather than from a classification of group actions. [given, construct] 

2.1 Put $$A(\phi)=\cos\phi\,\operatorname{diag}(2,-1,-1)/\sqrt6+\sin\phi\,\operatorname{diag}(0,1,-1)/\sqrt2,\qquad0\le\phi\le\pi/3.$$ This traces the ordered eigenvalue chamber on the unit matrix sphere. Its interior has three distinct eigenvalues and stabilizer $V_4$; its endpoints have a repeated eigenvalue and stabilizers the groups preserving the unoriented first and third axes respectively. Choose a smooth increasing function $\phi(r)$ on the open interval with $$\phi(r)=c_0\tan^2r\text{ near }0,\qquad\pi/3-\phi(r)=c_1\tan(\pi/4-r)\text{ near }\pi/4,$$ where $c_0,c_1>0$. For existence first choose the positive derivative equal to the derivatives of these formulas in small endpoint intervals, with constants sufficiently small that its integral is below $\pi/3$, and add a nonnegative interior bump with the required remaining integral. Integrating defines the desired function, including both endpoint formulas. Set $$\Psi([R(\cos r\,e_1+i\sin r\,e_2)])=R A(\phi(r))R^T.$$ The stabilizer computations make this well-defined on the quotient, continuous and bijective, hence a homeomorphism by compactness. At $r=0$ its value is precisely the displayed $B$. Smoothness at the two exceptional orbits still requires the following coordinate checks. [step 1.1, construct] 

3.1 Near the real orbit, phase normalization gives $[u+iy]$ with $|u|=1$, $y\perp u$ small. The equivalences are $(u,y)\sim(-u,-y)$ and, after conjugation, $y\sim-y$. They are exact coordinates: the larger Gram eigenvalue has a uniquely determined real axis and phase up to sign, and its smooth dependence follows either from the two-by-two eigenvalue formula with distinct eigenvalues or [F1]. In a tangent frame of $u^\perp$, the quotient normal coordinate is $w=(y_2+iy_3)^2$. If an element of $SO(3)$ preserving the line of $u$ acts as $u\mapsto\epsilon u$ and $y\mapsto By$, its action in these coordinates is $y\mapsto\epsilon By$; squaring removes $\epsilon$. Thus the normal action is exactly the action on trace-free symmetric matrices on $u^\perp$, by $C\mapsto BCB^T$, of rotation weight two and reflection conjugation. In the matrix sphere its normal coordinates can be chosen by diagonalizing the separated first eigenline and taking the remaining two-by-two trace-free block; [F1] makes these actual smooth coordinates. For $y=\tan r\,e_2$, the image block has positive amplitude proportional to $\sin\phi(r)$. Since $|w|=\tan^2r$ and $\phi=c_0|w|$, the quotient-to-target normal map is a positive constant times $w\sin(c_0|w|)/|w|$. The scalar factor is a smooth function of $|w|^2$, with nonzero value at zero. The separated eigenline is unchanged. This proves smoothness and invertibility of the derivative there, so [F1] gives a local diffeomorphism. [F1, step 2.1, construct] 

4.1 Near the isotropic conic, put $u=(e_1+ie_2)/\sqrt2$, $v=(e_1-ie_2)/\sqrt2$. A normal slice is $[u+t v]$ for small complex $t$. Rotations about $e_3$ act on $t$ with weight two. Rotation by $\pi$ about $e_1$ composed with conjugation acts by $t\mapsto\bar t$. The conic and its conjugate are identified freely, so the quotient slice is the associated disk bundle over the unoriented third axis with this weight-two/reflection action; it is not an additional branched slice. These are actual local coordinates: the differential of rotations moving the third axis supplies the two tangent directions, while the real and imaginary parts of $t$ supply its two independent normal directions. Apply [F1] in local group coordinates. For real $t\ge0$, the normal form of step 1.1 gives $t=\tan(\pi/4-r)$. The target repeated-eigenvalue slice has the same weight-two/reflection action, and its normal amplitude is proportional to $\sin(\pi/3-\phi)$. Thus its normal coordinate is a positive constant times $t\sin(c_1|t|)/|t|$, again smooth with invertible derivative at zero. The third axis is unchanged. This proves smoothness and local invertibility at the other endpoint. On interior orbits both the orbit coordinates and the increasing radial coordinate are smooth with smooth inverse. The global bijection is therefore a diffeomorphism everywhere. [F1, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.1, construct] 

5.1 For the Euler sign use the orientation double cover $S^2\to B=\mathbb{RP}^2$. The quotient normal bundle pulls back to the weight-two plane bundle $\operatorname{Sym}^2_0(TS^2)$ with the squared normal coordinate of step 3.1. The tangent plane of $S^2$ has clutching degree two, calibrated positively by the gradient of height, whose two zeros have derivative $+I$ and $-I$ and both have positive local index: in the two oriented stereographic coordinates the coordinate change is $z\mapsto1/z$, whose derivative is $-z^{-2}$ on the equator; with the upper-to-lower boundary orientation this has degree two. Squaring the rotation transition gives clutching degree four. A section constant and nonzero on one hemisphere extends over the other with signed zero count equal to that boundary winding: cut into small disks around its zeros and sum boundary winding, the internal boundary contributions cancel. Hence the pulled-back Euler number is four in the fiber orientation of $(\Re w,\Im w)$. At $[e_1]$ the complex orientation is $(x_2,y_2,x_3,y_3)$, which is the negative of the base/normal order $(x_2,x_3,y_2,y_3)$. Squaring the last two coordinates preserves orientation off zero. The normal orientation induced by the quotient ambient orientation and the oriented base cover is therefore the OPPOSITE of $(\Re w,\Im w)$, giving Euler number $-4$ upstairs. The deck involution reverses both base and normal orientations, so downstairs this is the normal orientation local system. Lifting a transverse section counts each downstairs signed zero twice, proving $-4=2e(B)$ and $e(B)=-2$. Reversing the ambient orientation reverses the induced normal orientation and gives $+2$. This proves the smooth identification and its sign calibration. [step 3.1, step 4.1, construct]


6.1 Here are the asserted actual band and cap coordinates; they use the displayed embedding, not a classification by Euler number. Write $u=(\sqrt{1-s^2}\cos\theta,\sqrt{1-s^2}\sin\theta,s)$ and set $a=A_{33}=(3s^2-1)/\sqrt6$, $D=A_{11}-A_{22}+2iA_{12}$, $Z=A_{13}+iA_{23}$. Then $D=3(1-s^2)e^{2i\theta}/\sqrt6$ and $Z=3s\sqrt{1-s^2}e^{i\theta}/\sqrt6$, while $\frac12|D|^2+2|Z|^2=1-\frac32a^2$. Choose $0<\epsilon<1/4$ and cut at $a_c=(3\epsilon^2-1)/\sqrt6$. The level is an ordinary three-sphere in $(D/\sqrt2,\sqrt2Z)$. The region $|s|\le\epsilon$ projects radially onto that level, and is a collar graph over its image, with inward depth $a_c-a=3(\epsilon^2-s^2)/\sqrt6$. This lies in a genuine level collar, since every such $a$ is strictly above the pole value $-\sqrt{2/3}$. The ratio of the two coordinate magnitudes, with signed second amplitude, is $2s/\sqrt{1-s^2}$, strictly increasing. Reparameterizing by the second amplitude gives exactly the band in the Statement; its sole identification is $(\theta,s)\sim(\theta+\pi,-s)$. Its core is the round first-coordinate circle, and its transverse direction rotates by one half-turn when that core is traversed once. Its boundary is the $(2,1)$ curve on a product torus, an unknot: decreasing its first-coordinate amplitude to zero while increasing the positive second amplitude gives an explicit isotopy through embedded circles to the second-coordinate circle. For the cap use the representative $s\ge\epsilon$, put $v=\rho e^{i\theta}$, $s=\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$ and $0\le\rho\le\sqrt{1-\epsilon^2}$. It is a smooth disk, meeting each level $a=(2-3\rho^2)/\sqrt6$ once in such a circle. On that level multiply the first amplitude by $1-q$, $0\le q\le1$, and set the second positive amplitude to the square root of the remaining radius squared. These are embeddings of the whole disk for every $q$: away from the center the level coordinate has nonzero radial derivative, and the positive second amplitude makes the angular parametrization injective. Near the center the first amplitude is $O(\rho^2)$ and the second is $c\rho+O(\rho^3)$ with $c>0$, so the family is smooth and immersive there as well. At $q=1$ the disk is the standard plane section in this four-ball. This isotopy extends ambiently preserving its boundary three-sphere: in boundary-product charts its velocity has zero normal component on the boundary, so that component is the boundary coordinate times a smooth function; extend these coefficients in normal coordinates to the disk, patch by a finite partition and integrate the resulting boundary-tangent compactly supported field. Near the center use ordinary disk normal charts. The final plane disk is boundary-parallel, hence so is the original cap. This proves the actual band/cap assertions, without identifying any unrelated saddle embedding or any arbitrary projective plane. ∎ [F1, step 2.1, step 3.1, step 4.1, step 5.1, construct]
