## Mathematical findings relevant to a scaffold

### Bayesian formulation, priors, and function-space well-posedness

For separable Banach spaces `X,Y`, a measurable forward map `G:X→Y`, prior `μ0`, independent noise `η~Q0`, and translated observation law `Qu`, Dashti–Stuart §§3.1–3.2 formulate the posterior only after the likelihood ratio exists: `Qu << Q0` for `μ0`-almost every `u`, with `dQu/dQ0(y)=exp(-Φ(u;y))`; `Φ` must be jointly measurable and `Z(y)=∫exp(-Φ(u;y)) μ0(du)` finite and positive for data in the asserted almost-sure set. Then `dμy/dμ0=Z(y)^-1 exp(-Φ(u;y))`. Absolute continuity with respect to the prior implies prior-a.s. properties transfer to posterior-a.s. properties, but it is not a universal necessity: the notes explicitly mention Gaussian settings with posterior not absolutely continuous w.r.t. prior. Fixed-data pointwise statements require their own normalizer and version qualifications; the conditional-law theorem is initially only almost everywhere in data.

Prior series `u=m0+Σj γj ξj φj` are useful only after proving convergence in a named separable carrier space and identifying sample regularity. §§2.1–2.6 give distinct constructions: uniformly bounded positive series (sufficient `ℓ1` coefficient envelope and positive lower bound on `m0`), Besov/q-exponential series with a.s. `Xt,q` membership iff `t<s-d/q`, and Gaussian/Karhunen–Loève series with covariance eigenvalues behaving as `j^(-2s/d)` and a.s. Sobolev membership for `t<s-d/2`. Gaussian continuity needs further eigenfunction and summability assumptions; for `C=A^-s`, the stated assumptions imply Hölder regularity below `1 ∧ (s-d/2)`. A covariance/cylinder specification alone is not yet a Borel random element of a chosen function space; separability, tightness, and sample regularity matter.

The PDE examples retain their exact forward assumptions. For elliptic Darcy flow, `κ∈L∞` and `ess inf κ>0` give the weak solution by Lax–Milgram and `||p||V≤||f||V*/κmin`; the coefficient-to-solution map is locally Lipschitz only on sets with a common positive lower bound. Uniform priors are built to preserve such a bound a.s.; log-Gaussian `κ=exp(u)` is positive a.s. but has no uniform lower bound over prior draws. The notes derive all polynomial moments of `||p||V` for the log-Gaussian coefficient using Fernique, but specifically do not claim arbitrary exponential moments of that solution norm.

### Hellinger stability and discretization

Dashti–Stuart §4.1: let `Φ` be continuous on `X'×Y`; for data in a bounded `Y`-ball require a lower bound `Φ(u;y)≥-M1(r,||u||X)` and data-Lipschitz control `|Φ(u;y)-Φ(u;y')|≤M2(r,||u||X)||y-y'||Y`, with `M1,M2` nondecreasing and positive `M2`. Require `μ0(X')=1`, positive prior mass on some bounded subset of `X'`, and integrability `exp(M1)(1+M2²)∈L1(μ0)` (the weaker `exp(M1)` condition establishes propriety alone). These imply finite positive evidence and a locally Lipschitz posterior map `y↦μy` in Hellinger distance. Proof ingredients: the lower potential bound plus prior integrability gives finite `Z`; for positivity at fixed `y`, choose a point in `supp(μ0)∩X'` (this has full prior measure in a separable Banach space) and use joint continuity at `(u*,y)` to bound `Φ` on a spatial neighborhood; the support-point property gives that neighborhood positive prior mass, hence `Z(y)>0`. For the uniform lower evidence bound needed for all `y` in a bounded data ball (as used in Theorem 4.5), choose `u*` in the support, use continuity to bound `Φ(u;0)` on a bounded spatial neighborhood of positive prior mass, then use the stated data-Lipschitz inequality and monotonicity of `M2` to extend that bound uniformly over the bounded data ball. This gives a common positive lower bound for `Z(y)` there. Then apply exponential difference bounds to both normalizers and square-root densities, use these positive lower bounds, and combine terms in the Hellinger integral. Important source-proof qualification: Dashti–Stuart Theorem 4.3 as printed instead asserts that continuity makes `Φ` bounded above on `B'×{y}` for a bounded positive-mass set `B'`; continuity on a bounded noncompact subset does not imply this. The support-point argument supplies the needed lower bound under the stated separable-space continuity and support assumptions; record it as the explicit proof repair rather than repeating the printed inference. Hellinger control transfers posterior expectations only for functions with suitable second moments under both posteriors. It does not say the inverse solution itself is stable in a point metric.

§4.2 proves an analogous discretization statement. If potentials `Φ,ΦN` share a lower envelope `-M1(||u||)`, their uniform-in-N difference is at most `M2(||u||) ψ(N)` with `ψ(N)→0`, and `exp(M1)(1+M2²)` is prior integrable, then `dHell(μ,μN)≤C ψ(N)` for large N. This gives a route from forward/likelihood approximation error to posterior error, but only after proving the common lower bound, weighted integrability, and the stated potential error. No rate is automatic from mesh refinement.

The library defines root-L2 Hellinger and a different TV convention. Its published `thm-hellinger-tv-comparison-under-library-convention` gives `TV≤h≤sqrt(2 TV)` (verify exact direction/convention in carrier); do not copy numerical factors from another source without converting conventions. Existing functional BvM and credible-set suppliers are model-specific and do not imply general inverse-problem coverage.

### MAP, posterior consistency and credible-set caveats

For Gaussian prior `N(0,C)` on separable Banach `X`, the small-ball MAP functional is `I(u)=Φ(u)+||u||²_E/2` on Cameron–Martin space `E`, `+∞` outside. Under the notes’ lower quadratic control `Φ(u)≥M(ε)-ε||u||²_X`, local upper boundedness and local Lipschitz continuity, ratios of posterior small-ball probabilities converge to `exp(I(z2)-I(z1))` for `z1,z2∈E`. Existence/minimizing-subsequence compactness then additionally invokes compact embedding `E↪X` (for Gaussian measures, the unit ball embedding theorem). MAP is therefore a small-ball/Cameron–Martin statement, not “maximize an infinite-dimensional density” and not a posterior mean or uncertainty summary.

Consistency and contraction are treated in the notes as a research topic/referenced literature, not established by the generic Hellinger well-posedness theorem. Posterior stability under perturbing data is not asymptotic posterior concentration as information grows. Contraction requires a specified sequence of experiments, a truth, a metric, testing/separation and prior mass/sieve conditions. Credible mass is conditional probability under the chosen prior/model; nominal frequentist coverage needs extra calibration assumptions and may fail. The current library already has a direct counterexample `ex-posterior-credible-interval-need-not-have-nominal-frequency-coverage`, and published, source-specific functional BvM and adaptive credible-set pages. Use these as caveats and avoid an unqualified credible-coverage theorem.

### Function-space algorithms and limits

Cotter et al. §4 derive pCN by discretizing a prior-preserving OU dynamics. For target `dμ/dμ0 ∝ exp(-Φ)`, Gaussian `μ0=N(0,C)`, propose `v=sqrt(1-β²)u+βξ`, `ξ~μ0`, and accept with `min(1,exp(Φ(u)-Φ(v)))`. The proposal preserves/reverses `μ0`, so no infinite-dimensional Lebesgue density ratio is needed. Their Theorems 6.2–6.4 show in the specified Gaussian Hilbert setting that Crank–Nicolson midpoint discretization is the unique member of that one-parameter family with equivalent forward/reverse proposal flows, the unadjusted random walk proposal gives singular reversed flow (all proposals rejected in the function-space limit), and pCN/CN are defined with acceptance tending to 1 as step size tends to zero under their potential bounds. This dimension robustness is an algorithm/design property, not automatic rapid mixing, geometric ergodicity, posterior propriety, or correctness of a finite mesh forward model. pCN invariance still requires its exact MH construction; mixing/ESS are functional- and target-dependent. The paper’s application tables are numerical evidence, not universal efficiency guarantees.

Dashti–Stuart §5 also covers prior-reversible MH, independence sampling, pCN, SMC tempering and Gaussian-prior Langevin/SPDE invariance. SMC’s displayed finite-particle error theorem assumes globally bounded potential (both above and below), a fixed number J of tempering stages and its stated random-measure TV metric; authors note the resulting constant is poor and practical likelihoods often violate boundedness. Their infinite-dimensional Langevin invariance proof assumes Gaussian trace-class covariance and globally bounded derivatives/growth conditions on Φ; those are sufficient proof conditions, not necessary or a plug-in general guarantee.

### Transport and variational computation; current research

The 2026 preprint gives a finite-dimensional conditional-flow construction. With joint law `π(dx,dy)=πy(dx)πY(dy)`, independent reference `η`, and an invertible block-triangular transport `T(x,y)=(Ty(x),y)` pushing `η⊗πY` to `π`, disintegration gives `Ty#η=πy` only for `πY`-almost every y (or under their stronger pointwise conditions). It parameterizes `Ty` as a neural ODE. A vector field continuous in time and uniformly globally Lipschitz in state on the finite interval gives unique global solutions and a bi-Lipschitz flow with inverse from backward integration. C1-diffeomorphism and the classical Jacobian/divergence density formula require additional spatial differentiability (for example, a continuously differentiable state field with suitable bounded/continuous derivative); global Lipschitz continuity alone does not imply C1. The preprint states the stronger C1 conclusion from global Lipschitz alone without spelling out these extra hypotheses. Its particular smooth neural-network parameterization may supply them, but a reusable algorithm/theorem contract must state them. A merely Lipschitz field needs an a.e./Sobolev-flow change-of-variables framework instead. Conditional flow matching uses `Xt=(1-t)X0+tX1`, `X0~η` independent and `(X1,Y)~π`; the ideal field is `E[X1-X0 | Xt=x,Y=y]`, characterized as the L2 regression minimizer and satisfying the continuity equation under cited regularity assumptions. Training needs prior-predictive simulations/forward solves but no likelihood evaluation or ODE solve in the regression loop; sampling is parallel and amortized.

Their accuracy claims are finite-dimensional and diagnostic-dependent: if proposal flow density is tractable, independent MH is exact asymptotically (when the chain has the needed irreducibility/ergodicity, which is not automatic from the flow), while acceptance estimates TV brackets and importance weights estimate chi-square/KL bounds only with support and finite moment conditions. The article reports that posterior mean/credible-band pictures can look accurate while acceptance (and thus full-distribution agreement) is poor; small-noise/concentrated directions and observations in the tails of prior predictive law are failure modes. The level-set EIT example also demonstrates parameter-space posterior mismatch despite plausible field summaries. Its likelihood-free Lotka–Volterra experiment cannot use the likelihood-based MH correction or its TV/KL diagnostics. The authors explicitly say function-space flow-matching error theory, Wasserstein-based analysis, and matched total computational budgets remain open/future work. Treat all reported neural performance as empirical, not a posterior guarantee.

Transport-based variational approximation optimizes a discrepancy/objective within a restricted transport family; the objective’s global minimizer, optimization convergence, tractable Jacobian/density, support, and posterior fidelity are separate obligations. The library’s published VI scope covers ELBO/KL decomposition and a finite coordinate ascent algorithm, with a counterexample to mean-field variance calibration; it is not a general transport map or function-space flow theorem.
