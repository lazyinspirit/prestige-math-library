## What the article establishes

The model is the linear Gaussian white-noise experiment `Y(g)=<Tu†,g>+σZ(g)` for every `g` in a separable Hilbert space `Y`, with bounded linear `T:X→Y`, fixed truth `u†∈X`, and isonormal noise. The sample is a generalized random functional, not generally an `Y`-valued vector; the authors place it in a negative Hilbert scale. The tested feature is a bounded linear functional `φ∈X*`, with one-sided hypotheses `<φ,u†>≤0` versus `>0`. A Gaussian prior `N(m0,C0)` is used, with positive trace-class covariance and independent noise.

The posterior remains Gaussian. Its covariance is `C=σ² C0^(1/2)(C0^(1/2)T*T C0^(1/2)+σ²I)^−1 C0^(1/2)`; its mean is the corresponding affine Gaussian conditioning formula (equations (1.5), pp. 3). The MAP test compares the posterior mass of the two half-spaces. Since the posterior marginal of `<φ,U>` is a one-dimensional normal, posterior mass above zero exceeds one half exactly when `<φ,m> > 0`. Thus the test is the threshold rule `Y(Φ_MAP) > <m0,T*Φ_MAP−φ>`, where `Φ_MAP=T C0^(1/2)(C0^(1/2)T*T C0^(1/2)+σ²I)^−1 C0^(1/2)φ` (Theorem 2.3, pp. 4–5). This proof is a reduction to the Gaussian marginal CDF and substitution of the posterior mean; it does not require a finite-dimensional discretization.

If `T` is compact and `C0` commutes with `T*T`, the probe is a weighted Tikhonov solution to the adjoint feature equation: it minimizes `||T*Φ−φ||² + σ²||C0^(−1/2)V*Φ||²` on `ran(VC0^(1/2))`, with `T=V|T|` (Theorem 2.4, pp. 5–6). The argument jointly diagonalizes `T*T` and `C0`, computes the coefficient of each singular vector, and verifies the normal equation. This condition matters: the minimization characterization is not stated for noncommuting covariance and normal operator.

For fixed truth, exact rejection probability is normal-tail probability `Q((<u†,T*Φ>−<m0,T*Φ−φ>)/(σ||Φ||))` (Theorem 2.5, p. 6). Without a restricted truth class, level control is often impossible: if `C0` commutes with `T*T` and `φ` is not an eigenvector of `C0^(1/2)T*T C0^(1/2)`, the supremum rejection probability over `<φ,u†>≤0` is one (Theorem 2.6). The proof scales null vectors in the hyperplane `<φ,w>=0`; any nonzero pairing with `T*Φ` makes the numerator arbitrarily large. For injective compact `T`, joint diagonalization further shows eigenvectors here have only finitely many nonzero singular coordinates (Theorem 2.7), yielding the stated level-one corollary. A proper level-`α` calibration in the exceptional eigenfeature case requires a matching prior mean and gives equality at the boundary truth zero (Theorem 2.9). In that special case the MAP test equals the unregularized unbiased-feature test and both are uniformly most powerful (Theorem 2.10); this does not extend to arbitrary features.

The general regularized-test results quoted from the authors' 2024 paper require a Banach pair `(V,V')`, dual pairing bounded by the two norms, a truth in the unit ball of `V`, bounded `T*:Y→V'`, and `φ` in the `V'`-closure of `ran(T*)` (Assumption 1, pp. 10). For any nonzero probe `Φ`, critical value `σ||Φ||Q^(−1)(1−α)+||T*Φ−φ||_{V'}` gives level at most `α`; the error term pays for feature approximation over the truth ball (Theorem 2.12). Power is an explicit normal tail determined by the normalized objective `J(Φ)=(||T*Φ−φ||_{V'}−<Φ,Tu†>)/||Φ||`. The paper shows the optimal probe exists, lies in `ran(T)`, and can be approximated arbitrarily closely by MAP probes from a sequence of positive trace-class covariances, under its coordinate nonvanishing condition (Theorems 2.14–2.15). This covariance construction is oracle-dependent on `u†`; it is an approximation/attainability result, not an adaptive algorithm with known-truth-free optimality. The paper explicitly notes that the infimum need not be attained by a trace-class covariance (Remark 2.16).

For spectral-source analysis the assumptions are: `T` injective Hilbert–Schmidt; truth `u†=(T*T)^(ν/2)w` with `ν>0`, `||w||≤ρ`; covariance `C0=γ²(T*T)^μ` with `μ≥1`; and the prior mean set to the level-calibrating value (Assumption 2, p. 13). The prior is trace class under these assumptions. A source-space support caveat is proved: for `V=ran((T*T)^(ν/2))`, Gaussian prior mass of `V` is one if `m0∈V` and `(T*T)^(μ−ν)` is trace class, and zero otherwise (Theorem 3.1). In particular, merely choosing a covariance whose formal smoothness resembles the truth does not ensure positive prior mass on the source class.

Under `μ>ν/2−1`, the authors bound the source-weighted feature bias by `(σ/γ)^(ν/(μ+1))||φ||` and the probe norm by `(γ/σ)^(1/(μ+1))||φ||` (Theorem 3.2). Inserting these estimates into the exact power formula gives a nonasymptotic lower bound (Theorem 3.3, pp. 14–15). Consequences include power tending to one for fixed positive feature and `γ=γ0 σ^ω`, `−μ<ω<1` (Corollary 3.4), plus a separation scale `σ^(ν/(ν+1))` for a specified shrinking-feature regime and matched covariance scaling (Corollary 3.5). These are lower bounds for this calibrated MAP test under the stated source assumptions, not minimax lower bounds or universal minimax rates. The optimization over `γ` in Theorem 3.7 needs an expected feature magnitude `ξ`; choosing `ξ` equal to the true feature is an oracle choice. The article notes that the source-space worst case for calibration and for power need not coincide (Remark 3.6), explaining the residual factor in its bound.

An a-posteriori scale is chosen by minimizing an empirical objective over `γ0`, penalized by `ω(log γ0)²`, then setting `γ=γ0σ` (p. 18). Because the same data choose and evaluate the probe, the ordinary fixed-probe power formula does not directly describe the one-sample procedure; the authors distinguish same-sample and independent two-sample evaluation. Numerical experiments use three discretized operators: polynomially smoothing convolution, a second antiderivative, and exponentially smoothing backward heat. They compare compatible and incompatible features, 1-sample and 2-sample tuning, and report good empirical power/low empirical level in these examples (pp. 19–27). This is simulation evidence, not a theorem: the paper itself calls for theory of the one-sample procedure and reports tuning instabilities/outlier values, particularly for the severely ill-posed heat example.

## Complete lecture-note chapter: active network tomography

Earl Lawrence, George Michailidis, and Vijayan N. Nair, “Statistical inverse problems in active network tomography,” in *Complex Datasets and Inverse Problems: Tomography, Networks and Beyond*, IMS Lecture Notes–Monograph Series 54 (2007), 24–44, DOI 10.1214/074921707000000049. The complete author-posted arXiv v1 is https://arxiv.org/abs/0708.1079 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/0708.1079v1. Retrieved 18 October 2026. PDF: `.autopilot/scaffold-refresh-2026-10-18/sources/reader-2/0708.1079v1.pdf`; SHA-256 `6dc5dd8b9955c14a765a467631ab83d8159fff5cbde34afb6e3b2a132a685da5`. All 21 pages were extracted and read in bounded chunks, including the application, discussion, and references. The arXiv record and title page confirm authors, title, date, version, series, pages, and DOI. The text identifies itself as a review of active network tomography and also develops parametric delay inference; it is a monograph chapter, not general course notes.

The observation model is a known rooted tree with independent link-level random variables, observed only through path aggregates at chosen receiver sets. Identifiability is a property of both the link law and the probing design. In the introductory binary two-receiver example, separate unicast experiments reveal only products of link success probabilities; simultaneous multicast exposes their shared-link dependence and can identify all three parameters. Conversely, for additive Gaussian link delays the joint path law may identify only sums of means, even with multicast. These are concrete nonidentifiability examples (pp. 25–26), not generic claims that simultaneous measurements always identify a model.

For flexible mixtures of k-cast probing schemes on a tree, the reviewed result states that link-loss identifiability holds iff every receiver is covered and every internal node is a splitting node in at least one scheme. The chapter reports the same criterion for discrete delay distributions (pp. 28–29), citing prior papers; it gives the operational definition of a split and counterexample designs. Full proofs of those cited criteria are not reproduced, so they need independent full-text sourcing if used as formal suppliers. It explains the design tradeoff: bicast/unicast lowers measurement dimension and traffic but can be less statistically efficient than multicast, while multicast is costly and inflexible.

For link-loss likelihoods, the authors detail an EM procedure for bicast/unicast schemes: use current link success probabilities to calculate path probabilities and conditional expected latent link successes, then update each link probability by expected successes divided by probes traversing that link (pp. 29–31). They state the limitation: general k-cast E-steps become complex and EM is intractable for large networks. For discrete delay laws, the likelihood is multinomial over path-sum outcomes; the chapter reviews EM and a grafting strategy that fits subtree-local EMs and combines them by fixed-point iteration, improving cost with some efficiency loss. These are descriptions of methods/results published elsewhere, not newly proved convergence guarantees here.

For parametric delays, a zero-delay mixture model is identifiable when every link has positive atom at zero under the stated split/coverage conditions: conditioning on one path having zero delay and a sibling path positive isolates the sibling's delay distribution, then the shared component can be recovered. If zero atoms vanish, extra distributional structure is needed; the chapter demonstrates nonidentifiability for normal means and gives gamma examples where higher joint moments recover shared-link shape/scale combinations (pp. 34–35). It writes observed-data likelihoods for exponential and gamma delay families and explains why even small trees require numerical EM. The method-of-moments alternative minimizes squared discrepancies between empirical and model path moments, uses Gauss–Newton with step halving to ensure objective decrease, and can use GLS weights for efficiency. The chapter asserts consistency/asymptotic normality for a fixed network, then reports limited simulations and a network-simulator study. Those empirical examples have finite repetitions and show model-misspecification and dependence effects; they do not establish universal EM convergence, efficient inference, or robust confidence coverage.

The statistics scaffold can use this chapter as a concrete example of identifiability depending on experiment design, nonidentifiable additive path models, and latent-variable likelihood/moment procedures. Its assumptions of independent links and temporal homogeneity must remain visible; the simulator section shows sensitivity to spatial dependence and nonuniform delay laws. It does not provide general minimax rates, adaptation theory, or modern confidence coverage.

## Complete minimax detection article

Yuri I. Ingster, Theofanis Sapatinas, and Irina A. Suslina, “Minimax signal detection in ill-posed inverse problems,” *Annals of Statistics* 40(3) (2012), 1524–1549, DOI 10.1214/12-AOS1011. arXiv:1001.1853v4, updated 25 September 2012; https://arxiv.org/abs/1001.1853 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/1001.1853v4. Retrieved 18 October 2026. Local PDF `.autopilot/scaffold-refresh-2026-10-18/sources/reader-2/1001.1853v4.pdf`, SHA-256 `cdcfa8e49d867d9f270965afc763b7cd4e05afa60a45c431efbc550645fa24fa`. I read all 26 pages, including references. The companion detailed proof supplement is cited in the article as DOI 10.1214/12-AOS1011SUPP; I attempted Project Euclid's full/PDF paths, which returned anti-bot HTML, and did not obtain or read the supplement. Thus statements and proof architecture below are from the complete main article, but detailed proofs referred to only in the supplement remain an explicit gap.

The Gaussian white-noise experiment `dYε=Af dt+εdW` is reduced by SVD to `yk=bk θk+εξk`, or `xk=θk+εσkξk` with `σk=1/bk`. Mild, severe, and extreme ill-posedness mean respectively polynomial growth of `σk`, exponential growth, or `σk+1/σk→∞`. Alternatives constrain `θ` to an `ℓq` ellipsoid, `q∈(0,2]`, and remove an `ℓ2` ball of radius `rε`; this separation from zero avoids a trivial testing problem. The paper defines randomized tests, type-I and maximal type-II errors, Neyman–Pearson constrained risk and total error, and a separation rate by the transition from asymptotically impossible testing (`β→1−α`, total error→1) to consistent testing (both errors→0) (pp. 1–5).

For `q=2`, a variational problem minimizes `uε²=(2ε⁴)⁻¹Σηk⁴` over alternatives subject to ellipsoid and separation constraints. Its extremal sequence has `η̃k²=z0²σk²(1−A ak²)+`; Lagrange multipliers select an effective spectral dimension. The test is a centered weighted chi-square statistic with weights proportional to `η̃k²`. The article states the general theorem: `uε→0` makes testing impossible; `uε→∞` permits consistent testing; and when the largest normalized weight tends to zero, Gaussian sharp asymptotics hold with type-II error `Φ(H(α)−uε)+o(1)` and total error `2Φ(−uε/2)+o(1)`. The article's explanation obtains the lower bound through a symmetric product two-point prior and a chi-square likelihood-ratio distance; asymptotic normality of its log likelihood yields the Gaussian lower bound. Convexity/maximin arguments give the matching weighted-chi-square upper test. Full derivations are deferred to the unread supplement; do not represent them as locally source-verified proofs.

The paper gives separation rates for Sobolev axes `ak≈k^α` and analytic axes `ak≈exp(αk)`, under mild singular-value loss `σk≈k^β` and severe loss `σk≈exp(βk)`. For `q=2`, rates include `ε^(4α/(4α+4β+1))` in mildly ill-posed Sobolev; `ε(log ε⁻¹)^(β+1/4)` in mildly ill-posed analytic; `(log ε⁻¹/β)^(-α)` in severely ill-posed Sobolev; and `ε^(α/(α+β))` in severely ill-posed analytic. Sharp Gaussian asymptotics are asserted only when weights vanish; severe cases generally do not satisfy that condition, so one has distinguishability rates but not the same sharp normal limit. Sparse `q<2` has a separate extreme problem and boundary parameter `λ=(α+β)/2−β/q`; rates and tests differ on `λ≤0` versus `λ>0`. Do not reuse the `q=2` formula across sparse classes.

Adaptation is a combined-alternative problem over compact ranges of unknown smoothness and ill-posedness parameters. The authors define an adaptation payment `uε,ad`: for mild Sobolev `q=2`, it is order `sqrt(log log ε⁻¹)`; for severe Sobolev/analytic, `log log ε⁻¹`; and mild analytic classes have no diverging payment in the stated setting. Adaptive tests combine a grid of centered chi-square tests or coordinate thresholding; thresholds control the union error. This is a minimax detection source, distinct from estimation risk. Its adaptation proof also lies in the unavailable supplement; formal use requires consulting that companion text or another complete proof source.

## Complete adaptive regularization article

Housen Li and Frank Werner, “Adaptive minimax optimality in statistical inverse problems via SOLIT—Sharp Optimal Lepskiĭ-Inspired Tuning,” *Inverse Problems* 40(2) (2024), DOI 10.1088/1361-6420/ad12e0. arXiv:2304.10356v2, updated 11 December 2023; https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.10356 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.10356v2. Retrieved 18 October 2026. PDF `.autopilot/scaffold-refresh-2026-10-18/sources/reader-2/2304.10356v2.pdf`, SHA-256 `1d7b1bdfdf5ff31934016539cf8d5f244462a387acb5ed62896392b437a4cd18`. All 28 pages, including appendix and references, were extracted and read in bounded chunks.

The model is an injective Hilbert–Schmidt map between separable Hilbert spaces observed in Gaussian white noise. For an ordered nonnegative spectral filter `qα(T*T)T*Y`, assumptions include bounded filter factors `αqα(λ)≤C` and `λqα(λ)≤1`, monotonicity in α, source class `f=ϕ(T*T)g`, `||g||≤ρ`, convexity of `x↦xϕ⁻¹(√x)`, and a filter-qualification bias bound. Candidate α's are selected so estimator variance trace grows geometrically. SOLIT picks the smallest index whose pairwise estimator differences, over every finer candidate, stay below an explicit two-index noise quantile plus variance threshold. Inputs are observed data, known σ, T, and filter; no unknown truth or smoothness parameter is needed.

The main theorem gives a risk oracle inequality comparing SOLIT with a deterministic oracle that balances pairwise squared bias and variance. Its proof reduces to two conditions: ordered filters make the bias nonincreasing, and geometric variance growth makes a variance-ratio sum a geometric series. A second oracle comparison bounds that oracle by the candidate minimizing a source-bias plus variance envelope. For polynomially decaying eigenvalues `λk≈k^(−a)`, `a>1` (Hilbert–Schmidt), and source index `ϕν,τ(x)=x^ν(−log x)^(−τ)` (or `ν=0,τ>0`), SOLIT achieves the stated minimax MSE order uniformly over unknown smoothness, with no extra logarithmic adaptation loss. For severe `λk≈k^a exp(−b k^ϑ)`, the theorem requires `0<ϑ≤1/2`, and the polynomial-smoothness rate is slower than known-smoothness minimax by a logarithmic factor; the authors conjecture optimality in a range, not prove it. For `ϑ>1`, the required geometrically spaced variance candidates may not exist. The paper supplies proof arguments for its theorem in the main text/appendix, plus implementation of trace-based candidate selection and generalized chi-square quantiles. Numerical studies on antiderivative, satellite gradiometry, and heat inversion support performance; the heat case and spectral cutoff candidate construction show irregularity, and empirical plots are not theorem guarantees.

This is a modern source for adaptive regularization and model selection in estimation, not feature testing or confidence coverage. Use its algorithm only with its full filter, source, and noise assumptions, and preserve the severe ill-posedness caveats.

## Complete general-source minimax estimation note

LiTao Ding and Peter Mathé, “Minimax rates for statistical inverse problems under general source conditions,” arXiv:1707.01706v2 (15 November 2017), https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.01706 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.01706v2. Retrieved 18 October 2026. PDF `.autopilot/scaffold-refresh-2026-10-18/sources/reader-2/1707.01706v2.pdf`, SHA-256 `a07a84b74fde8099292498be139e96de92c155efa5576fc713733da7f4c5b767`. The 7-page complete note was extracted and read, including its proof, examples, and references. This is minimax estimation, not detection.

For compact `T` with singular values `sj` and Gaussian sequence model `zj=θj+σσjξj`, `σj=sj⁻¹`, assume truth is in ellipsoid `Σ aj² θj²≤Q²` with nondecreasing positive `aj`. The truncated SVD estimator through dimension `D` has exact worst-case squared risk `Q²/a_(D+1)² + σ² Σ_(j≤D) sj⁻²`, by bias–variance decomposition; both the tail-bias bound and its attaining one-coordinate truth are given. Their theorem proves minimax RMS error lies between the infimum of this cutoff tradeoff and that same quantity multiplied by 2.2. The lower-bound proof embeds hyperrectangles in the ellipsoid, uses product priors and one-dimensional minimax bounds to show arbitrary estimators cannot beat the best truncation estimator by more than a universal factor, then uses concavity of `J(r)=Σ min(rj,σ²sj⁻²)` to compare the best ellipsoid truncation risk with the worst hyperrectangle risk. General source sets `Aϕ={x=ϕ(T*T)v:||v||≤1}` become ellipsoids with axes `ϕ(sj²)`; examples classify interaction between polynomial/exponential singular decay and smoothness. The rate theorem is order-sharp up to constants, not a sharp-constant Pinsker theorem.
