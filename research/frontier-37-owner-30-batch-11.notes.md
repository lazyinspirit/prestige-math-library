## Proof dependency audit

The periodic chain is the core definition and kernel formula → square identity → `thm-marcel-riesz-conjugate-function-theorem` → bounded Riesz projection and partial sums → strict-range norm convergence. The theorem's endpoint proof computes the periodic transform of an arc indicator; Fejér approximants of that arc have sup norm at most one and converge in L², so the logarithmic singularity rules out a bounded polynomial-core L∞ extension. Skew pairing then rules out a bounded L¹ extension. The partial-sum theorem separately uses the published logarithmic Lebesgue constants, the exact L¹/L∞ convolution operator lower bounds, and the published **sequential** uniform boundedness theorem under Countable Choice. These are different endpoint obstructions.

On the line, the choice-free pointwise truncation definition precedes the signum multiplier computation; Hölder proves each truncation absolutely defined for every x when 1≤p<∞. The local sine-integral lemma derives the uniform oscillatory bound and π/2 value by integration by parts and a damped Fubini integral under Countable Choice. It avoids consuming published `lem-uniform-sine-integral-bound-and-dirichlet-value`, whose stated proof assumes full AC. The multiplier gives the L² isometry, square identity and skew adjointness, with no line zero-mode exception. The B interval computation is made from truncations and identified with the L² extension; positive smooth mollifications give common L¹/L² or L∞/L² tests for the two strong endpoint counterexamples. These do not refute weak (1,1) or BMO bounds.

For Riesz transforms, the L² multiplier definition precedes the principal-value kernel lemma. Grafakos Proposition 5.1.14's polar-coordinate Fourier calculation and Lemma 5.1.15's rotational sphere identity are the proof route. The kernel constant follows by evaluating `∫_{B_n}|x_1|` both in polar coordinates and by `(n−1)`-ball slices (with `n=1` separate). The finite Fourier multiplier identity then proves the L² square-sum result. Direct differentiation and oddness supply the size, first-difference and spherical-cancellation estimates before any later Calderón–Zygmund definition is invoked.
