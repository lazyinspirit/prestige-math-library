1. Introduction
1.1. What is a knot? I propose as a starting point a sort of slogan: a knot is a tangled
filament. Untangling this definition requires us to expand or gloss the terms (tangled,
filament) recursively into terms simple enough to test against our intuition. In place of
the term ‘tangled’ we might substitute ‘entwined’, ‘twisted’, ‘coiled’, ‘snarled’, ‘muddled’
etc. Some of these terms suggest the result of performing specific geometric operations
(twining, twisting, coiling) that implicitly take place in an ambient (three dimensional)
space, while others suggest an idea of topological complexity which might take the form
of an obstruction to an (as yet) undefined notion of elementary simplification. In place of
the term ‘filament’ we might substitute ‘fiber’, ‘thread’ or ‘strand’ — what is apparently
important here is the one dimensionality of a filament (especially when combined with the
three dimensionality of its ambient embedding).
Our slogan reveals (or, anyway, suggests) two important aspects of knothood:
(1) it involves the ways that one dimensional objects can be embedded in three dimen-
sional space; and
(2) it involves a calculus of local topological operations that create complexity or reduce
it.
A different starting point, arising from the intuition that knots are geometric objects of
some sort, would be to draw some pictures and then try to figure out what we have drawn:

A knot then is an equivalence class of embedded circles in 3-dimensional space, where
two circles are equivalent (i.e. represent the same knot) if they may be interpolated by
a ‘suitable’ continuous family of embedded circles. The embedded circles themselves are
representatives of the knot; our language in the sequel will not always make this distinction
precise, but we should make sure we always understand it is there.
Why do I say ‘suitable’ continuous family? Why don’t we define two embedded circles to
be equivalent (i.e. to define the same knot) if they may be interpolated by any continuous
family of embedded circles? Consider the following sequence of diagrams:
It is hard to dispute that this is a continuous family of embedded circles that interpolates
between the right handed trefoil (on the left) and the unknot (on the right).
All the
‘knottedness’ has been shrunk down to a point and disappeared. If transformations such
as these are permitted, one suspects that any two knots will turn out to be equivalent, and
knot theory will become trivial.
So: some kinds of continuous deformations are allowed, and some are not. To decide
which are which we must now (unavoidably, I think) become slightly more formal and
technical.
One loose end we should tie up first is the question of what exactly is the 3-dimensional
space in which our knots are embedded. One natural choice is to work in R3, ordinary 3-
dimensional Euclidean space. This will do for now, although at some point it will be more
natural to work in the compact space S3, which may be thought of as the set of unit vectors
in R4 or more abstractly as a space obtained from R3 by adding a single compactifying
point ‘at infinity’.
Now that we have decided where our knots are embedded we need to be more careful
about what exactly we mean by an embedding and what we mean by a continuous family.
In order to avoid certain pathologies it makes sense to restrict attention to knots of finite
combinatorial complexity (whatever that means). An example of the kind of pathology we
do not allow is this (figure adapted from an original by Alberto Verjovsky):

example of what is known as a wild knot. Wild knots and their theory are very interesting,

One way to do this is to restrict attention to smooth knots — i.e. knots with represen-
tatives that are the images of a smooth map f : S1 →R3 — i.e. the coordinate functions
are continuous with continuous derivatives of all orders — which is both a topological
embedding (i.e. injective) and an immersion (i.e. the vector f ′ is nowhere zero). And we
may now say that two smooth knot representatives are equivalent if they are the images of
smooth maps f0, f1 : S1 →R3 for which there is a smooth map F : S1 × [0, 1] →R3 for
which the factors ft := F(·, t) : S1 →R3 are all themselves smooth knot representatives.
This rules out the phenomenon observed in the figure above: although each knot in the
family is the image of a smooth map S1 →R3, the second derivatives of these maps must
necessarily blow up as the ‘knotted’ part shrinks to a point, and therefore the family of
embedded circles cannot be parameterized by a smooth S1 × [0, 1] →R3.
Example 1.1. The unknot is the equivalence class of smooth knots represented by some
(any) round circle in R3 (exercise: these are all equivalent).
Example 1.2. For p, q coprime integers the (p, q)-torus knot is the knot represented by the

Another way to achieve a similar result is to restrict attention to polygonal knots — i.e.
knots with representatives which are the union of a finite sequence of straight edges (the
meaning of ‘straight’ here implicitly uses the linear structure of R3). And we may now say
that two polygonal knot representatives are equivalent if one is obtained from the other by
a finite sequence of combinatorial moves of two kinds:
(1) subdivision of an edge (or its inverse); or
(2) pushing an edge across a disjoint triangle (or its inverse).
The meaning of the first (subdivision) move should be clear. The second move means: we
take an edge σ of a polygonal circle C which is simultaneously an edge of a triangle ∆that
is disjoint from C except along σ ⊂∂∆. We may then replace C with the new polygonal
circle C′ defined by C′ := (C −σ) ∪(∂∆−σ). I.e.:

1.2. Knot diagrams and Reidemeister’s Theorem. At this point we need to confront
a rather important issue: what exactly are these pictures we have been drawing up to now,
and what exactly is their relationship to circles (smooth, polygonal or otherwise) in R3?
An embedded polygonal circle C in R3 may be projected orthogonally to a plane R2.
Let π : R3 →R2 denote the projection. We consider projections that satisfy some nonde-
generacy conditions:
(1) distinct vertices of C map to distinct points of R2 (in particular, no edge of C is
perpendicular to R2);
(2) if e is an edge and v is a vertex of C disjoint from e then π(v) is disjoint from π(e);
and
(3) no point of C is in the image of 3 distinct points of C.

1.3. Knot invariants. We now have a workable definition: a knot is an equivalence class
of (polygonal or smooth) embedded circle in R3 up to the relation of (polygonal or smooth)
equivalence. Nondegenerate projection to a plane sets up a bijection between knots and
equivalence classes of knot diagrams up to the relation generated by planar isotopy and
Reidemeister moves.
Now that we know what knots are, and have at least two rather distinct ways of talking
about them, we may start to do knot theory. The most basic question one might ask is
simply: what knots are there? It is not clear at the outset what counts as an answer. The
class of all knots forms a (nonempty!) set, and we can ask how many elements there are
in this set. There are evidently only countably many equivalence classes of (polygonal)
knot diagrams, so there are countably many knots; but are there finitely many or infinitely
many? Are there more than one? Can we enumerate them? How would we get started
addressing such questions?
Since the definition of knots depends on a rather opaque equivalence relation we might
want to focus our initial attention there, and ask: given two knot representatives, how can
we tell whether they represent the same knot? Or: how can we tell whether they represent
different knots?
Let’s let K denote the set of all knots, and D the set of all knot diagrams. Let ∼be the
equivalence relation on D generated by planar isotopy and Reidemeister moves, so that
there is a bijection K →D/ ∼. A knot invariant is a function x : D →X which is constant
on the equivalence classes of ∼. It thereby induces a function which by abuse of notation
we denote x : K →X, and if we have knots K1, K2 with x(K1) ̸= x(K2) we can deduce
that K1 ̸= K2.
Let’s consider some examples.
Example 1.8 (Knot type). The simplest invariant is just the map D →K that takes a
diagram to its underlying knot type. This map inverts the bijection K →D/ ∼, a fact

one) it is essentially useless as a tool for calculation, since it just hands our knot back to
us with no more information.
Example 1.9 (Crossing number). Suppose we have a numerical function f on D which takes
values in a well-ordered set. Then we may define a knot invariant by D →minD′∼D f(D).
For example, there is an invariant c : D →N which counts the number of crossings
in a diagram. The crossing number of a knot K (denoted c(K)) is then the minimum
number of crossings in any diagram representing K. At first glance an invariant of this
kind might seem useless, since there is no apparent way to calculate it. One thing that is
easy to compute is an upper bound: c(K) ≤c(D) for any diagram D representing K. In
particular, c(K) = 0 for the unknot.
Example 1.10 (3-colorings). A segment of a diagram D is an arc of the diagram running
between two consecutive undercrossings. Other arcs of the diagram may cross under a
segment, but no arc may cross over it (by definition).
If you draw a diagram D, the
segments are the continuous strokes that may be made without lifting the pen (or chalk

Exercise 1.12. Show that τ3(K) is always a power of 3.
In general, what does τ3 tell us about a knot? We are not yet in a position to answer
this question in full generality. Nevertheless we can relate τ3 in a useful way to crossing
number c(D). If c(D) = 0 or 1 then there is only one segment, and evidently τ3(D) = 3.
Suppose c(D) > 0 so that c(D) is equal to the number of segments of D. Each segment
may be colored in at most 3 ways, so τ3(D) ≤3c(D). In fact it is not hard to see that if
c(D) ≥2 then τ3(D) ≤3c(D)−1. For, in this case there is at least one crossing at which
at least two different segments meet. Either exactly two segments meet there (in which
case both segments must necessarily have the same color) or three segments meet there
(in which case the color of two of them uniquely determines the third). In either case the
3-coloring condition imposes at least one ‘constraint’ on the choice of colors, so that the
number of 3-colorings is at most 3c(D)−1. Since this inequality holds for every diagram, and
since τ3 is a knot invariant, it follows that c(K) ≥1 + log3 τ3(K) providing τ3(K) > 3.
For the unknot this tells us nothing new, since we already know c(K) = 0 in this case;
and for the figure 8 knot it tells us nothing at all. But for the right handed trefoil it gives
the inequality c(K) ≥3 which, together with the inequality c(K) ≤3 (which may be
deduced by exhibiting the standard diagram) shows that in fact c(K) = 3.
Determining the crossing number of a knot in general is a difficult problem. The extent
to which we can make progress on this problem in the sequel will give us a rough estimate
of the power of our methods.
The crossing number of the unknot is 0. Are there any other knots with crossing number
0? It seems unlikely. But to prove it we must develop some machinery, and this is the
purpose of the next section.
2. Surfaces
2.1. The Jordan Curve Theorem. In fact there is only one knot with crossing number
zero — the unknot. This will follow from two facts. The first is a topological characteri-
zation of the unknot.
Proposition 2.1. An embedded circle in R3 is the unknot if and only if it bounds an
embedded disk.
Proof. The unknot is represented by a round circle C which certainly bounds an embedded
disk D. If C′ is any other representative then C and C′ are related by smooth equivalence;
we claim that this implies that C′ also bounds an embedded disk D′. Imagine that the

space R3 −C outside C is filled with a malleable rubber, which may stretch indefinitely
but does not tear or rip. As we move C around via the track of the smooth equivalence,
the space outside compresses to avoid the space that C moves into, and stretches to fill
the void it leaves. The disk D is also carried along by this process, and at the end of the
process it gives a new embedded disk D′ in R3 −C′ as claimed. This proves one direction
of the proposition: every representative of the unknot bounds an embedded disk.
To prove the other direction, suppose C is a smooth circle bounding a smooth embedded
disk D.
To say that D ‘is’ a disk is to say that there is a smooth parameterization
f : D2 →D where D2 is the ‘standard’ unit disk in R2, and where the Jacobian df of f
is injective on the tangent space at every point of D2 (this just means that D looks like a
flat plane to first order near every point). In particular, if α ⊂D2 is a very small round
circle centered at 0 then f(α) is close (to first order) to a very small ellipse contained in
a plane in R3. Such an ellipse is evidently equivalent to the unknot (by a family of affine
transformations). On the other hand, α and ∂D2 may be interpolated by a 1-parameter
family of concentric round circles in D2, and the images of these circles under f is a smooth
equivalence between C and f(α). Hence C represents the unknot, as claimed.

2.2. The Classification of Surfaces. A surface is a space that looks locally 2-dimensional;
i.e. every point has a little open neighborhood which is topologically equivalent to the open
unit disk in the plane. One usually throws in some technical terms like ‘countable basis’
and ‘Hausdorff’ (if you know what those words mean); but we will soon narrow our focus
so that these conditions hold automatically.
We shall be interested in surfaces that are compact and triangulated. In other words,

special kind of two-dimensional simplicial complex, for which every point is in the interior
of a unique triangle, edge or vertex.

χ(P) := #triangles −#edges + #vertices

Let us now state our first version of the Classification of Surfaces:
Theorem 2.8 (Classification of Closed Oriented Surfaces). Let S be a closed oriented
connected surface. Then χ(S) = 2 −2g for some non-negative integer g called the genus of
S. This may take any non-negative value, and any two closed oriented connected surfaces
with the same genus are topologically equivalent.
Example 2.9 (The 2-sphere). The 2-sphere S2 may be triangulated by subdividing the
equator into three segments, and then thinking of both the northern and the southern
hemispheres as triangles for which these three segments are the boundary edges. Thus
χ(S2) = 2 −3 + 3 = 2.
Example 2.10 (The torus). The torus T may be obtained from a square by identifying
opposite sides by translation. As such it admits an obvious triangulation (inherited from
a triangulation of a square) with two triangles, three edges and one vertex. Thus χ(T) =
2 −3 + 1 = 0.

Definition 3.1 (Seifert surface). If K is a knot, the genus of K (denoted g(K)) is the

disk reduces the Euler characteristic by 1, and we attach c(D) disks, one for each crossing.
It follows that χ(Σ) ≥1 −c(D). Since Σ has one boundary component, χ(Σ) = 1 −2g(Σ)
and therefore g(Σ) ≤c(D)/2 and g(K) ≤c(K)/2.
In our example n = 4 and c(D) = 7 so χ(Σ) = −3 and g(Σ) = 2. In fact, this is the
genus of the knot 76, though we do not yet have the tools to prove it.
3.3. Linking number. A link is an equivalence class of smooth (or polygonal) disjoint
embedded circles in R2 up to smooth (or polygonal) equivalence. A knot is a special case of
a link with one component. For each n the n-component unlink is represented by n disjoint
unknots, each unknot embedded in a separate 3-ball disjoint from the others.
Links, just like knots, are represented by diagrams, and two diagrams represent the same
link if they are related by Reidemeister moves. A link is oriented if each of its components
is separately oriented; thus a link with n components admits 2n distinct orientations.
Definition 3.11 (Linking number). Let L be an oriented link with 2 components K1 and
K2. The linking number of the two components, denoted ℓ(K1, K2), may be computed
from any diagram by counting the number of positive overcrossings of K1 over K2 minus
the number of negative overcrossings of K1 over K2:
Proposition 3.12. Linking number is an invariant; i.e. it does not depend on the choice
of diagram representing a link. Furthermore it is symmetric: ℓ(K1, K2) = ℓ(K2, K1) and
changes sign if the orientation of one of the components of a link is reversed.
Proof. Changing the orientation of one of the components changes a positive overcrossing
into a negative overcrossing and vice versa, proving the third claim.
To show that linking number is an invariant, we must just check that it does not change
under a Reidemeister move.
Reidemeister 1 creates (or removes) a crossing, but this
crossing necessarily involves a single component, and can’t contribute to linking number.
Thus we just need to check invariant under Reidemeister 2 and Reidemeister 3, which is
straightforward and left as an exercise.
Once we know that linking number is an invariant, consider what happens to a diagram
when we take a link and ‘rotate it’ through 180 degrees about the horizontal axis contained
in the plane of the projection. The effect is to ‘reflect’ the diagram in this axis and at the
same time to change overcrossings to undercrossings and vice versa.
Thus, under this
transformation, each positive crossing of K1 over K2 is changed into a positive crossing of
K2 over K1 and vice versa. The identity ℓ(K1, K2) = ℓ(K2, K1) follows.

we establish the following three facts:
(1) we shall associate to any oriented embedded circle C in R3 the free Abelian group
of additive invariants which consists of functions from oriented circles in R3 −C to
Z, and which satisfies a certain additivity axiom (to be described below);
(2) we shall show that linking number with C, and signed intersection number with Σ
are both additive invariants; and
(3) we shall show that as an abstract group, the group of additive invariants is isomor-
phic to Z, generated by the function that takes the value 1 on a particular knot (an
oriented meridian for C).

with Σ are both equal to 1 for a meridian, and therefore they are equal for any embedded
oriented circle C′ in R3 −C.

Lemma 3.15. Signed intersection number with Σ is an additive invariant.

two oriented circles in R3−C that intersect Σ transversely. To say that C′ and C′′ represent
the same knot type in R3 −C means that there is a smooth map g : S1 × [0, 1] →R3 −C

map g so that it intersects Σ transversely; in other words, we may arrange that g−1(Σ) is a
system X of embedded circles and properly embedded arcs in S1 × [0, 1]. The orientations
on Σ and on S1 × [0, 1] determine orientations on the components of X; there are several
ways to do this, and we leave it as an exercise to the reader.
As the oriented boundary of the oriented surface S1 × [0, 1] the orientations on S1 × 0
and on S1 × 1 respectively disagree and agree with the orientations on C′ and C′′ induced
by g. It follows that the signed intersection number of C′′ with Σ is the signed intersection
number of X with S1 × 1, whereas the signed intersection number of C′ with Σ is the
negative of the signed intersection number of X with S1 ×0. Thus to prove that the signed
intersection numbers with Σ are the same for C′ and C′′ it suffices to show that the signed
intersection number of X with ∂(S1 × [0, 1]) is zero. But the circle components of X do
not intersect ∂(S1 × [0, 1]) at all, whereas each proper arc intersects it twice, with opposite
sign. The claim follows.

4. The knot group
4.1. A return to coloring. Recall that we defined a 3-coloring of a diagram D to be an
assignment of one of three colors to each of the segments of D so that at each crossing, the
colors assigned to the three segments that meet at the crossing are either all the same or
all different.
If we are drawing an actual diagram on paper, then we know what we mean by using
different colors to draw the segments of the diagram.
But if we want to abstract the
mathematical essence of what a coloring ‘is’, then it is easy to see that it is nothing more
than a map of sets: a map from the set of segments to some fixed 3 element set, and
subject to certain local conditions. As Tolstoy might say, all 3 element sets are alike —
they are all interchangable qua sets — but if we consider 3 element sets endowed with extra
structure then it becomes possible to express the coloring conditions (all colors the same
or all colors different) in more meaningful terms.
Let’s take the three ‘colors’ to be elements of the Abelian group Z/3Z of integers modulo
3; in other words, each segment ei of the diagram D gets assigned an element xi ∈Z/3Z.
Then the coloring condition at each crossing (involving segments ei, ej, ek) becomes the
algebraic condition xi + xj + xk = 0 mod 3.
Since Z/3Z is a field, and the coloring
conditions are linear, the set of 3-colorings gets the structure of a (finite dimensional)
vector space over Z/3Z. In particular, the cardinality of this set is a power of 3.
Once we frame things in this way we can start to fiddle with some of the parameters
and see what happens. Let’s try to replace the field Z/3Z with another field Z/pZ for a
different prime p. Suppose e is a segment in a diagram D colored by some x ∈Z/pZ. If

that of D outside the region in which we have performed the move, the three segments
meeting at the new crossing must all have the same coloring, and therefore we get the
equation 3x = 0. Since x was arbitrary this implies that x = 0 unless p = 3. Our attempt
to generalize 3-colorings to p-colorings has fallen at the first hurdle.
Let’s take a step back. We changed the parameter 3 to the parameter p but we kept
the same algebraic equation xi + xj + xk = 0. Are we free to change the equation? The
equation xi + xj + xk = 0 is symmetric in the three variables corresponding to the three
segments meeting at a crossing. But at a crossing the diagram is not symmetric in the
three segments: one segment (e.g. ei) crosses over, while the other two (ej, ek) may be
interchanged by a (local) 180 degree rotation. Thus we are compelled to look for equations
which are symmetric in xj and xk but not necessarily in all 3 variables. Arguing as above,
the only linear equation of this form which is compatible with Reidemeister 1 (up to
multiplication by a scalar) is xj + xk = 2xi.

With the aid of some group theory and commutative algebra we can recast the discussion

The group H is 2-step solvable; this means that it admits a surjective homomorphism
to an Abelian group for which the kernel is also Abelian. The surjective homomorphism
H →C∗takes a matrix to its upper left entry; and the kernel is the subgroup H1, which
is isomorphic to the additive group C.
The knot group G is typically not 2-step solvable (in fact, it is rarely solvable at all).
But any homomorphism from G to any 2-step solvable group factors through a maximal
2-step solvable quotient of G. Thus the information encoded in the Alexander polynomial
may be obtained directly by an analysis of the structure of this 2-step solvable quotient.
We have already seen that there is a maximal Abelian quotient of G, namely Gab = Z.
The kernel is the commutator subgroup, usually denoted G′. Now, G′ is typically not
Abelian, but it admits a map to its own abelianization (G′)ab with kernel (G′)′ which is
usually denoted G(2). Although in general a normal subgroup of a normal subgroup is not
necessarily normal in the bigger group, the subgroup G(2) is normal in G. We may therefore
form the 2-step solvable quotient G/G(2) which maps to Z with kernel the Abelian group
(G′)ab.
Let’s write the Abelian group (G′)ab as A. The generator 1 of Z is in the image of some
g ∈G/G(2), and conjugation by g induces an automorphism t : A →A. We may therefore
think of A as a Z[t, t−1]-module.
It turns out that A is finitely generated as a Z[t, t−1]-module, and that for any presenta-
tion, there are always at least as many relations s as generators r. The ideal generated by
the r × r minors of the presentation matrix is independent of choices, and called the fitting
ideal. Alexander [1] showed that for any knot K there is always a square presentation
matrix, so that the fitting ideal is principal, and a generator for this ideal (normalized by
multiplication by a suitable unit t±n so that it has no terms with negative exponent, and
has a nonzero constant term) is the Alexander polynomial ∆K(t).

5.1. Kauffman bracket. Up till now we have made extensive use of a diagrammatic

moves on a diagram.

that skein relations could give rise to more than one polynomial invariant, the HOMFLY

In the 40 years since the discovery of the Jones polynomial, knot theory has developed
into a remarkably deep and sophisticated subject, with significant applications to theoret-
ical physics, the biology of DNA, and many other fields.
Mathematically speaking, the theory of irreducible representations of the knot group
into SL(2, C) turns out to be intimately connected to hyperbolic geometry, and the ge-
ometrization conjecture of Thurston (proved as it applies to knots and links by Thurston,
and in full generality by Perelman).
Polynomial invariants such as HOMFLY are subsumed under the auspices of Khovanov
Homology (which sheds light on exotic smooth structures on 4-manifolds) and Heegaard
Floer Homology (which has its foundations in symplectic topology and Gromov’s theory
of pseudo-holomorphic curves, and which is by now connected to many different aspects of
3 and 4-manifold topology).
