## Statement

Work in ZF. Let $K$ be a number field, $R=\mathcal O_K$, $p$ a rational prime, and $P$ a nonzero prime of $R$ above $p$. Put $S=R_P$, write $p=u\pi^e$ in this discrete valuation ring, and set $f=[R/P:\mathbb F_p]$. Define $\widehat S=\varprojlim_m S/\pi^mS$ and $K_P=\operatorname{Frac}(\widehat S)$. Then $\widehat S$ is a complete discrete valuation ring with uniformizer $\pi$, residue field $R/P$, and dense subring $S$; $K_P$ is the completion of $K$ for $\operatorname{ord}_P$, and is a finite extension of $\mathbb Q_p$ of degree $ef$. Its valuation satisfies
$$\operatorname{ord}_p N_{K_P/\mathbb Q_p}(x)=f\operatorname{ord}_P(x),\qquad |N_{K_P/\mathbb Q_p}(x)|_p^{1/(ef)}=p^{-\operatorname{ord}_P(x)/e}\quad(x\ne0).$$
There are canonical algebra isomorphisms
$$R\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Z_p\cong\prod_{P\mid p}\widehat{R_P},\qquad K\otimes_{\mathbb Q}\mathbb Q_p\cong\prod_{P\mid p}K_P.$$
For a finite extension $L/K$ of number fields and any $P$, the inclusions induce continuous embeddings $K_P\hookrightarrow L_Q$ for $Q\cap R=P$, and a canonical isomorphism
$$L\otimes_KK_P\cong\prod_{Q\mid P}L_Q,\qquad \sum_{Q\mid P}[L_Q:K_P]=[L:K].$$
For $x\in L$, the image of $N_{L/K}(x)$ in $K_P$ is $\prod_{Q\mid P}N_{L_Q/K_P}(x)$, and the corresponding trace is the sum of local traces. The same relative tensor, dimension, norm and trace formulas hold at every archimedean place $v$, with $K_v=\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$ and the product indexed by the archimedean places of $L$ above $v$. In the complex case, orient each embedding of $L$ so that its restriction is the chosen embedding representing $v$. In particular, for a quadratic extension $L/K$, its archimedean local algebra is $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$ over $\mathbb R$, and $\mathbb C\times\mathbb C$ over $\mathbb C$. The nontrivial quadratic automorphism swaps factors in the split cases and acts by complex conjugation on $\mathbb C/\mathbb R$. The respective norm maps on these algebras are $(a,b)\mapsto ab$, $z\mapsto z\overline z$, and $(a,b)\mapsto ab$. Thus the diagonal global norm equals the product of local norms at every finite or archimedean place. These constructions require neither an extension theorem for arbitrary valuations nor a general norm-equivalence theorem.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The fields, primes and rings in the statement; $\operatorname{ord}_P(\pi)=1$.

[F1] The proof of [[thm-number-field-integral-ideal-factorisation-in-zf]] proves that every $R_P$ is a DVR, every nonzero integral ideal has unique finite prime factorisation, $K=\operatorname{Frac}(R)$, and every ideal and local ring admits least-natural-number codes after fixing an integral basis.

[F2] [[thm-ring-of-integers-free-of-rank-degree]] gives one integral basis of $R$, of size $[K:\mathbb Q]$; [[lem-nonzero-number-field-ideal-has-finite-quotient]] gives finite nonzero ideal quotients.

[F3] [[thm-chinese-remainder-theorem-for-comaximal-ideals]] gives the finite CRT isomorphism, with product equal to intersection for pairwise comaximal ideals.

[F4] [[thm-p-adic-completion-agrees-with-the-fraction-field-of-zp]] identifies $\mathbb Z_p=\varprojlim_m\mathbb Z/p^m\mathbb Z$ and $\mathbb Q_p=\mathbb Z_p[1/p]$.

[F5] Norm and trace are determinant and trace of multiplication on a finite basis, by [[thm-field-norm-and-trace-agree-with-operator-determinant-and-trace]].



[F6] [[thm-primitive-element-theorem-for-finite-separable-extensions]] gives a primitive element for the finite characteristic-zero extension $L/K$. [[thm-fundamental-theorem-of-algebra-minimum-modulus-proof]] gives a root of every nonconstant complex polynomial; successive division by linear factors splits any one such polynomial after finitely many steps.



## Proof

1.1 Fix one integral basis and its explicit natural-number code for $R$, and code fractions and finite tuples by natural numbers. Every nonempty subset of $K$ or $S$ then has a least-coded member. In particular every residue class in any finite quotient below has a specified representative, without a sequence of arbitrary choices. The finite domain $R/P$ is a field, and the kernel of $\mathbb Z\to R/P$ is generated by a rational prime $p$; its dimension $f$ over $\mathbb F_p$ is finite. The DVR result gives $p=u\pi^e$ with $e>0$, and $S/\pi S=R/P$. [F1, F2, given]

2.1 For each $m\ge1$, localization gives an isomorphism $R/P^m\to S/\pi^mS$. Here is the detail. If $s\notin P$, then $(s)+P=R$; expanding a Bezout equation to the $m$th power gives $(s)+P^m=R$, so $s$ is invertible modulo $P^m$. Thus the map is surjective. If $a\in R$ maps to zero, then $ta\in P^m$ for some $t\notin P$. For $a\ne0$, factor the principal ideals $(t)$ and $(a)$: the exponent of $P$ in $(t)$ is zero, and that of $(a)$ must be at least $m$. Thus $a\in P^m$. For the last assertion, division of the factorisation of $(a)$ by $P^m$ leaves an integral ideal, so $(a)\subseteq P^m$. The case $a=0$ is immediate. This also shows $R\cap\pi^mS=P^m$. [F1, F3, step 1.1, algebra]

2.2 We give the complete-ring construction explicitly. For $A=\varprojlim_m S/\pi^mS$, addition and multiplication are coordinatewise and $S\to A$ is injective because a nonzero element of a DVR has finite valuation. The kernel of $A\to S/\pi^mS$ is exactly $\pi^m A$: if $x$ is in that kernel, use its component modulo $\pi^{m+r}$, divide by $\pi^m$, and reduce modulo $\pi^r$ to define a compatible element $y$ with $x=\pi^m y$. The quotient is independent of the lift because cancellation in $S$ identifies $\pi^mS/\pi^{m+r}S$ with $S/\pi^rS$. Multiplication by $\pi$ on $A$ is injective by the same calculation one coordinate higher. A nonzero $x\in A$ therefore has a unique finite order $a\ge0$ and can be written $x=\pi^a v$ with nonzero residue $v\bmod\pi$. Each component of $v$ is a unit in $S/\pi^mS$, so their unique inverses form $v^{-1}\in A$. Products have additive orders; hence $A$ is a domain and its nonzero ideals are generated by an element of least order. Thus $A$ is a DVR with uniformizer $\pi$ and residue field $S/\pi S$. [F1, step 1.1, algebra]

3.1 A Cauchy sequence in $A$ has an eventually constant residue in each $S/\pi^mS$; these uniquely determined residues are compatible and define its limit. Hence $A$ is complete. A least-coded lift in $S$ of the $m$th coordinate of $x\in A$ approximates it modulo $\pi^m$, proving density by a specified sequence. In the field $E=A[1/\pi]$, every element is $\pi^{-a}x$ with $x\in A$; a Cauchy sequence has valuations bounded below, since a Cauchy tail differs from its first term by integral elements. Multiplying it by one fixed power of $\pi$ reduces completeness to that of $A$. The density of $S$ gives density of $K=\operatorname{Frac}(S)$ in $E$. Every Cauchy sequence in $K$ converges in $E$, and equivalent sequences have the same limit; conversely the specified approximating sequences just constructed represent every element. This identifies $E$ with the Cauchy completion of $K$ and proves all these assertions in ZF. [F1, step 2.2, algebra]

4.1 Put $d=ef$. The $e$ successive layers of $A/pA=A/\pi^eA$ are each additively isomorphic to $S/\pi S$ by multiplication by $\pi^j$ and cancellation. It is an $\mathbb F_p$-vector space of cardinality $p^{ef}$, hence dimension $d$; the cardinality assertion follows by counting the fibers of each quotient map in the finite filtration. Select a finite $\mathbb F_p$-basis and lift it to $b_1,\ldots,b_d\in S$, using the least-coded eligible tuple. Given $x\in A$, there is a unique digit tuple $c_{i,0}\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}$ such that $x-\sum_i b_i c_{i,0}\in pA$. Divide by $p$ and repeat this uniquely specified operation. Completeness gives $$x=\sum_{i=1}^d b_i z_i,\qquad z_i=\sum_{r\ge0}c_{i,r}p^r\in\mathbb Z_p.$$ If such a sum vanishes, reduction modulo $p$ forces all $z_i$ into $p\mathbb Z_p$; division and repetition force each into every $p^r\mathbb Z_p$, hence to zero. Thus $A$ is free over $\mathbb Z_p$ on these $d$ elements. The scalar map is the continuous extension of $\mathbb Z\to A$ and is injective because the order of a nonzero $p$-adic scalar is $e$ times its $p$-adic order. Finally $A[1/p]=A[1/\pi]$, since $p=u\pi^e$, proving $[E:\mathbb Q_p]=ef$. The same argument identifies the $p^rA$ neighborhoods with $p^r\mathbb Z_p^d$, so the coordinate and valuation topologies agree. [F4, step 2.2, step 3.1, algebra]

5.1 For completeness we verify the norm formula without a valuation-extension theorem. Multiplication by a unit of $A$ is an invertible integral $\mathbb Z_p$-matrix, so its determinant is a unit of $\mathbb Z_p$. Multiplication by $\pi$ has cokernel $A/\pi A$ of size $p^f$. For any full-rank integral square matrix $M$ over $\mathbb Z_p$, elementary row and column operations diagonalize it: choose an entry of least $p$-adic order in the remaining finite submatrix, move it to its first position, and subtract multiples to kill its row and column; it divides every entry in that submatrix. Induction gives nonzero diagonal entries, and their quotient cardinalities are $p^{\sum_i\operatorname{ord}_p d_i}=p^{\operatorname{ord}_p\det M}$. The operations have unit determinants and preserve the cokernel. Applied to multiplication by $\pi$ this gives $\operatorname{ord}_p N(\pi)=f$. Every nonzero element of $E$ is a unit times an integral power of $\pi$, including negative powers, so multiplicativity of determinants proves $\operatorname{ord}_p N(x)=f\operatorname{ord}_P(x)$. The displayed absolute-value formula follows. In particular $p^{-\operatorname{ord}_P(x)/e}$ is a complete nonarchimedean absolute value extending the usual one on $\mathbb Q_p$. [F5, step 2.2, step 4.1, algebra]

5.2 Factor $pR=\prod_{P\mid p}P^{e_P}$ by [F1]. The finite factors are pairwise comaximal, as are all their positive powers: for $I+J=R$, expand $1=(a+b)^{r+s-1}$ with $a\in I$, $b\in J$ to see $I^r+J^s=R$. Hence [F3] gives compatible isomorphisms $$R/p^mR\cong\prod_{P\mid p}R/P^{me_P}\cong\prod_{P\mid p}R_P/\pi_P^{me_P}R_P.$$ Taking inverse limits commutes with this finite product simply by regrouping coordinates. The multiples $me_P$ are cofinal among positive integers, so each factor limit is $\widehat{R_P}$. On the other hand the fixed integral basis identifies $\varprojlim_m R/p^mR$ with $\mathbb Z_p^{[K:\mathbb Q]}$, and identifies the same module with $R\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Z_p$. These identifications preserve multiplication: the integral basis has finitely many integral structure constants, and multiplication reduces them coordinatewise. This proves the ring isomorphism. Inverting $p$ gives the claimed $K\otimes\mathbb Q_p$ isomorphism, because $R[1/p]\otimes_{\mathbb Z[1/p]}\mathbb Q_p=K\otimes_{\mathbb Q}\mathbb Q_p$ on the rational integral basis and every local $\widehat{R_P}[1/p]=K_P$. [F1, F2, F3, F4, step 2.1, step 4.1, algebra]

5.3 We detail the relative compatibility of these isomorphisms. Let $T=\mathcal O_L$ and let $Q$ be a prime of $T$ above the rational prime $p$. Its contraction to $R$ is a nonzero prime $P$, since it contains $p$. For $a/s\in R_P$, $s\notin P$ implies $s\notin Q$, so $R_P\hookrightarrow T_Q$; units map to units. The chosen $\pi_P$ has positive finite $Q$-order $h$: write $\pi_P=a/s$, where $a\in P$, $s\notin P$. Thus $$\operatorname{ord}_Q(x)=h\operatorname{ord}_P(x)\quad(x\in K^\times).$$ The inclusion extends to $K_P\hookrightarrow L_Q$ by taking limits of the specified $K$-approximations; it preserves this scaled valuation and is therefore injective. This proof uses only completeness already established. [step 3.1, step 4.1, algebra]

6.1 Apply step 5.2 to $K$ and $L$. They identify $B=K\otimes_{\mathbb Q}\mathbb Q_p$ with $\prod_{P\mid p}K_P$ and $C=L\otimes_{\mathbb Q}\mathbb Q_p$ with $\prod_{Q\mid p}L_Q$. Under the natural map $B\to C$, the $Q$ coordinate of an element is its $P=Q\cap R$ coordinate embedded as in step 5.3. To check this beyond the dense original fields, first work in $R\otimes\mathbb Z_p$: every element is a limit of elements of $R$ by truncating its finitely many $\mathbb Z_p$ coordinates. The $Q$-coordinate map is continuous, as is the embedding from the $P$ coordinate, so they agree on that entire integral tensor product, then after inverting $p$ on $B$. Thus its idempotent $\epsilon_P$, with one coordinate $1$ and the others $0$, acts on $C$ by keeping exactly the $Q$ factors over $P$. [F2, step 5.2, step 5.3, algebra]

7.1 Tensor associativity gives $C=L\otimes_K B$. More explicitly, $(\ell\otimes k)\otimes z\mapsto\ell k\otimes z$ and $\ell\otimes z\mapsto\ell\otimes(1\otimes z)$ are inverse maps. Applying the idempotent $\epsilon_P$ gives $$L\otimes_K K_P=\epsilon_P C\cong\prod_{Q\mid P}L_Q.$$ The left side is a $K_P$-space of dimension $[L:K]$, using any one finite $K$-basis of $L$. Consequently the product is nonempty and its factor dimensions are finite and sum to $[L:K]$. This also proves existence of a prime above every $P$ without selecting a maximal ideal of an arbitrary ring. [step 6.1, algebra]

8.1 For $x\in L$, form its multiplication matrix in a finite $K$-basis of $L$. Extending scalars to $K_P$ leaves its determinant and trace equal to the images of the global norm and trace. Under step 7.1 multiplication is the direct sum of multiplication by $x$ on the finitely many $L_Q$ factors. A basis formed by concatenating finite bases of these factors makes the matrix block diagonal. Its determinant is the product of the block determinants and its trace is the sum of block traces. This proves the claimed formulas, also for $x=0$. All bases, prime lists and residue representatives used above were single finite witnesses or least-coded choices in countable sets; all infinite recursions were uniquely specified residue or digit operations. No form of countable or dependent choice was used. [F5, step 7.1, algebra]

9.1 Having established the finite-place case in step 8.1, let $v$ now be archimedean, represented by an embedding $\sigma:K\to F$, where $F=\mathbb R$ if $\sigma$ is real and $F=\mathbb C$ if it is nonreal. By [F6] write $L=K(\alpha)$, and let $G(T)\in K[T]$ be the monic minimal polynomial of $\alpha$, of degree $d=[L:K]$. The power basis gives $L\otimes_{K,\sigma}F\cong F[T]/(\sigma G)$. Since the characteristic is zero, $G$ and $G'$ have gcd $1$; a Bezout identity remains such an identity under $\sigma$, so $\sigma G$ has no repeated root. Over $\mathbb C$, [F6] factors it into $d$ distinct linear factors. Over $\mathbb R$, its finitely many nonreal roots come in conjugate pairs, so grouping each pair gives a monic irreducible real quadratic and leaves a finite list of real linear factors. The quadratics have no real root, and division over $\mathbb R$ shows that they are irreducible. All these factors are pairwise comaximal. [F3, F6, step 8.1, algebra]

10.1 CRT therefore identifies $L\otimes_K F$ with a product of copies of $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb C$ when $F=\mathbb R$, and with $\mathbb C^d$ when $F=\mathbb C$. For a nonreal root $z$ of a real quadratic, evaluation identifies its quadratic quotient with $\mathbb C$: it is injective by irreducibility and surjective because $1,z$ span $\mathbb C$ over $\mathbb R$. Evaluation at each root also defines an embedding of $L=K[T]/(G)$ extending $\sigma$, since a unital map from the field $L$ into $\mathbb C$ is injective. These are all extensions, since any extension is determined by the image of $\alpha$. When $\sigma$ is real, conjugate nonreal roots give the same archimedean place and real roots give distinct real places. When $\sigma$ is nonreal, its conjugate differs from $\sigma$, so each complex place over $v$ has exactly one embedding representative extending $\sigma$. This gives precisely the asserted indexing by $w\mid v$. [F3, step 9.1, algebra]

11.1 These factors are the actual archimedean completions $L_w$. For a real factor, the image of $L$ contains $\mathbb Q$, hence is dense in $\mathbb R$. For a complex factor over a real place, the image contains a nonreal root $z$, and therefore contains $\mathbb Q+\mathbb Q z$, dense in $\mathbb C$ because the real-linear map $(a,b)\mapsto a+bz$ is invertible and rational grids are dense in $\mathbb R^2$. For a complex base place, the image of $K$ already contains a nonreal element $z$, and the same argument proves density. The fields $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb C$ are complete. Cauchy sequences from a dense countable subfield therefore give these completions, using least-coded approximants if a specified sequence is needed. The product dimensions are the degrees of the displayed factors, hence sum to $d$. [step 10.1, algebra]

12.1 Scalar extension of the global multiplication matrix and the block determinant argument of step 8.1 now prove the archimedean norm and trace formulas as well. If $d=2$ and $F=\mathbb R$, the two possibilities are two real linear factors or one conjugate pair, giving $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb C$ respectively. For $F=\mathbb C$, there are two distinct linear factors and the algebra is $\mathbb C\times\mathbb C$. On either split product, multiplication by $(a,b)$ has diagonal entries $a,b$, so norm $ab$ and trace $a+b$. On $\mathbb C/\mathbb R$, multiplication by $z=a+ib$ in the basis $1,i$ has matrix with rows $(a,-b)$ and $(b,a)$, so norm $a^2+b^2=z\overline z$ and trace $2a$. The nontrivial quadratic automorphism sends $\alpha$ to the other root of its minimal polynomial; it therefore swaps the two factors in the split cases and is complex conjugation on the nonsplit real factor. This supplies the claimed quadratic local norm maps and the diagonal global norm identity at every place. The polynomial factorisations, choices of root representatives and bases here are finite; no arbitrary algebraic closure or infinite family of choices is used. [F5, step 8.1, step 10.1, step 11.1, algebra] ∎

## Source notes

It does not assert a choice-free theorem for arbitrary complete valued fields or arbitrary valuation extensions.
