## Statement

Assume AC. Let $H$ be a compact connected oriented finite PL surface with nonempty boundary, let $M=H\times[0,1]$, and write $p:M\to H$. Let $P$ be a finite disjoint family of compact connected two-sided properly embedded locally flat PL surfaces, with supplied pair collars, each with nonempty boundary contained in $\operatorname{Int}(H\times\{1\})$. Suppose each inclusion $\pi_1P_i\to\pi_1M$ is injective. Then an ambient PL isotopy of $M$, fixed on all of $\partial M$, carries $P$ to a family on which $p$ is a homeomorphism on every component. Each component is therefore boundary-parallel to a subsurface of the upper end, with an actual parametrized product after the usual boundary-rim collar adjustment. All boundary markings are retained.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The actual finite product, the collared embedded surface family and its component injections.

[F1] Injective finite PL surface maps are coverings or annular boundary collapses, fixing boundary local homeomorphisms. [[thm-pi-one-injective-finite-pl-surface-maps-are-covers-or-annular-collapses]]

[F2] A winding-one proper annulus in a supplied parametrized standard solid torus has marked products on both sides. This requires that actual solid-torus parameter, not merely a primitive loop. [[lem-finite-pl-winding-one-annuli-in-standard-solid-tori-have-marked-products]]

[F3] Standard PL spheres have compatible ball parameters; proper disks in a parametrized ball cut it into parametrized balls. Given disk windows, disk attachment/removal and paired-window reconstruction are finite parameter operations. [[thm-finite-pl-schoenflies-in-dimension-three]], [[lem-parametrized-pl-balls-absorb-standard-boundary-windows]]

[F4] Finite PL surface circles have disk/annulus parameters and minimal peripheral positions. Polygonal disk boundary maps extend; finite collars and strip parameters exist. [[lem-finite-pl-peripheral-multicurves-admit-minimal-annular-position]], [[lem-finite-polygonal-disk-and-collar-surgery]], [[lem-compact-locally-collared-pl-subpolyhedron-has-pl-collar]], [[lem-nullhomotopic-circles-on-finite-oriented-pl-surfaces-bound-pl-disks]]

[A1] [[def-axiom-of-choice]] is retained for [F1,F2,F4].

## Proof

1.1 We record the finite empty-ball moves used throughout. The standard product is a handlebody: cut $H$ by co-cores of a finite graph thickening to a disk; the corresponding rectangles cut $M$ to a ball, and restoring each rectangle attaches a marked one-handle. Such a marked handlebody has a standard embedding in PL three-space with connected exterior. Construct it from a ball and disjoint thin bands about a planar finite graph; disk-window parameters in [F3] match each actual attaching pair to that model. Its exterior is connected, since a polygonal path can first avoid the finite graph and then be pushed off the finitely parametrized band neighborhoods. A sphere inside this model bounds a standard ball by [F3], and its bounded side lies in the handlebody: the connected exterior, containing infinity, is on the other side. Thus $M$ is irreducible. If an interior disk cap disjoint from the entire $P$ family has boundary nullhomotopic in a component of $P$, cap it with the surface disk from [F4]. The resulting sphere has a ball side containing no other surface component: those components are connected, miss its boundary, and have boundary on $\partial M$ outside its interior. The rest of the same surface component is outside for the same reason. Hence its surface-disk patch can be pushed across that empty ball. In the marked ball parameter this is interpolation of a disk roof to its opposite window, fixed on its rim, followed by finite collar extension. This is an actual ambient isotopy fixed on $\partial M$, not a replacement by an unrelated disk. [F3, F4, given, construct]

1.2 We also need simultaneous disk placement in a ball. A finite disjoint proper disk family in a parametrized ball cuts it into parametrized balls by [F3], one disk at a time. Suppose a target disk family has exactly the same boundary circles and the same nesting on the boundary. Its cut balls have the same incidence tree: an outermost boundary cap identifies a leaf, and deleting that leaf proves this by finite induction. Parametrize corresponding disk faces with identical boundary values; on their two copies prescribe the same map through their gluing identifications. Extend over the complementary boundary disks and then over each cut ball by [F3,F4]. Regluing gives a ball homeomorphism fixed on its full boundary and carrying the first disk family to the target. It is isotopic to identity relative boundary: in convex ball coordinates, shrink its support by the formula $h_t(x)=t h(x/t)$ for $|x|\le t$ and $h_t(x)=x$ otherwise, with $h_0=1$. Use a polyhedral radial norm. On cones over its finite affine pieces this is a finite PL map in $(x,t)$, and every slice is a homeomorphism. Thus these simultaneous placements are ambient relative isotopies. The analogous disk-arc placement follows by cutting a disk along disjoint arcs, matching the resulting polygonal disks and using the same two-dimensional construction. In particular through-arcs of a rectangle, whose left and right endpoint orders agree, can be carried to disjoint graph arcs fixing their endpoints. [F3, F4, construct]

2.1 Disk components of $P$ are first put in graph position and peeled off temporarily. Their boundary circles are nullhomotopic in $H$, since the upper end retracts from $M$, and hence bound disks there. Choose an innermost such boundary circle among the whole boundary family. Its upper disk contains no other boundary circle: an essential upper circle cannot lie in that disk, and any inessential one would contradict innermostness. The surface disk and this upper disk bound an empty boundary-attached ball by step 1.1. Mark its upper window and use [F3] to make the surface disk a small graph roof over that window, keeping its rim fixed. Removing the region above this roof leaves another actual product with a dented upper end; its parameter is the finite roof-straightening prism construction in the ball-window lemma. Work there and restore this roof at the end. The remaining surfaces stay outside the peeled ball. Repetition handles all disk components. All later graph normalizations in the dented product transport back to graphs by that same fiberwise roof parameter. [F3, F4, step 1.1, step 1.2, construct]

2.2 For the remaining base, let $H$ be a pair of pants. Its essential upper circles are parallel to its three rims. Simultaneously move these boundary circles to distinct levels in the vertical rim annuli, keeping each parallel-family order. This is a boundary coordinate change, extended through a collar, not yet part of the desired relative isotopy. Its inverse will be included in the model choice below. Projection is now a proper map of each surface to the pair of pants, with boundary local homeomorphisms. Apply [F1]. In the covering case every boundary degree is one in absolute value, so a $d$-sheeted cover has $3d$ boundary circles and Euler characteristic $-d$. The oriented surface formula gives $2-2g-3d=-d$, hence $g=1-d$ and $d=1$. Such a component is a pair of pants with one rim of each type. The collapse case is an annulus whose two rims have the same type. Thus only these two types occur. Choose two standard disjoint vertical rectangles cutting the pants product to a ball. Minimize the total number of their intersection components with the surface family, keeping the boundary positions fixed. Circle intersections can be removed using step 1.1: an innermost rectangle cap misses the whole family, and injectivity supplies its surface disk; the replacement adds no trace on the other rectangle. Thus all remaining traces are arcs. [F1, F3, F4, step 1.1, step 1.2, construct]

3.1 The disk and annulus bases of the induction are now explicit. If $H$ is a disk, injectivity makes every component a disk, so steps 1.2 and 2.1 apply. If $H$ is an annulus, every remaining component has cyclic injected group and therefore is an annulus by the graph-thickening normal form. Its essential upper rim has winding $\pm1$ in $M$, a supplied standard solid torus. Use [F2] with that actual parameter. For a finite disjoint annulus family, choose a meridian whose boundary meets every rim once, after simultaneous boundary normalization [F4]. Remove circle intersections from that disk by innermost disk exchanges on the annuli; their injections make those circles null, and the annulus disk patches miss every other annulus. There is then exactly one spanning disk-intersection arc per annulus. The arcs are disjoint. Cut at the meridian; [F2]'s marked-ball construction gives a ball and a proper disk family, with paired meridian windows. Use step 1.2, matching both window maps, to put these disks at nested graph roofs with the same boundary pairs. The nesting is the noncrossing arc nesting, so the incidence trees match. These relative isotopies glue across the fixed meridian windows. Restoring the upper boundary position puts all annuli at graph roofs over their actual upper annular strips. [F1, F2, F3, F4, step 1.2, step 2.1, construct]

4.1 Each pants component has one through-arc on every rectangle joining the corresponding distinct rim sides. Each annulus component has one return-arc whenever its rim type meets that rectangle. For a return-arc choose an outermost cap towards its vertical rim side; its interval on that side contains no other endpoint. The cap boundary-compresses its annulus. The annular boundary strip between the two rims contains no other boundary circle of the entire family, since each such circle meets this rectangle side once. We justify the product used here. Glue that strip to the annulus to form a torus and compress it along the displayed cap; it becomes a sphere. Irreducibility supplies a ball. The restored cap neighborhood is outside that ball: its strip along the ambient boundary is joined to $\partial M$ through the gap in the compressed strip, whereas an interior ball cannot contain ambient boundary. Restoring the cap therefore adds one parametrized one-handle to a ball. The marked disk-window construction [F3] gives an actual STANDARD solid-torus parameter, and its cocore meets the annulus core once. Enlarge its boundary collar slightly so the annulus is proper in that standard solid torus, retaining the boundary-strip marking. Now [F2] applies with winding one. Its side against the displayed strip is the required marked product. It contains no other surface: every other component is disjoint from its frontier and has boundary outside that strip. Move this annulus to a thin collar roof and peel off its product. Retain the rectangle marks in this operation. Every other chosen vertical rectangle meets this enclosure in either nothing or one meridian disk: its only frontier trace is the one spanning annulus arc and its boundary-strip interval. These disks are disjoint. Cut the standard solid torus at one meridian and then at the others; [F3] gives parametrized balls. Match their disk windows in their circle order to standard meridian slices, and cone the remaining marked boundary maps. Thus the enclosure parameter makes all those rectangle sections actual meridian slices simultaneously. The remaining product coordinates are then the two-dimensional strip-roof straightening, times this marked rim circle; the finite polygonal prism maps respect every rectangle section. Repeat until no annulus component remains. [F2, F3, F4, step 1.1, step 3.1, step 2.2, construct]

5.1 Only pants components remain. On each vertical rectangle their through-arcs have the same endpoint order on its opposite sides, since they are disjoint. Place them at graph arcs by step 1.2, extending the two-dimensional moves over thin normal rectangle collars. The two rectangles connect all three rim types, so that order is the SAME on every rim. Cutting gives proper disks in the cut ball, one from each pants component. Their boundary circles are ordered graph circles on the vertical boundary of that ball. Cone a finite boundary subdivision to one interior vertex of the base disk and assign increasing interior heights in the same order. Linear extension gives disjoint graph disks with exactly those boundary circles. Step 1.2 puts the actual disk family onto these models, relative to all cut-ball faces. Regluing gives an ambient relative isotopy. The initial boundary coordinate change is removed by conjugation: choose its collar map and these model roofs together in the rim quadrant. The original upper parallel circles bound nested trimmed pants; assign graph heights in their common order, zero on their own rims, and use the finite disk-arc parameter to carry their rim sections to the chosen vertical levels. Thus its inverse carries the chosen models to graphs over precisely those trimmed pants. Conjugating the boundary-fixed normalization by this coordinate change is fixed on the original entire boundary at EVERY time. Restore the peeled annuli and disks in reverse order with their retained roof coordinates. This proves the three base types with all markings, not merely a final boundary homotopy. [F3, F4, step 1.2, step 2.1, step 2.2, step 4.1, construct]

6.1 Induct on $(g(H),b(H))$ lexicographically for all other surfaces. Choose an essential nonperipheral circle $c$ in $H$, disjoint from $\partial P$. Such a circle exists: if a boundary curve of $P$ is essential nonperipheral take a parallel copy; otherwise the entire boundary family lies in rim collars or disk regions and an essential handle or planar separating circle avoids it. Put $A=c\times[0,1]$ transverse to $P$, with the number of intersection circles least among boundary-fixed isotopies. There are no intersection arcs, since $\partial P$ misses $\partial A$. Inessential circles on $A$ are removed by step 1.1. Every remaining circle is essential on $A$ and on $P$, by the injection of the vertical annulus into $M$ and the supplied injections. Cut along $A$. Each cut surface piece remains injective: its inclusion into its old component injects because cutting along essential circles is an injective surface amalgam, and then its map into the product is injective. The same conclusion follows from an essential compression disk: its disk on the uncut surface cannot cross a remaining intersection circle, since that would make that circle inessential. [F4, step 1.1, step 5.1, construct]

7.1 Each resulting base surface has smaller complexity. A nonseparating cut lowers genus; a separating cut either lowers genus on the piece or cuts off a planar region with at least two old rims, thereby lowering that piece's boundary count. Near each cut face first make all intersection circles horizontal in its annulus coordinates. Push those new boundary circles up the cut-face annuli and slightly into their upper end, keeping their projected circle maps injective and their parallel order. This is again a finite boundary coordinate change with its collar extension. Apply the induction hypothesis in every cut product, fixing its entire current boundary. It supplies graph pieces and their actual parallel product parameters. Now return the cut-face circles one at a time to their original levels. As long as a graph component meets a given cut face only once, the inverse rim-quadrant map retains a graph: there is one boundary arc at that rim, so no fold can be forced by another shore of the same component. Different components retain their finite vertical order. The sole possible obstruction is a component already having a rim on that SAME cut face when its next rim is returned. This case is treated next. [F4, step 5.1, step 6.1, induction, construct]

8.1 In that obstruction, the graph base contains two boundary circles parallel to the SAME boundary circle of the cut base. It must be the annulus between them: an embedded connected subsurface with both of these as boundary cannot cross either circle to leave that annular strip. Thus the component $C$ is an annulus. Its inductive parallelism is an ACTUAL annulus-times-interval parameter. Pull it back through the preceding boundary coordinate change. Its boundary target is the annular strip $A_0$ between the two original levels on that cut face; this is the only subsurface on that boundary end with those two rims and annular topology. Consequently the intervening region $V$ is already a parametrized standard solid torus, not an unclassified cyclic manifold. Every other cut-surface component inside $V$ injects its group into $V$: a loop killed in $V$ is killed in the cut product and hence in that surface. Its boundary lies in $A_0$ and is essential there, so it is an annulus, and its core has winding $\pm1$. It is neither a disk (its rim is essential) nor a closed positive-genus surface (such a surface cannot inject into a cyclic group). No closed sphere component can appear by cutting the given boundary surfaces along essential circles. [F1, F2, F4, step 6.1, step 7.1, construct]

9.1 Enlarge the ACTUAL $V$ of step 8.1 through a thin collar of $C$, so $C$ is a proper annulus inside the enlarged $V^+$ and its rims lie on a slightly extended boundary strip $A_0^+$. The known annulus-times-interval parameter extends across this collar, giving a standard solid-torus parameter for $V^+$. Choose the collar thin enough to miss every other surface outside $V$ and every other boundary rim; there are finitely many disjoint compact pieces. Thus the annulus family consists of $C$ and the components already inside $V$, all proper in the ACTUAL standard $V^+$. Apply [F2] there. To select an empty product from the finite annulus family, choose a meridian meeting every rim once and clear its circle intersections as in step 3.1. All remaining intersection arcs have their endpoints on the one boundary interval corresponding to $A_0$. An outermost such arc towards that interval has consecutive endpoints. The two corresponding rims are therefore adjacent in the entire rim family on $A_0$. The marked product of [F2] on that annulus's side against their intervening strip contains no other cut-surface component: any such component is disjoint from its frontier but has boundary outside that empty strip, so is outside. Push this annulus through its empty product to the other side of the original cut annulus $A$, fixed on the original $\partial M$. This removes its two intersection circles and creates none; all moves occur in the displayed product and its thin collar. This contradicts the minimality chosen in step 6.1. The obstruction in step 7.1 therefore cannot occur. All inverse rim moves retain graph pieces, and they glue over the two cut faces to a surface on which $p$ is locally a homeomorphism. The induction uses the pulled-back marked products at each occurrence; no projection of a covering-space isotopy was involved. [F2, F3, F4, step 3.1, step 6.1, step 7.1, step 8.1, construct]

10.1 Finally local injectivity of projection is global on each connected component. If two points in one component lie above the same base point, take a path in that component from the upper one to the lower one. Continue the lower sheet below this path using local inverse charts. It cannot terminate at the boundary, because that boundary has height one and the continued sheet is BELOW a sheet of height at most one. Compactness and local inverse charts continue it for the whole path. At its endpoint there is a third strictly lower point over the same base point. Repeat to obtain arbitrarily many points in that fiber. But a projection fiber in the compact surface is discrete by local injectivity and hence finite, a contradiction. Thus projection is a homeomorphism on each component. Its graph and the upper image bound the explicit graph-collar region, with boundary rim quadrants restored by the finite window parameters. This is the actual marked boundary-parallel product. Every auxiliary boundary coordinate change was used only to conjugate a boundary-fixed isotopy, so the final ambient isotopy is fixed on all of $\partial M$. This completes the induction and the assertion. [F3, F4, step 5.1, step 9.1, construct] ∎
