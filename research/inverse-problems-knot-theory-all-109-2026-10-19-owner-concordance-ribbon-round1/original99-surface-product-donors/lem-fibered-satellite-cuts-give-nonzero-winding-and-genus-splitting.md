## Statement

Assume full AC. Let a smooth fibered satellite knot be written $K=P(C)$ with essential companion torus $T$, nontrivial companion $C$, and pattern not isotopic to the core of its solid torus. Let $w$ be its signed winding number. Then $w\ne0$ and
$$g(K)=g(P(U))+|w|g(C).$$
More precisely, a product decomposition obtained by cutting the given fiber produces connected-surface mapping-torus descriptions of the companion and pattern-closure exteriors, with fiber surfaces of their minimal Seifert genera. If the symmetric Alexander polynomial of $K$ has nonzero coefficients in both its highest degree and the immediately next degree, then $P(U)$ is nontrivial. No instanton theorem is assumed or proved by this assertion.

## Proof

1.1 Let $\chi:\pi_1E_K\to\mathbb Z$ be the given fiber homomorphism. Its cyclic cover is the actual product $F\times\mathbb R$, so its kernel is the free group $\pi_1F$. The peripheral marking gives $\chi(\mu_C)=w$: an oriented companion meridian disk meets the pattern algebraically $w$ times. It gives $\chi(\lambda_C)=0$, since a companion Seifert surface lies outside the pattern solid torus and bounds that longitude. If $w=0$, its entire injected torus group would lie in this kernel, giving an injection $\mathbb Z^2\to\pi_1F$. This is impossible. In a graph universal-cover tree, a nontrivial free-group element has its unique repeated-word translation axis. Every commuting element preserves that axis and acts by an integral translation; a deck element fixing a vertex is the identity. Thus an abelian subgroup containing a nontrivial element embeds in the cyclic translation group. This rules out $\mathbb Z^2$, and proves $w\ne0$. [given, A1, F5, construct, algebra]

1.2 We prove the finite vertical-annulus operation needed here. For one injected proper annulus $A\subset H\times I$, $H$ a compact oriented surface with boundary, with one rim on each end, projection gives a free homotopy of its essential rims. Use [F2] on the upper end and extend through its collar to align the upper rim with the lower circle $c$, fixing the lower end and $\partial H\times I$. Choose a proper arc $k$ in $H$ meeting $c$ minimally in one or two points. Such an arc is explicit in a disk-and-bands parameter: if the two sides of $c$ reach boundary, join the boundary sides through $c$ once; if one side has no boundary, it has positive genus, and an essential returning arc through one of its bands gives two crossings. In the two-crossing case the intervening segment of $k$ is not homotopic relative endpoints into $c$ on its side. That side injects into $H$ by the surface amalgam normal form, so it is not homotopic into either subarc of $c$ even in $H$. Put $A$ transverse to the rectangle $k\times I$ and minimize its intersections. Circle intersections are null on the rectangle and hence null on $A$ by injection; innermost empty-ball moves remove them. A returning intersection arc on $A$ with both ends on one rim would cut off an annulus disk. Its projection makes the corresponding segment of $c$ homotopic into $k$ relative endpoints. With one crossing there are not two endpoints; with two crossings this gives the forbidden boundary-parallel returning arc just excluded. Thus all remaining rectangle arcs span the two ends. [F2, F3, construct]

2.1 Use [F5] to take compatible finite structures on the smooth pair and its transverse cut faces. Make $T$ transverse to the original fiber and minimize its intersection circles by ambient isotopies. An inessential circle on either injected surface is inessential on both; the finite innermost-disk and empty-ball move removes it. The exterior is irreducible, since an interior sphere in its standard $S^3$ has a ball side disjoint from the connected knot tube. Hence all remaining circles are essential on both surfaces. Cutting $E_K$ at $F$ gives the actual product $F\times I$ and cutting $T$ gives injected proper annuli. There is at least one circle, since otherwise an injected torus would lie in a product with free fundamental group. [F2, F3, F5, step 1.1, construct]

2.2 On that rectangle the spanning arcs have the same endpoint order at bottom and top. Since the two rim circles were aligned, each endpoint matches itself. A finite disk-arc isotopy puts these arcs at the vertical intervals, keeping the rectangle boundary fixed. Cutting along the rectangle now cuts $A$ into proper disks with exactly the same boundary curves as the cut pieces of $c\times I$. These disk families are relatively isotopic in the cut product: separate their common boundary collars, remove innermost intersection circles by the irreducible empty-ball move, and then each matched disk pair bounds a ball. Every other disk has boundary on the ambient boundary outside that ball, so is outside; finite paired-window ball parameters realize the moves while fixing the cut faces. Regluing gives an isotopy carrying $A$ to $c\times I$, fixed on the lower end and vertical boundary. For finitely many disjoint bridging annuli, do this to the first and cut at its now vertical annulus. The cut pieces remain actual products over $H$ cut at $c$. Every remaining annulus lies in one such product, so repeat there, fixing its new vertical faces. This finite induction makes the whole annulus family vertical; upper-end maps may change, but the lower end and all old vertical boundary remain fixed. [F2, F3, step 1.2, construct]

3.1 No one of these annuli has both rims on the same end. If there were one, [F1] gives its actual parallel product against that end. Any other cut annulus inside this product has both rims there, since its other-end rim could not lie in this region disjoint from the opposite end. Apply [F1] to the finite family inside and take the end-nearest member; its product is empty of all remaining annuli. It meets only that one fiber face, so is embedded after regluing. Pushing across it removes two intersection circles of $T$ and $F$, contradicting the minimum. Thus every cut annulus joins the lower and upper fiber faces. [F1, F3, step 2.1, construct]

4.1 The upper-end qualification matters. The preceding cut-product isotopy need NOT descend as a self-isotopy of the original mapping torus. Instead let $h$ be its terminal product homeomorphism, with lower restriction the identity and upper restriction $h_1$. If the original gluing is $\phi$, give the target product the NEW gluing $\phi' =\phi h_1^{-1}$, using the convention that $\phi$ identifies upper to lower. Then $h$ induces an actual homeomorphism of the original exterior to that new mapping torus. The new gluing preserves the finite vertical circle family, since the original gluing paired exactly the original annulus rims. Pull back the target level bundle. This is a genuine bundle on the original exterior in which the actual torus is a subbundle, and its zero fiber is the ORIGINAL smooth cut fiber, because $h$ fixes the lower face. Its integral cohomology class is still $\chi$: $h$ fixes the old vertical boundary annulus, so the positive knot meridian still crosses the new base once, and [F5] says that meridian generates the exterior abelianization. This argument constructs a useful bundle and does not falsely project an unmatched boundary isotopy or identify a different fiber by fiat. [F3, F5, step 3.1, step 2.2, construct]

5.1 Each side of the torus now inherits a bundle whose fiber may have finitely many components. Its components are permuted transitively because the side is connected. For the companion side the number is exactly $|w|$: its induced fundamental-group image in the base is $w\mathbb Z$, since its meridian maps to $w$ and generates its abelianization. Choosing one component and its first return gives a connected-surface mapping torus for $E_C$; if $w<0$, reverse the base orientation to use the positive companion-meridian class. Its boundary is connected: the same primitive companion homomorphism maps the boundary-torus meridian to one and longitude to zero, so a boundary fiber is one circle. Round its finitely many polygonal boundary corners inside the original smooth $F$, extending over the corresponding finite rim quadrants; this is a local rounding of disjoint finite curves and changes neither genus nor the product marking. Denote the resulting connected smooth zero-fiber piece by $F_C$. For the pattern side the base image is all of $\mathbb Z$, since the pattern meridian maps to one, so its zero fiber $F_X$ is connected. Its boundary is the knot rim and the $|w|$ longitude rims on the outer torus. [step 1.1, step 4.1, construct, algebra]

6.1 Filling the companion side by the unknot exterior extends this bundle across $|w|$ meridian disks. On its solid torus use the map to the base whose degree on its core meridian is $w$; its fibers are exactly those $|w|$ disks. Its boundary matches the torus bundle, after the marked circle coordinates are adjusted through their finite collars. The capped connected surface $F_P=F_X\cup(|w|\text{ disks})$ is therefore a fiber in a connected-surface mapping-torus description of $E_{P(U)}$. For a completely marked extension, extend each circle return map over its cap disk by coning; their cyclic disk-family mapping torus is a solid torus with meridian slope $\lambda_C$. A change of the other boundary coordinate by an integral meridian twist extends over that solid torus, so the gluing can be made exactly the original pattern-closure gluing. The zero surfaces $F_C,F_X$ were actual pieces of the original smooth fiber; cap disks and their finite collar corners can be rounded in the actual solid torus, preserving genus and its longitude marking. No smoothing of an arbitrary topological three-manifold is required. [F3, step 5.1, construct]

7.1 These fiber surfaces realize their knots' MINIMAL Seifert genera. A connected-surface mapping torus has cyclic cover the surface times $\mathbb R$. If its fiber has genus $g$ and one boundary, its Alexander module over $\Lambda$ has presentation $tI-B$ on $H_1(F;\mathbb Z)$, where $B$ is the integral automorphism induced by first return. Choose an integral basis from a finite surface graph. The determinant of $tI-B$ has degree $2g$, leading coefficient one and constant coefficient $\det B=\pm1$, so its breadth is $2g$. This is the actual cover homology presentation: identify the module with the free abelian group $H_1F$ and let $t$ act as $B$; evaluation of Laurent words gives exactly those relations. By [F4] the breadth is at most twice any smooth spanning genus. Extend its preferred-longitude boundary to the knot through the provided tube collar, reversing its surface orientation if the base was reversed. The actual smooth zero fiber then provides the opposite upper bound. Thus the fiber genus is the knot genus, for $K,C$ and $P(U)$. [F4, step 5.1, step 6.1, construct, algebra]

8.1 Euler characteristic now gives the claimed exact equality, rather than a quoted general satellite inequality. The $|w|$ companion fiber pieces have Euler characteristic $|w|(1-2g(C))$. Also $\chi(F_X)=\chi(F_P)-|w|=1-2g(P(U))-|w|$. Gluing them along circles changes no Euler characteristic. Hence $1-2g(K)=1-2g(P(U))-2|w|g(C)$, or $g(K)=g(P(U))+|w|g(C)$. [step 5.1, step 6.1, step 7.1, algebra]

8.2 Suppose finally that $P(U)$ were the unknot and $\Delta_K$ had nonzero highest and adjacent coefficients. The actual satellite Alexander-cover calculation gives $\Delta_K(t)=\Delta_C(t^w)$ under symmetric normalization: in lifted Mayer–Vietoris the outer longitude is zero on the companion cover because it bounds its lifted Seifert surface; $H_0$ of the lifted torus maps isomorphically to companion $H_0$, so the module splits as the pattern-closure module plus the induced companion module. Square Seifert determinants give the product formula, and the unknot factor is one. All occurring exponent differences on the right are divisible by $|w|$, so adjacent nonzero exponents force $|w|=1$. The capped pattern fiber $F_P$ is a disk by step 7.1, so $F_X$ is then an annulus. [F4, step 1.1, step 5.1, step 7.1, construct, algebra]

9.1 The pattern exterior is consequently the mapping torus of an oriented annulus. An orientation-preserving annulus homeomorphism preserving its two boundary components is isotopic, allowing boundary rotations, to the identity. To see this in the finite PL coordinates here, align a spanning arc with its image after allowing the integer boundary twist; cutting that arc gives a disk, whose residual boundary-fixed map is isotopic to identity by the marked disk Alexander construction. The integer twist has the explicit isotopy $(\theta,s)\mapsto(\theta+tks,s)$ when boundary rotations are allowed. Its mapping torus is therefore $T^2\times I$. This is a marked product whose ends are the ACTUAL inner and outer pattern tori, not merely an abstract unmarked exterior identification. Therefore the companion torus cobounds a product with the inner knot-boundary component of $E_K$, contradicting its essentiality by [F7]. The smooth implementation is given next so no smooth-core isotopy or arbitrary PL-to-smooth assertion is hidden in this contradiction. [F2, F3, F7, step 8.2, construct, algebra]

10.1 All product maps needed in this smooth-knot application can be chosen smooth from the outset, as follows. Use the finite PL arguments only to specify their finite disk/rectangle/circle incidence patterns; begin with the actual smooth surfaces and collars. Circle bigon moves in a smooth surface use smoothly parametrized disk regions and the smooth interval-window version of [F6]. For the empty sphere balls use its smooth Schoenflies supplier, rounding the finitely many disk seams in their supplied quadrant collars first. First match the two smooth disk boundary collars while fixing the cut face. In coordinates $(s,u,r)$ with cut face $r=0$, write them as $u=f(s,r)$ and $u=g(s,r)$ with $f(s,0)=g(s,0)$. The map $u\mapsto u+t\eta(r)\zeta(u)(g(s,r)-f(s,r))$, using fixed smooth cutoffs, has positive $u$ derivative after the radial support is made small, since $g-f$ vanishes at $r=0$. It is identity on the cut face and sends one collar to the other on a smaller collar. Marked smooth ball windows then put the two disks of each empty-ball move in a lens parameter: away from this retained common boundary collar the two disks are graphs $z=\pm h(x,y)$ over a disk, with $h>0$ inside and $h=0$ at the rim. Interpolation between these graphs is a smooth embedding family because its two base coordinates are unchanged. Choose it constant on the already matched smaller rim collars and stationary near the time endpoints; [F6]'s compact-source extension realizes it ambiently, with support away from the protected cut faces. Thus all innermost disk moves and disk-family placements in steps 1.2 and 2.2 are smooth embedding moves, not smooth Alexander isotopies of arbitrary three-ball maps. The sphere boundary extensions used to match cut-ball parameters are smooth as well: standardize the image of one boundary hemisphere with its FULL disk parameter by the smooth-window isotopy in [F6]; the residual boundary map is identity on that hemisphere, and its map on the complementary disk is isotopic to identity by the proved relative smooth disk theorem, with matching collars. This supplies an actual sphere isotopy; extend it over the ball with the radial formula $r\omega\mapsto r h_{\alpha(r)}(\omega)$, where $\alpha=0$ on an inner ball and $\alpha=1$ near the boundary. This is smooth because it is identity on an entire inner ball. Reflection handles the necessary fixed orientation sign. In the half-product use of step 3.1, these same operations implement [F1]'s finite proof: all cut-face rim moves are smooth collar maps; its annulus enclosures are smooth balls with one marked smooth handle, hence actual parametrized smooth solid tori; their meridian cuts and ordered disk placements use the just established smooth windows; and ordered disk/annulus graph roofs are interpolated with strict height order and fixed collar values. No residual arbitrary ball diffeomorphism is isotoped by radial shrinking. This gives the needed smooth empty parallel products for the intersection-minimization move. The rectangle moves of step 2.2 and their finite successive cut products therefore have smooth terminal diffeomorphisms fixing the lower and old vertical faces. Thus step 4.1 defines a smooth target gluing and a smooth bundle, with smooth first-return maps and smooth marked cap-disk extensions. For the annulus return in step 9.1, first straighten its boundary circle maps by their increasing angular lifts, extended through smooth collars. Its spanning arc and image are aligned by the finite smooth disk moves above; the residual boundary-fixed disk map is handled by the actual relative smooth disk isotopy in [F6]. The integer annular twist is isotoped through $(\theta,s)\mapsto(\theta+tks,s)$, allowing boundary rotations. Its smooth isotopy to identity gives the explicit diffeomorphism of its mapping torus to $T^2\times I$, retaining both end tori. Consequently the product contradiction in step 9.1 is in the intended smooth category. This proves $P(U)$ nontrivial and completes all assertions without a global smoothing theorem or a stronger unneeded core-isotopy assertion. [F1, F2, F3, F6, F7, step 1.2, step 2.2, step 3.1, step 4.1, step 9.1, construct] ∎
