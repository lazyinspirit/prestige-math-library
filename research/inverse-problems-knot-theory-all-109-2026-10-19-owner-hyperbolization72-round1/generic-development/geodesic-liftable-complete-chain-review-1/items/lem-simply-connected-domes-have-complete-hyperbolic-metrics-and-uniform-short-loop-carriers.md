## Statement

Assume AC. Let $D\subset S^2$ be a simply connected hyperbolic domain, let $L=S^2\setminus D$, and suppose its closed hyperbolic convex hull $C=\operatorname{CH}(L)$ has nonempty three-dimensional interior. Give $S=\partial C\cap\mathbb H^3$ the length metric induced by ambient hyperbolic lengths of rectifiable curves. Then $S$ is a complete hyperbolic surface, is simply connected, and with that metric is isometric to $\mathbb H^2$.

For $z\in D$, expand horoballs based at $z$ until the first one touches $C$, and let $r(z)$ be the contact point. This defines a continuous proper surjection $r:D\to S$, natural under all orientation-preserving Möbius transformations. If $\rho_D$ is the curvature-minus-one Poincaré metric, every rectifiable curve $\alpha$ in $D$ satisfies

$$\ell_S(r\alpha)\le4\ell_{\rho_D}(\alpha).$$

In particular, if a discrete torsion-free group $H$ preserves $D$, the map descends to a proper surjection $r_H:D/H\to S/H$. Both are surfaces with simply connected covering spaces $D,S$, and under their deck-group identifications the induced map on fundamental groups is the identity of $H$. The same length inequality holds for quotient loops, including loops arbitrarily far out a puncture. The intrinsic metric on $S/H$ is complete. If $H$ is non-elementary and $X=D/H$ has a supplied compact truncation by finitely many standard puncture cusps for its disk metric, then $S/H$ also has a compact standard cusp truncation and finite area. The map gives a bijection of their cusp ends and identifies their primitive peripheral cyclic subgroups, up to conjugation and inversion.

If moreover $H$ is non-elementary and $D$ is a full component of its ordinary set, then $\partial D=\Lambda H$. In that case $S$ is exactly the union of exposed faces of $\operatorname{CH}(\Lambda H)$ whose exterior round disk is contained in $D$. This specifies the $D$-facing piece and does not assert that it is all of the convex-hull boundary.

The nonempty-interior condition is automatic when such an $H$ is non-elementary: the only excluded simply connected hyperbolic-domain case is the complement of a nontrivial closed round-circle arc; its dome is interpreted as the double of a hyperbolic half-plane, and any group preserving that arc preserves its two endpoints and is elementary. This lemma does not identify $\operatorname{CH}(S^2\setminus D)$ with $\operatorname{CH}(\Lambda H)$, or assert geometric finiteness of $H$; finite area of the dome quotient is concluded only under the explicit conformal-quotient cusp hypothesis.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The actual domain, hull and length metric. The group is supplied only for the quotient conclusions.

[F1] AC is [[def-axiom-of-choice]]. Equicontinuous maps of a compact metric domain into a proper metric space with pointwise bounded images have a uniformly convergent subsequence by [[cor-arzela-ascoli-subsequence-theorem-for-proper-metric-targets]]. Hyperbolic three-space is proper: in the hyperboloid model a closed distance ball has bounded Euclidean coordinates and is closed.

[F2] The complete-surface facts used below have the following precise derivation. For a complete locally hyperbolic surface $Y$, the full Statement of [[thm-cartan-hadamard]] directly makes $\exp_p:(T_pY,\exp_p^*g)\to Y$ a universal covering with complete pulled-back metric. This constructs the universal cover here; it does not presuppose a separate cover-existence or completeness theorem. On that tangent plane, use polar coordinates about zero. For curvature $-1$, [[prop-curvature-tensor-of-constant-sectional-curvature]], [[thm-differential-of-the-exponential-map-in-terms-of-jacobi-fields]] and [[thm-existence-and-uniqueness-of-jacobi-fields-from-initial-data]] give the orthogonal angular Jacobi field $\sinh(t)E(t)$, where $E$ is the parallel unit field: the scalar equation is $j''-j=0$, with data $j(0)=0,j'(0)=1$. Together with [[thm-gauss-lemma]] this makes the exponential metric $dt^2+\sinh^2t\,d\theta^2$, exactly the hyperbolic-plane metric. Thus the actual exponential universal cover is isometric to $\mathbb H^2$. A radius-$a$ ball about a point of the surface is a standard hyperbolic ball if every noncontractible based loop there has length greater than $4a$: otherwise a shortest displacement of a nonidentity deck transformation produces a loop of length at most $4a$; conversely such displacements are the only identifications of that radius ball. Minimizing paths and compact metric balls are available by [[thm-hopf-rinow]]. These suppliers require only countable choice, implied by [F1].

[F3] The disk uniformizer exists by [[thm-riemann-mapping-theorem]] after moving one complementary point to infinity; simple connectedness implies the required zero complementary winding numbers. If $f:\mathbb D\to D\subset\mathbb C$ is univalent, normalized at $z=f(0)$, the actual quarter-disk theorem [[thm-koebe-one-quarter-theorem]] gives $\delta_D(z)\ge|f'(0)|/4$. Since $\rho_D(z)=2/|f'(0)|$, it follows that $1/\delta_D(z)\le2\rho_D(z)$. This estimate is applied only to the simply connected domain $D$, not to the finitely punctured approximants.

[F4] Jordan separation and its planar extension are supplied by [[lem-jordan-schoenflies-extension-for-plane-curves]]. The elementary-group and elliptic/parabolic/loxodromic restrictions for discrete torsion-free Möbius groups are in [[lem-local-thin-components-of-torsion-free-kleinian-quotients]].

## Proof

1.1 Use the Klein ball for convexity, moving an interior point of $C$ to the origin. In that model the closed hull is the intersection with the open unit ball of the Euclidean closed convex hull of $L$. Its ideal boundary is exactly $L$: a point of the strictly convex unit sphere outside the closed set $L$ has a separating supporting cap and cannot belong to its closed Euclidean convex hull. For each direction $v\in S^2$, let $t(v)v$ be the first exit from $C$ along its ray, with $t(v)=1$ if the whole ray remains in $C$. An inscribed ball gives $t(v)\ge a>0$. Every supporting affine plane has distance at least $a$ from the origin; equivalently the polar convex body is contained in the ball of radius $1/a$, and $1/t(v)$ is its support function. Hence $t$ is continuous, and is locally Lipschitz where $t<1$. The directions with $t<1$ are precisely $D$. The radial exit map $q(v)=t(v)v$ therefore is a homeomorphism $D\to S$, with inverse $x\mapsto x/|x|$. In particular $S$ is a connected simply connected topological surface. On a closed angular disk compactly contained in $D$, its radial graph and its inverse have bounded Lipschitz constants in both Euclidean and hyperbolic metrics: its radii are bounded away from zero and one. This also proves that its intrinsic length metric is finite locally and induces its surface topology. [given, construct, algebra]

2.1 Choose nested finite subsets $L_n\subset L$ with dense union, containing four noncoplanar points whose hull contains the origin in its interior. Such four points can be supplied by moving the origin to the interior of one nondegenerate ideal tetrahedron in $C$; if necessary initially choose that interior point rather than the original one. Write $C_n=\operatorname{CH}(L_n)$ and $S_n=\partial C_n\cap\mathbb H^3$. The closed Euclidean hulls converge to the closed hull of $L$: approximate each finite convex combination by points of the dense union, and use that in $\mathbb R^3$ each convex combination reduces to at most four terms by eliminating an affine dependence. The polar bodies decrease to the polar body of $C$ and are all contained in a fixed ball. Their support functions converge uniformly, because their common Lipschitz bound plus pointwise convergence on the compact direction sphere gives uniform convergence by a finite net. Thus the radial functions $t_n$ converge uniformly to $t$. On every angular disk compactly contained in $D$, all sufficiently large $S_n$ have the same radial graph coordinates as step 1.1 with uniform bounds on radii, Lipschitz constants, and inverse Lipschitz constants. This proves the needed local boundary convergence as well as convex-set convergence, without appealing to a theorem about nonsmooth convex-surface metrics. [F1, step 1.1, choose, construct, algebra]

2.2 For $z\notin L$, a horoball about $z$ meets $C$ first at a unique ordinary point. To verify existence and uniqueness, normalize $z=\infty$ in upper half-space. Since $L$ is a compact subset of the finite plane, $C$ has finite maximal height, attained on a compact subset. Equivalently, the Busemann function based at $z$ has compact sublevel sets on $C$ and attains its minimum. Its restriction to any geodesic segment between two distinct minimizers is strictly convex unless that segment is directed toward $z$; the latter case is not constant either. Hence there is exactly one minimizer. This is $r(z)$. The tangent horosphere has a supporting plane at the contact point, normal to the geodesic toward $z$. Compactness of minimizers and uniqueness prove continuity in $z$. The construction uses only horospheres and isometries, so is Möbius natural. It is onto: for any $p\in S$ choose a supporting plane there and its outward normal endpoint $z$; that endpoint is outside $L$, since otherwise the ray from $p$ toward $z$ would lie in $C$ and enter the exterior tangent horoball. The tangent horoball then touches $C$ precisely at $p$. [step 1.1, construct, algebra]

3.1 Each $C_n$ is an ideal polyhedron. Indeed it is the convex hull of finitely many extreme points on the ideal sphere, and all its vertices are those ideal points. Its ordinary boundary is a finite union of totally geodesic ideal polygonal faces, joined along geodesic edges with no ordinary vertex. In the interior of a face the length metric is hyperbolic. At an ordinary edge, reflect one face across the edge in its intrinsic hyperbolic plane; the two half-disks then form a hyperbolic disk, irrespective of the exterior dihedral angle. There is consequently a smooth hyperbolic atlas on $S_n$. Its metric is complete: an intrinsic Cauchy sequence is ambient Cauchy and has an ordinary limit, since hyperbolic space is complete; the boundary is closed there, and a finite face or unfolded-edge chart gives intrinsic convergence at that limit. The universal-cover description and the short-loop criterion in [F2] apply to $S_n$. [F2, step 2.1, construct, algebra]

3.2 The map $r$ is proper. If $K\subset S$ is compact, consider all pairs $(p,z)\in K\times S^2$ for which the horosphere based at $z$ through $p$ supports $C$ on its inner side. The support inequalities are closed conditions on $(p,z)$: for each $q\in C$ the normalized Busemann value at $q$ is at least its value at $p$. Thus this set of pairs is compact. None has $z\in L$, because the ray from $p$ to such an ideal point lies in $C$ and strictly decreases that Busemann value. Its projection to $S^2$ is consequently a compact subset of $D$. By step 2.2 that projection is exactly $r^{-1}(K)$. This proves properness without a boundary-extension theorem for a Riemann map. [step 1.1, step 2.2, construct, algebra]

3.3 Define the round-disk density $\tau$ on $D$ by taking the infimum of the curvature-minus-one hyperbolic densities of round disks contained in $D$ and containing the evaluation point. In a finite plane chart, let $h(z)$ be the Euclidean radius of the largest horoball based at $z$ whose interior misses $C$. Then $\tau(z)=1/h(z)$. Here is the normalization calculation: if $m:\mathbb D\to B\subset D$ is Möbius with $m(0)=z$, the horoball at $z$ tangent to the plane over $\partial B$ has radius $|m'(0)|/2$; move $z$ to infinity by inversion, or apply a parabolic fixing $z$ to move the tangency point vertically over $z$, to obtain this formula from the concentric-disk case. These horoballs all avoid $C$, so $2/|m'(0)|\ge1/h(z)$. Conversely the support plane normal to the first-contact horosphere defines a round disk in $D$ through this same calculation and gives equality. For the finite hulls put $D_n=S^2\setminus L_n$ and define $h_n,\tau_n,r_n$ in the same way. The minimizer argument of step 2.2, convex-set convergence, and uniqueness imply $r_n(z)\to r(z)$ and $h_n(z)\to h(z)$, uniformly on compact subsets of $D$: if uniform convergence failed, extract convergent basepoints in that compact set, use the fixed initial ideal hull to bound the minimizers, and take a subsequential support inequality limit, which has the unique limiting contact point. Thus $\tau_n\to\tau$ uniformly there as well. [step 2.1, step 2.2, construct, algebra]

3.4 Under the additional full-ordinary-component hypothesis, $\partial D\subset\Lambda H$: a boundary point in the ordinary set would have a connected ordinary neighborhood meeting $D$, hence would belong to that same open component. Conversely $\Lambda H\subset\partial D$. To see this without geometric finiteness, let distinct $h_n\in H$ take an ordinary basepoint of $\mathbb H^3$ to a given limit point $\xi$. Normalize representing complex matrices by their Euclidean norm and take a convergent subsequence. Discreteness forces the unnormalized norms to tend to infinity, so the nonzero limiting matrix has determinant zero and rank one. The associated projective maps converge away from its one kernel point to its one image point; the hyperboloid or positive-Hermitian-matrix model shows this image point is $\xi$. The closed invariant set $\partial D$ has more than one point for a non-elementary group. Choose a point of it different from that kernel point. Its images stay in $\partial D$ and tend to $\xi$, proving the inclusion. Thus $\partial D=\Lambda H$. For any support plane $P$ of $C$ on the $D$ side, its exterior round disk $B$ is contained in $D$, and $L\cap\partial B\subset\partial D$: a point interior to $L$ cannot be approached from the open disk $B\subset D$. Its exposed face is the convex hull in $P$ of those ideal contacts, by the Euclidean supporting-plane description in the Klein model. All those contacts lie in $\Lambda H$, so this face is also the exposed face of $\operatorname{CH}(\Lambda H)$ at $P$. Every point of $S$ lies on a support plane of this kind. Conversely any support plane of $\operatorname{CH}(\Lambda H)$ whose exterior disk lies in $D$ has the same contact set with $L$ and hence the same exposed face for $C$. This proves exactly the claimed $D$-facing-piece identification, including the case where $\operatorname{CH}(\Lambda H)$ itself is planar. It makes no claim about the other facing pieces or the geometric finiteness of $H$. [F1, F4, step 1.1, step 2.2, construct, algebra]

4.1 For clarity, metric projection to a closed convex set in $\mathbb H^3$ is nonexpansive. A nearest point exists by ambient properness, and is unique because the midpoint of two different minimizers is strictly closer, as follows from the hyperbolic midpoint cosine formula. If $p,q$ are the projections of $x,y$, minimality along the segment $pq$ makes the angles $xpq$ and $yqp$ at least $\pi/2$, by differentiating the hyperbolic cosine formula for a triangle. Thus $x$ lies in the outer half-space bounded by the plane perpendicular to $pq$ at $p$, and $y$ lies in the outer half-space at $q$. The distance between those two half-spaces is $d(p,q)$: their common perpendicular is $pq$, and perpendicular Fermi coordinates, or the same cosine formula, show that moving either endpoint off this perpendicular increases the distance. Hence $d(p,q)\le d(x,y)$. Applied on every partition of a rectifiable path this also proves length nonincrease. Let $\pi_n$ be projection onto $C_n$. For $x\in S$, $\pi_n(x)$ lies on $S_n$, since $x$ cannot be interior to $C_n\subset C$. Convex-set convergence gives $\pi_n(x)\to x$. Therefore for $x,y\in S$, $$\limsup_n d_{S_n}(\pi_nx,\pi_ny)\le d_S(x,y).$$ Here one projects each path on $S$; its projection lies in $S_n$. Conversely, paths on $S_n$ of uniformly bounded length with endpoints tending to $x,y$ stay in a compact ambient ball. Parametrize them with a common bounded speed. By [F1] a subsequence converges uniformly, and local boundary convergence puts its limit on $S$. The length of the limit is at most the liminf of their lengths, by taking finite partitions. Thus $d_S(x,y)\le\liminf d_{S_n}(\pi_nx,\pi_ny)$, proving equality of the limit. The same conclusion holds for any endpoints on $S_n$ tending to $x,y$: the uniformly Lipschitz graph charts of step 2.1 join those endpoints to $\pi_nx,\pi_ny$ by paths whose lengths tend to zero. [F1, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.1, construct, algebra]

4.2 On the ideal-polyhedron boundary the first-contact map is explicitly length nonincreasing from the $\tau_n$ metric. A face lies in a support plane $P$ and corresponds to its ideal polygon in the round disk bounded by $\partial P$. Normalize that disk to the unit disk. The normal horoball calculation of step 3.3 shows that the first-contact map from that polygon is the hyperbolic isometry to the face. Between adjacent faces of exterior angle $\theta$ the inverse image of their edge is the spherical bigon bounded by their two disks. Normalize the edge to the vertical line from zero to infinity. The bigon has coordinates $z=e^{u+iv}$, $u\in\mathbb R,0\le v\le\theta$. The tangent horoball touches the edge at height $e^u$, so $h_n(z)=|z|$ and $\tau_n|dz|=|du+i\,dv|$, while $r_n$ sends $(u,v)$ to the edge coordinate $u=\log(\text{height})$. This is Euclidean orthogonal projection. Face polygons and these bigons exhaust $D_n$: for any contact point the support normals form exactly the single normal direction in a face or the angular interval at an edge; there is no ordinary vertex. These coordinate formulas give local length nonincrease across seams as well as on each piece, by subdividing a curve or taking limits of polygonal curves. Therefore $\ell_{S_n}(r_n\alpha)\le\ell_{\tau_n}(\alpha)$ for every rectifiable curve in $D_n$. [step 3.1, step 3.3, construct, algebra]

5.1 Fix $p\in S$, an angular coordinate disk $W$ about its direction, and a smaller disk with closure inside $W$. Put $p_n=\pi_n p$. The uniform graph bounds give $b>0$ such that every ambient ball of radius $b$ about $p_n$, for all sufficiently large $n$, meets $S_n$ only in the larger graph patch, and the angular coordinate of that intersection stays inside $W$. Any based loop of intrinsic length less than $b$ stays in that patch and is nullhomotopic in $S_n$, because the whole patch is a disk. Choose $s>0$ with $32s<b$. By [F2] the intrinsic radius-$4s$ ball about $p_n$ has its standard hyperbolic metric, including distances between points of its radius-$s$ subball: a minimizing shortcut has length at most $2s$, stays in the radius-$3s$ ball, and lifts to the same standard disk. Choose pointed isometries of the closed radius-$s$ hyperbolic disk onto these radius-$s$ balls. As maps into $\mathbb H^3$ they are equicontinuous and bounded. A subsequence converges uniformly by [F1]. The distance convergence in step 4.1 shows that the limit preserves all intrinsic distances. It covers an intrinsic neighborhood of $p$: approximate any point at distance less than $s/4$ by its graph-coordinate points on $S_n$; step 4.1 places those points inside the radius-$s/2$ balls for large $n$, and compactness of that parameter disk supplies a preimage in the limit. Thus $S$ is locally isometric to $\mathbb H^2$. Transitions are restrictions of hyperbolic isometries, determined by three noncollinear points, so these local charts give a smooth curvature-minus-one metric with exactly the specified length distance. Its completeness follows as in step 3.1, now using the graph charts of step 1.1 at the ambient limit of a Cauchy sequence. Since $S$ is simply connected by step 1.1, [F2] makes it globally isometric to $\mathbb H^2$. [F1, F2, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.1, step 4.1, construct, algebra]

5.2 Let $\alpha$ be a rectifiable curve in a compact subset of $D$. The convergence in step 3.3 is uniform on its image. Lower semicontinuity of length, proved by finite partitions as in step 4.1, and the intrinsic distance convergence there yield $$\ell_S(r\alpha)\le\liminf_n\ell_{S_n}(r_n\alpha)\le\ell_{\tau}(\alpha).$$ For the second inequality use the uniform convergence of the positive continuous densities $\tau_n$ on that compact set and step 4.2. After moving a point of $L$ to infinity, the Euclidean disk of radius $\delta_D(z)$ centered at $z$ is contained in $D$, and its central hyperbolic density is $2/\delta_D(z)$. Thus $\tau(z)\le2/\delta_D(z)\le4\rho_D(z)$ by [F3]. Integration proves the claimed length inequality. It also shows that the image curve is rectifiable. Every compact-parameter rectifiable curve has compact image in $D$, so this proves the stated assertion. [F3, step 4.1, step 3.3, step 4.2, algebra]

6.1 A group $H$ as in the statement acts on $S$ by orientation-preserving intrinsic isometries. Its action is free because a nonidentity element fixing an ordinary point of $\mathbb H^3$ is elliptic, excluded by discreteness and torsion-freeness; it is proper because the ambient action is proper. Local metric disks therefore give covering projections onto $S/H$, and the metric on that quotient is complete: a Cauchy sequence can be lifted successively in sufficiently small isometric disks, with summable successive distances, to a Cauchy sequence in complete $S$. The map $r$ is equivariant by step 2.2. Since $D$ and $S$ are simply connected, a loop and its lifted endpoint determine the same deck element before and after applying $r$, so $(r_H)_*$ is the identity of $H$ under those identifications. To prove $r_H$ proper, cover a compact subset of $S/H$ by finitely many relatively compact evenly covered disks and choose one compact lifted closure for each. Every point in its inverse image has an $H$-translate whose image under $r$ lies in the union of these compact lifts. The properness in step 3.2 makes their total preimage compact in $D$; its image in $D/H$ contains the desired closed inverse image, which is thus compact. A quotient loop lifts to an arc with deck-related endpoints, and step 5.2 applied to that arc proves the loop length inequality, independently of how far that loop lies toward an end. [F1, step 1.1, step 5.1, step 2.2, step 3.2, step 5.2, construct, algebra]

7.1 Finally, the interior condition does not suppress a non-elementary group case. If the hull has empty interior, $L$ lies in a round circle, by the Klein affine-span criterion. The complement of a simply connected domain is connected: if it split into nonempty disjoint compact sets $A,B$, their distance in a plane chart is positive; take a sufficiently fine finite grid, the cells meeting $A$, and one further layer of cells, all missing $B$. Its polygonal frontier lies in $D$ and can be resolved at its finitely many touching vertices into disjoint polygonal circles. The winding number of the union is one at a point of $A$ and zero at a point of $B$, so at least one frontier circle separates complementary points and cannot contract in $D$, by [F4]. A connected closed proper subset of a circle is an arc or a point, or the whole circle. A point, or two points, would give a nonhyperbolic or nonsimply connected domain; the whole circle has disconnected complement. Thus the only case here is a nontrivial proper closed arc. Its two endpoints are characterized intrinsically as endpoints of that arc, so every preserving group preserves their two-element set and is elementary. Its planar hyperbolic hull is a half-plane, and doubling across its bounding geodesic gives $\mathbb H^2$, the usual folded-dome interpretation. The preceding conclusions for a supplied non-elementary group therefore always use the genuine three-dimensional interior case. The elementary branch is therefore separate from the non-elementary finite-area conclusion below. [F4, step 1.1, step 5.1, step 6.1, construct, algebra]

7.2 Suppose now that $X=D/H$ has the supplied compact standard cusp truncation. In a puncture coordinate the cusp metric is $|dw|/(|w|\log(R/|w|))$, so its once-around based circles have length tending uniformly to zero outside deeper truncations. For any $\varepsilon>0$ choose a compact truncation $K_\varepsilon\subset X$ so that every point outside it has a nontrivial based loop of length less than $\varepsilon/4$. By step 6.1 the image loop is nontrivial and by step 5.2 has length less than $\varepsilon$. Surjectivity therefore puts the $\varepsilon$-thick part of $Y=S/H$ inside $r_H(K_\varepsilon)$: if $y$ is outside that compact image, every preimage lies outside $K_\varepsilon$ and supplies such a short nontrivial loop at $y$. The thick part is closed and hence compact. Apply the actual local-thin description in [F4] to the intrinsic disk group of $Y$, viewed as the real Möbius subgroup of the complex group, and choose $\varepsilon$ smaller than its universal threshold. Its two-dimensional thin components are exactly horodisks modulo primitive parabolic translations and finite-width strips about axes modulo primitive hyperbolic translations. This follows directly by intersecting the displacement regions with the preserved hyperbolic plane: $d(z,z+\ell)$ depends only on height in the parabolic case, and $\sinh(d(z,gz)/2)=\cosh(\operatorname{dist}(z,\operatorname{axis}g))\sinh(\ell(g)/2)$ in the hyperbolic case. Different components have different elementary stabilizers, as in that supplier. Every component meets the thick part on its boundary, unless the whole connected surface is thin; in that event lifting the single thin component shows that its entire disk group is elementary, contrary to the non-elementary $H$ and the identity of fundamental groups in step 6.1. There are only finitely many components meeting the compact thick part. Indeed in a small disk about any lifted thick point only finitely many deck transformations can move that disk by at most $2\varepsilon$ plus its diameter, by properness; each neighboring thin component contains a displacement region of one of these transformations. A finite cover of the compact thick part by such disks bounds their number. Each closed hyperbolic collar is compact by its finite width and closed core; each parabolic piece is a standard finite-area cusp. Adding the finitely many collars to the compact thick part gives a compact standard cusp truncation of $Y$. Its smooth compact part has finite area, and every cusp has area equal to the finite length of its truncating horocycle by integrating $dx\,dy/y^2$. Thus $Y$ has finite area. This uses the actual surjective short-loop carrier, not an assumed area comparison for a merely Lipschitz map. [F4, step 5.2, step 6.1, given, construct, algebra]

8.1 The cusp marking is exact. By properness of $r_H$, the inverse image of a compact cusp truncation of $Y$ is compact in $X$. Hence each sufficiently far-out connected cusp tail of $X$ maps entirely into a single cusp of $Y$. Its once-around primitive element has zero intrinsic translation length in $Y$, by the arbitrarily short image loops from step 7.2, so is parabolic there. Two different cusp ends of $X$ cannot map to the same cusp. Choose tracks from the fixed basepoint into their tails. The two image primitive classes then belong initially to conjugates of that same cyclic cusp subgroup of $Y$; conjugate the second class by the element specified by those tracks so that both lie in the same subgroup. They commute there. Pull that conjugation and the commutation relation back through the fundamental-group isomorphism of step 6.1. The corresponding primitive parabolics of the disk group of $X$ now commute, hence have the same parabolic fixed point by [F4]. Their original fixed points therefore lie in the same deck-group orbit, so they describe the same cusp of $X$, a contradiction. This argument uses conjugacy classes of peripheral subgroups and does not identify distinct basepoint tracks. Every cusp of $Y$ occurs. Choose points escaping along that cusp and preimages under the surjective map; the preimages leave every compact subset of $X$, because the image of any compact subset is compact. A subsequence therefore leaves through one of the finitely many cusps of $X$. The proper-end conclusion just proved forces that end to map to the selected cusp of $Y$. Finally a primitive cusp element of $X$ cannot become a proper power of the primitive parabolic of $Y$: its root would pull back through the group isomorphism to a root of that primitive parabolic of $X$. In a discrete torsion-free disk group every root of a parabolic is a parabolic with the same fixed point and belongs to its primitive cyclic stabilizer, by [F4], ruling out that proper power. The cyclic subgroups and their primitive generators are thus identified up to basepoint conjugation and choice of generator orientation. No result here controls the remaining boundary pieces of the three-dimensional convex hull. [F4, step 6.1, step 7.2, construct, algebra] ∎
