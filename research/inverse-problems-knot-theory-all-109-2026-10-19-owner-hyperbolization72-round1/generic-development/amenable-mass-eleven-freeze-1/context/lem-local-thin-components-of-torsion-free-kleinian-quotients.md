## Statement

Assume AC. There is a universal $\eta_*>0$ such that, for every discrete torsion-free $\Gamma\le\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb C)$ and every $x\in\mathbb H^3$, the subgroup generated by $\{g\in\Gamma:d(x,gx)\le\eta_*\}$ is trivial or abelian elementary. Its nontrivial case is a translation lattice of rank one or two fixing one ideal point, or an infinite cyclic loxodromic group fixing an ideal pair.

For every $0<\eta<\eta_*$ put $U_\eta=\bigcup_{g\ne1}\{x:d(x,gx)<\eta\}$. Its nonempty connected components are horoballs, with full parabolic stabilizer a translation lattice of rank one or two, or finite-radius tubes about complete geodesics, with full stabilizer infinite cyclic loxodromic. Every component is precisely invariant under that stabilizer. Distinct closed components have distance at least $(\eta_*-\eta)/2$, and the family of closed components is locally finite in $\mathbb H^3$. Their closed stabilizer quotients embed in $M=\mathbb H^3/\Gamma$. The closed tube quotients are compact; the horoball quotients retain their indicated translation ranks. The complement of the open thin set in $M$ is the closed set of injectivity radius at least $\eta/2$.

The quotient $M$ is a complete proper Riemannian manifold. No finite generation, finite volume or geometric finiteness is assumed. The number of thin components or cusp orbits need not be finite.

## Proof

1.1 We first prove the uniform small-matrix assertion. Near identity choose the determinant-one lift near $I$ and let $\rho(g)=\max(\|g-I\|,\|g^{-1}-I\|)$ for a fixed matrix operator norm. For small symmetric neighborhoods $V_r=\{\rho<r\}$ the closure is compact. If $g=I+X,h=I+Y$, then $[g,h]-I=(XY-YX)g^{-1}h^{-1}$; the reversed formula bounds its inverse. Thus $\rho([g,h])\le C\rho(g)\rho(h)$ for one fixed $C$ on a sufficiently small neighborhood. Choose $r$ with $Cr<1/2$. For a discrete subgroup $D$, every left-normed commutator of $j$ elements of $S=D\cap V_r$ has norm at most $r2^{-(j-1)}$ and stays in $V_r$. Since identity is isolated in $D$, all commutators of one sufficiently large length vanish, uniformly in the choices of entries from $S$. The subgroup $H=\langle S\rangle$ is nilpotent. Indeed the simple commutators of one shorter length commute with every element of $S$, hence generate a central subgroup; in its quotient the shorter commutators vanish, and induction on the length bound gives a central series. The commutator identities needed for passing from generators to their products follow by expanding $[ab,c]=a[b,c]a^{-1}[a,c]$ and $[a,bc]=[a,b]b[a,c]b^{-1}$; they show at each induction stage that the displayed generating commutators suffice. A discrete subgroup is closed, since an accumulation of distinct elements would give their pairwise quotients tending to identity, so its intersection with the compact closure of $V_r$ is finite. [A1, F1, given, construct, algebra]

2.1 We pass to displacement uniformly. Fix $o$ and a compact neighborhood $Q$ of its compact stabilizer $K$. Choose an identity neighborhood $V$ with $qVq^{-1}\subset V_r$ for all $q\in Q$, by uniform continuity on $Q$. Cover $K$ by finitely many open sets $W_1,\ldots,W_N\subset Q$ such that $W_j^{-1}W_j\subset V$. There is $\eta_0>0$ such that $d(o,go)<\eta_0$ implies $g\in Q\cap\bigcup_jW_j$. For otherwise isometries of displacement tending to zero have a subsequence converging into $K$: the inverse image of a compact model ball under the frame projection is compact by its local trivializations and compact fiber. Set $\eta_*<\eta_0/(N+1)$. Let $D$ be generated by the elements of a discrete torsion-free subgroup moving $o$ by at most $\eta_*$. This is a finite symmetric generating set, again by compactness of that inverse image. Let $H=\langle D\cap V_r\rangle$, nilpotent by step 1.1. In a word of length greater than $N$, the first $N+1$ prefixes $w_0,\ldots,w_N$ move $o$ by less than $\eta_0$, so two, $w_i,w_j$ with $i<j$, lie in one $W_l$. Hence $h=w_i^{-1}w_j\in V\cap D$ and $w_ihw_i^{-1}\in V_r\cap D\subset H$. Deleting the intervening block gives a shorter word in the same left coset of $H$: if the remaining suffix is $v$, then $w_jv=(w_ihw_i^{-1})w_iv$. Repetition gives a representative of length at most $N$ for every coset. There are finitely many such words, so $[D:H]<\infty$. Homogeneity transports the same $\eta_*$ to every model point. [F1, step 1.1, construct, algebra]

3.1 We classify this virtually nilpotent group without using any volume. No nonidentity element of a discrete torsion-free Möbius group is elliptic: diagonal eigenvalues of modulus one give either torsion or powers accumulating at identity. The other eigenvalue cases are a nontrivial unipotent, with one parabolic fixed point, and a diagonal matrix of nonunit eigenvalue modulus, with a loxodromic pair. If $D$ is finite it is trivial. Otherwise the normal core $H_0$ of $H$ has finite index in $D$, is nontrivial and nilpotent. Choose a nontrivial central element $z$ of $H_0$. If $z$ is parabolic its centralizer consists of translations fixing its unique point, by direct affine commutation with $w\mapsto w+1$. If it is loxodromic its centralizer consists of diagonal maps fixing its eigenlines individually. Thus $H_0$ is abelian and has one common ideal point or one common ideal pair. Normality makes $D$ preserve that same fixed set. A pair-interchanging Möbius map has order two, as the formula $w\mapsto a/w$ shows, so $D$ preserves each endpoint in the pair case. A discrete torsion-free diagonal subgroup is cyclic: $w\mapsto\log|w|$ has trivial kernel on it, compact annuli contain only finitely many subgroup elements, and division by its smallest positive value gives the cyclic assertion. In the one-point case, write maps as $w\mapsto aw+b$. A nonunit $|a|$ would conjugate the nontrivial translation in $H_0$ to translations tending to identity; a unit $a\ne1$ is elliptic. Hence $a=1$ and $D$ consists of translations. A discrete additive subgroup of $\mathbb C$ is rank one or two: choose a shortest nonzero vector, reduce the parallel coordinate modulo it, and, if noncollinear vectors exist, choose the least positive perpendicular coordinate in a bounded rectangle. Division with remainder in these two directions generates the whole group. This proves the first conclusion of the Statement. [step 1.1, step 2.1, given, construct, algebra]

4.1 The displacement region of a translation vector $v$ is a horoball, since $\sinh(d((z,Y),(z+v,Y))/2)=|v|/(2Y)$. For a loxodromic screw motion with translation length $L>0$ and rotation angle $\alpha$, at radial distance $r$ from its axis one has $\sinh^2(d(x,gx)/2)=\sinh^2(L/2)\cosh^2r+\sin^2(\alpha/2)\sinh^2r$. This follows by using hyperboloid coordinates $(\cosh r\cosh t,\cosh r\sinh t,\sinh r\cos\theta,\sinh r\sin\theta)$ in [F1] and shifting $(t,\theta)$ by $(L,\alpha)$. Thus each nonempty displacement region is a connected axis tube of finite radius. If two displacement regions meet, their elements lie in the group from step 3.1 at that point, and have the same parabolic fixed point or the same loxodromic pair. This equality propagates along any intersection chain, so each thin component has one fixed set. The full group preserving that fixed set is a translation lattice or a cyclic loxodromic group, by exactly the affine and diagonal arguments in step 3.1. Conversely all the nonempty displacement regions for elements of this full group are concentric horoballs or axis tubes, hence belong to the same component. In the parabolic case their union is the horoball for the shortest nonzero translation vector. In the cyclic loxodromic case only finitely many powers can satisfy $|n|L<\eta$; their tube radii have a finite maximum, giving a finite-radius tube. Equivariance now proves precise invariance under the full fixed-set stabilizer. [F1, step 3.1, construct, algebra]

5.1 We verify separation, including the closed boundaries. If $x$ lies in the closure of the $g$-displacement region at threshold $\eta$, and $y$ in that of $h$, then $d(x,gx)\le\eta$ and $d(x,hx)\le\eta+2d(x,y)$. If $d(x,y)<(\eta_*-\eta)/2$, both displacements are at most $\eta_*$, so step 3.1 forces the same fixed set. Thus closures of distinct components are separated by at least that constant. Their family is locally finite: any displacement region meeting a ball of radius $a$ about $x$ has $d(x,gx)\le\eta+2a$, and only finitely many subgroup matrices have that bounded displacement, by the compact frame fiber in step 2.1. This also applies to the closed regions. The quotient of a closed component embeds, since two lifts identified by $\Gamma$ must lie in the same closed component by the separation just proved, hence differ by its stabilizer. Local separation gives quotient-sheet neighborhoods, so it is a local embedding including the boundary. It is proper: lift a compact quotient neighborhood to finitely many compact model chart closures. Only finitely many translates of the closed component meet those closures, by local finiteness; moving these finitely many intersections back to the original component gives a compact set of representatives modulo its stabilizer. Thus the inverse image of a compact set in that stabilizer quotient is compact. In particular the injective local embedding is an embedding. The closed tube quotient is compact directly: use a bounded radial interval, angular circle and one closed axis interval of length $L$, with its endpoints identified by the screw rotation. [F1, step 2.1, step 3.1, step 4.1, construct, algebra]

6.1 Finally the quotient is a complete proper Riemannian manifold. The action is free and proper, by the compact-stabilizer argument of step 2.1 and the elliptic exclusion in step 3.1, so its charts are locally isometric covering charts. Quotient distance is $d_M(\pi x,\pi y)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}d(x,\gamma y)$: projection gives one inequality, and lifting a competing path gives the other. The infimum is attained, since only finitely many orbit points lie in a fixed compact model ball. Consequently every closed radius-$R$ quotient ball is the image of a compact closed model ball of radius $R$. It is compact, giving properness and hence metric completeness (a Cauchy sequence lies in one compact ball and converges there). The injectivity radius at $\pi x$ is half the infimum of nontrivial displacements: model balls smaller than half that infimum have disjoint deck translates, and their projections are isometric embedded balls; a path to a nontrivial translate of $x$ gives the reverse bound. Thus the complement of the open thin part is precisely the asserted closed thick part. Its injectivity radius is a $1$-Lipschitz function, since each half-displacement is $1$-Lipschitz and taking infima preserves that inequality. All conclusions are independent of total volume. [F1, step 2.1, step 3.1, step 4.1, step 5.1, construct, algebra] ∎
