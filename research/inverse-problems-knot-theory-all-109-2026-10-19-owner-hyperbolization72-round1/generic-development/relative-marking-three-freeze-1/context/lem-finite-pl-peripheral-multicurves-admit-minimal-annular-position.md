## Statement

Assume full AC. Let $\Sigma$ be a compact oriented closed finite PL surface, possibly disconnected. Let $P\subset\Sigma$ be a finite disjoint union of embedded parametrized PL annuli with essential core circles and whole torus components of $\Sigma$. Let $C\subset\Sigma$ be a finite disjoint union of essential embedded PL circles. Then an ambient PL isotopy of $\Sigma$ puts $C$ in a position with the following properties.

Every component of $C$ isotopic to a boundary circle of $P$ lies in $\operatorname{int}P$. Subject to that condition, $C$ is transverse to $\partial P$ and has the least possible number of intersection components $C\cap P$ among such positions. In each annular component of $P$, the intersections are either disjoint essential core circles or disjoint proper spanning arcs, or empty. No component has both kinds. The isotopy extends over a collar of $\Sigma$ in any supplied PL three-manifold with boundary $\Sigma$.

For completeness, essential freely homotopic circles on an oriented finite PL surface bound an annulus when disjoint, and when transverse and intersecting they have an embedded bigon. Their ambient isotopy and the finite-family position asserted here are proved below, rather than presumed from their homotopy classes. Minimization is constrained by the parallel-circle placement; unconstrained minimization need not put a parallel circle inside $P$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The compact finite oriented PL surface and the two finite families in the Statement, with the stated choice hypothesis.

[F1] Nullhomotopic simple circles on finite oriented PL surfaces bound parametrized PL disk regions. Boundary circles of a nondisk finite oriented PL surface generate injective infinite cyclic subgroups. Its proof also supplies a finite graph spine for a compact surface with boundary. Closed oriented surfaces have the stated finite genus models. [[lem-nullhomotopic-circles-on-finite-oriented-pl-surfaces-bound-pl-disks]] [[thm-classification-of-compact-connected-surfaces]]

[F2] Universal covers exist for locally simply connected surfaces; quotient covers realize subgroups; homotopies lift and covering groups inject. The universal deck group acts freely and transitively on each fiber. [[thm-universal-cover-existence]] [[lem-subgroup-quotient-of-universal-cover]] [[thm-deck-group-of-a-universal-cover-is-the-fundamental-group]] [[thm-homotopy-lifting-for-covering-maps]] [[thm-covering-maps-inject-fundamental-groups]]

[F3] Van Kampen identifies groups of surface gluings, and injective cyclic boundary amalgams have injective factors by normal form, with the permitted choice of transversals. [[thm-seifert-van-kampen]] [[thm-normal-form-for-free-products-with-amalgamation]]

[F4] Polygonal disks, strips, annuli assembled from them and prescribed boundary maps have finite PL parameters. Compact locally collared PL faces have collars. [[lem-finite-polygonal-disk-and-collar-surgery]] [[lem-compact-locally-collared-pl-subpolyhedron-has-pl-collar]]

## Proof

1.1 An essential simple circle $a$ in an oriented surface has infinite order. If it is nonseparating, join its two sides in the cut surface and close across one small segment; signed intersections with this transverse circle define an integer cocycle evaluating once on $a$. If it separates, its two sides are nondisk surfaces, since otherwise $a$ is inessential. Their boundary cyclic groups inject by [F1], and [F3]'s amalgam normal form injects that infinite cyclic subgroup into the whole surface. All nullhomotopies under discussion have compact image; in a locally finite triangulated surface they lie in a compact finite triangulated subsurface. Thus [F1]'s disk conclusion also holds in the noncompact covering surfaces used below. The same finite-subsurface argument proves infinite order of a compact boundary component of a connected noncompact oriented surface: a nullhomotopy of one of its powers would lie in a compact subsurface with that boundary, which cannot be a disk unless the entire boundary-side component were that compact disk. [F1, F3, given, construct]

2.1 First prove the disjoint-circle assertion. Let essential freely homotopic disjoint circles $a,b$ lie in a compact oriented subsurface containing their homotopy. Remove the interior of a collar $a\times[-1,1]$ and cap its two boundary circles by disks, with centers $p_-,p_+$. In the separating case take the capped component containing $b$; define a map from the original surface to it by collapsing the other capped side to the appropriate center. In the nonseparating case both caps are in one component; on the removed collar map each circle fiber to a path from $p_-$ to $p_+$, and outside the collar use the cap-collapse map. This is continuous because the collar-end circles collapse to the path endpoints. In both cases $a$ maps to a point and $b$ remains a simple circle, now nullhomotopic by the given homotopy. It bounds a disk by [F1]. This disk contains one cap center, both, or neither. No cap gives a disk bounded by $b$ in the original surface, impossible. One cap gives a parametrized annulus between $a$ and $b$. Both caps can occur only in the nonseparating case, and would give a once-punctured torus bounded by $b$ with $a$ nonseparating inside it. Then $b$ is zero in mod-two homology of the original surface, while a transverse circle through that punctured torus detects $a$ once modulo two, contradicting their homotopy. Thus the annulus exists. The disk/cone constructions in [F1,F4] give its finite PL parameter. [F1, F4, step 1.1, given, construct]

3.1 We need two elementary facts about noncompact covering surfaces, so prove their compact-core form. In a connected noncompact triangulated oriented surface $Y$, take a compact set and finitely many loops. A finite connected regular neighborhood of these data is a compact subsurface $K$: take small vertex disks and thin edge bands in a finite subdivision containing the data, add the required triangle interiors, and round their finite corner fans. Fill its compact complementary components. Whenever a noncompact complementary component meets two boundary circles of $K$, join these by an embedded arc in that component and add its thin band to $K$; any newly separated compact complementary component is filled. Each band reduces the total number of interfaces, so finitely many suffice. Every remaining complementary component is noncompact with one boundary interface. That interface has infinite cyclic injection into both sides unless $K$ is a disk, by step1.1 and [F1]. Van Kampen and normal form, successively over this finite star of complementary components, inject $\pi_1K$ into $\pi_1Y$; the disk case injects trivially. Thus if $\pi_1Y=0$, every compact set lies in a disk, because the graph spine of $K$ has rank zero. If $\pi_1Y=\mathbb Z$ and the data include a generator, every compact set lies in a compact annulus injecting isomorphically on groups: the graph spine has rank one, and pruning leaves reduces it to a cycle, whose oriented thickening is an annulus. [F1, F3, F4, step 1.1, step 2.1, given, construct]

4.1 The locally finite triangulations of covers of a finite surface are countable: the surface group is finitely generated by its finite edge loops, so a fiber is indexed by a quotient of a countable word set. Choose nested compact cores containing successive finite stars of that triangulation and the preceding core. In the simply connected case the cores are nested disks; their successive differences are annuli by cutting the outer polygonal disk around its inner parametrized disk and using [F4] to match their two collars, so compatible concentric disk parameters identify $Y$ with the plane. In the infinite cyclic case they are annuli and their two successive end regions are annuli by step2.1, giving $Y\cong S^1\times\mathbb R$. All parameters on a fixed compact set use finitely many of these PL annuli. The universal cover of a closed oriented surface admitting an essential circle is noncompact: a compact simply connected oriented surface is a sphere by [F1], whose Euler characteristic would be a positive multiple of the base characteristic, impossible for a torus or higher-genus base. The cyclic cover corresponding to $\langle a\rangle$ is also noncompact: a compact such cover would be a closed oriented surface with first homology rank one, contrary to its genus model. By [F2] their groups are respectively trivial and infinite cyclic. Thus the preceding plane and open-annulus descriptions apply to these particular covers. [F1, F2, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.1, given, construct]

5.1 Disk descent will be used only for these covers. If a disk $D$ in a covering surface projects injectively on its boundary, lift it to the universal plane of step4.1. Distinct deck translates of its boundary are disjoint. If two of its translated closed disks overlap, planar disk separation makes them nested or equal. Equality contradicts injectivity of the projected boundary, since a deck transformation preserving that boundary must fix each of its points. A finite strict nesting cycle is impossible; strict nesting gives infinitely many powers of one deck transformation whose translates meet the original compact disk. This is impossible: in the lifted locally finite cell system a compact disk meets finitely many cells; a deck transformation taking one such cell to another is unique, since it is determined on one point of that lift. Hence only finitely many deck translates can meet that disk. The lifted disk is therefore disjoint from every nonidentity translate and projects injectively. Here the deck group is the group of the universal cover over the original base; the lifted disk exists by [F2] since its domain is simply connected. This proves the claimed disk descent directly to that base. [F1, F2, step 4.1, given, construct]

6.1 Let transverse freely homotopic essential circles $a,b$ intersect. In the cyclic open-annulus cover for $\langle a\rangle$, their homotopy lifts to essential embedded closed cores $a_0,b_0$, each projecting homeomorphically to its base circle. There is an arc of a lift of $b$ with its two ends on $a_0$, bounding a disk with a subarc of $a_0$. To see existence, if $b_0$ meets $a_0$, a consecutive excursion of $b_0$ into either side of the separating core returns to it and is boundary parallel on that half-annulus. If $b_0$ misses $a_0$ but another lift meets it, that lift is a proper line or a circle disjoint from the separating core $b_0$. A proper line cannot join the two annulus ends while avoiding $b_0$; its excursions across $a_0$ therefore include a returning one. A proper arc returning to the boundary of a half-annulus cuts off a disk: place its compact image in a finite annulus and cut that annulus along the arc, or lift to its strip and use polygonal disk separation. This gives the desired disk. [F1, F2, F4, step 4.1, step 5.1, given, construct]

7.1 Choose such a disk innermost with respect to all lifts of $b$. Every other lifted $b$ arc in it has both ends on its $a_0$ shore; no closed lifted $b$ circle can lie inside, by infinite order. Thus an innermost one gives a disk with interior disjoint from all lifts of $b$. Next choose an innermost lifted $a$ arc in that disk; the resulting subdisk $E$ has two shores on lifts of $a,b$, and its interior misses both full lifted families. Each shore projects injectively: a wrap around its base circle would contain another base intersection with the other circle, contrary to the empty-shore choice. There are at least two base intersections, because their mod-two intersection is zero: a parallel copy of $a$ misses $a$, and the crossing cocycle evaluates equally on homotopic $a,b$. Thus the two projected corners cannot coincide either. The boundary of $E$ projects as an embedded bigon, and step5.1 makes its disk projection injective. This proves the bigon assertion. Pushing one shore across an empty inner bigon and slightly past the other removes two crossings; after finitely many such pushes the circles are disjoint, and step2.1 slides one through their annulus. These disk and annulus pushes, using [F4]'s collars, are ambient PL isotopies. For a compact surface with boundary, double it first. The fold retraction of the double onto either copy injects that copy on fundamental groups; each seam circle therefore injects by [F1], since a component with an essential circle is not a disk. A bigon whose shores lie in the original interior cannot contain an entire essential seam circle, so its disk stays in that original side. This gives the same assertion there, with interior support. [F1, F2, F3, F4, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 5.1, step 6.1, given, construct]

8.1 Choose a core $d_i$ of each annular component of $P$, and write $D$ for their disjoint family. Among transverse isotopic positions of the entire $C$ family choose one minimizing $|C\cap D|$; the attainable set is a nonempty set of finite nonnegative integers. A bigon between any component of each family contains an empty bigon for the two whole families. Indeed extra $C$ arcs enter only through its $D$ shore and extra $D$ arcs only through its $C$ shore. Taking an innermost such arc gives a smaller disk; alternate until the finite number of arcs inside drops to zero. There is no closed family component in a disk because all are essential. An empty bigon push contradicts minimality. By step7.1, any $c\subset C$ parallel to a $d_i$ therefore misses that $d_i$. It misses every other $d_j$ too: in the annulus between $c$ and $d_i$, a $d_j$ cannot cross $d_i$, so any entering arc returns to $c$ and cuts off a bigon; an entire $d_j$ contained there is parallel and was already handled. Thus no such intersection is possible at the minimum. [F4, step 2.1, step 7.1, given, construct]

9.1 First a nonperipheral $C$ component also misses each $D$ core whose class contains a peripheral $C$ circle $c$. In the annulus between that core and $c$, such a component cannot cross $c$, so every entering arc returns to the core and gives a bigon, excluded by step8.1. Group the parallel components of $C\cup D$ by their unoriented isotopy class. For any group containing a $D$ core, successive disjoint circles bound annuli by step2.1 and form one finite annular chain; on a torus this chain can be cyclic. Its annular regions contain no nonparallel component of $C\cup D$. Such a component wholly inside an annulus would itself be parallel; an entering arc must return to the same boundary because it cannot cross the other parallel family, and would give the forbidden empty-bigon reduction of step8.1. Thus the chains for distinct relevant classes can have disjoint collars and contain no other $C$ curves. Parametrize each chain with its circle levels. In each interval between consecutive $D$ levels, move its finitely many $C$ levels by an increasing PL interval isotopy into a small neighborhood of one of those $D$ levels lying in $\operatorname{int}P$. At the outside ends use the same move towards the nearest $D$ level; in a cyclic chain leave one small interval free. Fix the $D$ levels and the outer chain boundary. These interval isotopies preserve the order of the finite $C$ levels and extend as products on the annuli. They move all peripheral $C$ circles into $\operatorname{int}P$, without moving the other $C$ components. All choices of circle levels can be made on the actual finite annular pieces, and their boundary parameters match by [F4]. [F4, step 2.1, step 8.1, given, construct]

10.1 Now minimize the number of components of $C\cap P$ among transverse positions satisfying this peripheral placement. The set is nonempty by step9.1, so its least integer is attained. Suppose an intersection arc in an annulus of $P$ has both endpoints on the same boundary circle. It cuts off a disk; choose an innermost such arc, whose disk contains no other $C$ arc. It contains no closed $C$ circle, since those are essential in $\Sigma$. Pushing across the disk to just outside $P$ removes this return arc and decreases the number of intersection components by one. Its support misses every closed peripheral circle inside $P$, so the constrained placement is preserved. This contradicts minimality. Every remaining arc therefore spans its annulus. A closed component inside that annulus is an essential core because it is essential in $\Sigma$; a spanning arc cannot be disjoint from a core, as cutting along a spanning arc gives a disk. Thus the claimed alternatives hold. [F1, F4, step 9.1, given, construct]

11.1 Every component of $C$ isotopic to a boundary of $P$ was included in one of the core classes, since an annulus boundary is isotopic to its core. Whole torus components require no further boundary placement. The minimization in step10.1 is explicitly after this condition, and so does not confuse zero crossings outside an annulus with placement inside it. Finally each elementary surface isotopy extends over $\Sigma\times[0,\varepsilon]$ by using the time parameter $\min(t,\tau(r))$, where $\tau$ is a PL collar cutoff equal to one on the outer face and zero on the inner face. This minimum is PL, so composing it with the PL isotopy and using finite prism subdivisions gives a PL extension, a homeomorphism on each collar level at each time. Compose the finitely many isotopies used to reach the chosen positions; existential minima designate actual positions in the isotopy orbit, not a limiting process. This proves the boundary-collar assertion and all stated conclusions. [F4, step 7.1, step 9.1, step 10.1, given, construct] ∎

## Scope

This is boundary multicurve placement. It does not assert a relative three-manifold marking theorem or the full pared axioms after cutting a three-manifold. Its sphere and cyclic-cover arguments concern already triangulated surfaces and introduce no arbitrary-manifold triangulation theorem.
