## Concentration at a prescribed thin threshold

Work in the actual supplied geometric boundary model of the frozen theorem `thm-near-isometric-skinning-coderivatives-concentrate-on-geodesic-lifted-parts`, with its actual spot covering maps. For a covering $p:U\to X$, write $L_r(p)$ for the union of its degree-one lifts of the geodesic $r$-thin annuli and of the closed complementary regions. These complementary regions include their attached cusp ends. All thresholds below are smaller than the common thin-component constant.

We first prove $L_e(p)\subset L_r(p)$ whenever $0<e<r$. A degree-one lift of an $e$-thin annulus extends over its enclosing $r$-thin annulus: the same primitive core generator fixes the covering sheet, which is exactly the degree-one criterion for either annulus. Now suppose $y$ belongs to a degree-one lift of a complementary $e$-region $V_e$. If $p(y)$ lies outside the open geodesic $r$-thin part, its complementary $r$-region $V_r$ is a subset of $V_e$. Restricting the inverse section on $V_e$ gives a whole degree-one lift of $V_r$ through $y$.

Otherwise $p(y)$ is in an $r$-thin annulus $A$. The constant-Fermi-radius circle through $p(y)$ lies entirely in $V_e$. Indeed, if the core of $A$ was already $e$-short, the old thin annulus is its middle band and $p(y)$ is outside that band; if it was not $e$-short, the entire annulus $A$ avoids every old geodesic thin annulus. Thin annuli belonging to different core curves are disjoint. This circle represents the primitive generator of $A$, and its lift through $y$ is closed because the section over $V_e$ is single-valued. Consequently the component of $p^{-1}(A)$ through $y$ has degree one. The same argument applies on a boundary level circle. This proves the inclusion, including points in attached cusp ends.

Fix now a threshold $r>0$ before choosing an error $h>0$. Choose $e<\min(r,\varepsilon_h)$, where $\varepsilon_h$ is supplied by the frozen concentration theorem, and take its corresponding $\delta(h,e)>0$. For every unit opposite quadratic $q$ satisfying $\|Tq\|_1>1-\delta(h,e)$, that theorem and the inclusion just proved give
$$
 \int_{\bigsqcup L_r(p)}|q|
 \;\ge\;\int_{\bigsqcup L_e(p)}|q|\;>1-h.
$$
Thus arbitrary concentration error is available at the *previously prescribed* threshold $r$. This conclusion does not require interchanging the quantifiers of the frozen theorem.

The one-step curve/mass argument also applies at this same prescribed $r$. Every non-degree-one preimage of a base $r$-thin annulus is disjoint from $L_r$, so all such omitted preimages together have mass less than $h$. The primitive loxodromic image argument in the frozen proof is independent of the concentration threshold. The actual annulus-propagation supplier therefore applies with this omitted-mass bound and with the original $r$-annulus. In particular one may first choose $r$ for the finite-iterate quasiconformal length comparison, next obtain a positive mass bound $\zeta(r)$, and only then choose $h<\zeta(r)/(C(2S')^K)$ and its near-isometry threshold. The error choice does not force a new thin threshold or a new value of $\zeta$.

Repetition of an unoriented class alone is insufficient: the actual transverse gluing word must be cyclically reduced, and an orientation-reversing return must be squared before constructing the nonperipheral torus map.

## The transverse word retained by actual spots

Its graph of groups has these piece groups as vertex groups and the groups of the labeled gluing boundary components as edge groups. Concatenate these annulus maps with the gluing collars along a finite returned curve sequence. There is one positive-length graph edge crossing at every gluing, while the intervening vertex coefficient is the retained transverse conjugator, with the fixed boundary-path gauges. A potential cancellation is possible only when two successive gluing edges are inverses and the intervening annulus has both ends on the *same labeled boundary component*. In that case $a=b$. Membership outside that edge subgroup is unchanged by the two boundary-path gauges and by multiplying at either end by boundary-group elements. Hence this candidate cancellation is forbidden by graph-of-groups normal form. When the two boundary labels differ, the edges are not inverses in the first place. Consequently a returned sequence supplies a cyclically reduced graph-of-groups word $w$ with positive edge length. If $u$ is an oriented primitive generator on the returned curve, transporting the actual generators gives $wuw^{-1}=u^\epsilon$, where $\epsilon=1$ or $-1$. Replace $w$ by $w^2$ in the second case. The replacement is still cyclically reduced and has positive edge length. The generator $u$ has infinite order by the injective boundary group and the actual loxodromic core correspondence.

For completeness, the normal-form tree proves that the resulting commuting pair generates an injected $\mathbb Z^2$. The cyclically reduced word translates its tree axis by its positive edge length. The element $u$ fixes the initial boundary edge and, by commutation, all its translates along that axis; its fixed subtree contains the entire axis. If $u^m w^n=1$, translation on the axis forces $n=0$, and infinite order then forces $m=0$. By the actual end-preserving boundary marking, it is parabolic in a neighboring geometric piece exactly when its surface class is peripheral. This proves nonperipherality in the boundary-injective, peripherally marked setting. An application outside that setting must first supply its corresponding peripheral subtree and type-preservation argument; no vertex-carried shortcut is available.

## Rigidity needed for the round-packing construction

Here is the reflection-specific rigidity argument, once the packing reflection groups and their chambers have been constructed. It avoids importing rigidity for arbitrary recurrent Kleinian groups. Suppose a discrete group generated by hyperbolic plane reflections has a convex fundamental chamber $C$ with countable ideal boundary, and a quasiconformal sphere homeomorphism $h$ conjugates it to a Möbius/anti-Möbius group. Every mirror circle is taken to a round circle: it is the boundary fixed set of a reflection, and conjugacy takes that fixed set to the fixed set of its conjugate.

Fix a basepoint $o$ off the mirrors. The mirror planes are locally finite. Indeed a reflection in a plane at distance at most $R$ from $o$ moves $o$ by at most $2R$; discreteness and compactness of the point stabilizer imply that there are only finitely many group elements with this displacement bound. A geodesic ray from $o$ with endpoint outside the countable set $\bigcup_g g(\partial_\infty C)$ must cross infinitely many mirrors. Otherwise its tail stays in a chamber and its endpoint belongs to that chamber's ideal boundary. Each plane is crossed at most once. The distances of these crossed planes from $o$ therefore tend to infinity. The closed round cap on the side away from $o$ contains the ray's endpoint, and its spherical diameter tends to zero.

Work at a finite chart point $x$ where $h$ is differentiable with invertible real derivative $A$. Such points have full measure: total differentiability is proved in the actual quadrilateral-modulus core, and the area equality together with the inverse null-set property excludes the zero-Jacobian set for a quasiconformal homeomorphism. Write the shrinking cap boundary circles as $c_j+r_jS^1$. Since the caps contain $x$, $|c_j-x|\le r_j$ and $r_j\to0$. Pass to a subsequence on which $(c_j-x)/r_j\to a$. Differentiability, uniformly on the ball of radius $2r_j$ about $x$, gives Hausdorff convergence
$$
 \frac{h(c_j+r_jS^1)-h(x)}{r_j}\longrightarrow A(a+S^1).
$$
The sets on the left are Euclidean circles for large $j$, since they lie in a finite target chart. Their radii are bounded above and bounded away from zero by invertibility of $A$, and their centers are bounded. A subsequential limit is consequently a nondegenerate Euclidean circle. Thus $A(S^1)$ is a circle. Linear algebra implies $A^TA=\lambda I$ with $\lambda>0$. For an orientation-preserving $h$, this is a complex-linear derivative. Therefore its weak antiholomorphic derivative vanishes almost everywhere. Mollification (or the local distributional Cauchy–Riemann argument) makes $h$ holomorphic; a holomorphic sphere homeomorphism is Möbius. Orientation reversal is handled by one initial conjugation.

This resolves this particular rigidity operation under the displayed reflection-chamber inputs. It does not supply Brooks's other needed operation: an actual quasiconformal equivalence of infinite packings with matching continued-fraction data. Nor does it yet construct the initial geometric pieces, their reflective chambers, or the orbifold descent marking. Those operations remain part of the original proof obligation.

## Annular hole caps in the actual capture setting

The following finite operation is available in the actual capture ambient. Let $M$ be connected oriented irreducible with a supplied finite PL structure, let the components of the collared boundary subsurface $P$ inject into $M$, and let $W$ be the disjoint marked rectangle locus. Let $N$ be the finite interval-bundle enclosure constructed from the selected essential strips. Each component contains an actual $W$-labelled vertical strip, and its interval fibers are essential relative to $P$. This operation uses irreducibility; it is not asserted for the broader enclosure input which assumes only $\pi_2(M)=0$.

Suppose an annular vertical frontier $A$ of a component $N_0$ misses $W$, and a horizontal rim $k_0$ bounds a disk $D_0\subset\operatorname{int}P$ on the side outside its incident horizontal surface. Among such eligible disks choose one containing the least number of horizontal boundary circles. Its interior misses the other rim $k_1$ of $A$. Indeed, a horizontal component wholly contained in $D_0$ is planar. If it is not a disk, an inner boundary bounds a strictly smaller eligible hole disk. Thus all wholly contained horizontal components are disks. The opposite horizontal component of $N_0$ cannot be one of these unless the base of $N_0$ is a disk; that case has no exterior hole cap except on a spherical $P$ component. Such a sphere is an entire boundary component; irreducibility would make the connected $M$ a ball, with $P$ its whole boundary and no nonempty disjoint $W$ locus. This is incompatible with the selected essential-strip setting. Consequently $D_0$ is an exterior compression disk for the torus used below; other enclosure components have not yet been excluded from its interior.

Double $M$ along $P$, retaining all finite collar and corner parameters. The seam injects on fundamental groups by collared van Kampen and graph normal form. This double is irreducible by an actual boundary-pocket reduction. Minimize the transverse seam intersections of a sphere. An innermost sphere disk lies in one half, and its boundary bounds a seam disk because the seam group injects into that half. Push this latter disk inward; together with the sphere disk it bounds an interior ball by irreducibility. Restoring the finite seam collar makes it a boundary-attached parametrized ball. Shrink this pocket by finite ball-window parameters and push it through the opposite seam collar. Move every sphere sheet entering that disk window with the pocket. The innermost circle disappears; any other window circles disappear or persist, and no new ones are created, since the move is a product on the window collar and is the identity outside it. Thus the finite intersection count strictly decreases. A disjoint sphere lies in one original half and bounds its ball there. This proves irreducibility without treating the seam disk as if its interior had automatically missed all other sphere sheets.

The double of $A$ is an internal torus $T$, and the double of $N_0$ is an embedded circle bundle. Its circle fiber is nontrivial of infinite order in the double: it traverses one actual essential interval in each half. If the endpoints lie in different seam components, its graph word is reduced by the distinct edge labels; if they lie in the same component, a cancellation would say that the interval is homotopic into $P$. Both are excluded. Its positive-length cyclically reduced word acts by a nonzero translation on the actual normal-form tree. The fiber lies on $T$, so $T$ cannot be contained in a ball.

Compress $T$ on its exterior side along $D_0$. A neighborhood of $T$ and this disk is the explicit torus collar with one meridional two-handle, with a sphere as its other frontier. That sphere bounds a ball in the double. If the ball were on the side containing $T$, then $T$ would be contained in a ball, contrary to its infinite fiber. On the other side, capping the sphere produces a parametrized exterior solid torus $V$ with boundary $T$. Its core has infinite order, because the fiber is a nonzero power of that core. Thus $\pi_1(V)\to\pi_1(\operatorname{double}_P M)$ is injective.

The seam meets $T$ exactly in $k_0$ and $k_1$, and $D_0$ is a disk component of its intersection with $V$. The region incident to $k_1$ on the $V$ side is also a disk. Here is the necessary essential/inessential split. If $k_1$ is essential in its seam component and its incident region were not a disk, that one-boundary region injects into the seam by the finite surface-cut normal forms and has noncyclic group. Seam injection would then embed its group into the cyclic group of $V$, a contradiction. If $k_1$ is inessential, its two sides are its disk and the complementary surface minus that disk. The latter either contains actual seam boundary, impossible inside the internal $V$, or surjects onto the noncyclic group of a closed positive-genus seam component, again impossible. The spherical case was excluded above. The same argument excludes any additional whole closed seam component inside $V$; positive-genus components contradict injection into the cyclic group, and spherical components are absent here. Therefore the entire seam intersection is exactly two disjoint disks $D_0,D_1$.

Both disk boundaries are essential on $T$, so both are meridians in $V$. To verify the first meridian cut explicitly, let $E$ be a standard product meridian and make its boundary disjoint from the parallel meridian circle $\partial D_0$. The standard solid torus is irreducible by finite PL Schoenflies in its supplied standard three-sphere: its connected exterior lies on one side of each interior sphere, so the ball on the other side lies inside the torus. Remove intersection circles of $E$ and $D_0$ by innermost disk exchanges. The sphere from an innermost $E$ disk and its $D_0$ disk bounds a ball whose interior misses the remainder of $D_0$, since that remainder is connected to $\partial V$; the resulting relative disk isotopy removes that circle. Once disjoint, $D_0$ lies in the supplied product ball obtained by cutting at $E$. It divides that ball into two parametrized balls by the proper-disk-in-ball Schoenflies/window argument. The two copies of $E$ are on opposite sides, because $\partial D_0$ separates the boundary annulus between them. Regluing them joins those two balls along a disk window, giving a parametrized ball by finite window absorption. This is $V$ cut along $D_0$. In this ball $D_1$ is genuinely a proper disk, so the boundary-cap Schoenflies/window argument divides it into two parametrized balls. No essential meridian circle was capped by a nonexistent disk on $\partial V$. The doubling reflection preserves the exterior $V$, since it preserves the doubled enclosure and its exterior side. It reverses the normal to the seam disks at their interior points, hence exchanges these two balls. No classification of involutions of a solid torus is required. It follows that $B=V\cap M$ is exactly one of these balls, with boundary $A\cup D_0\cup D_1$.

This ball misses $W$, since its intersection with $\partial M$ consists exactly of the two disks in $\operatorname{int}P$ and $A$ misses $W$. Any other enclosure component meeting the interior of a cap disk must stay in $B$: it cannot cross the disjoint frontier $A$ or escape through $\partial M$. It would then miss $W$, contradicting the actual $W$ strip in every enclosure component. Thus the caps are clean. Finite disk and ball-window parameters identify $B$ with $D^2\times I$, matching its side annulus and both marked end disks. Adjoin it to $N_0$ to fill this base hole. The existing marked maps have not moved at all; they remain literally the same maps into the enlarged interval region. No other enclosure component is swallowed.

This closes the annular-frontier hole operation under the displayed actual hypotheses. It does not assert the half-disk/strip-frontier analogue, a global all-interval-bundle fusion theorem, periodic return realization, or full torus/annulus capture. ## The used-window double and half-caps

The strip-frontier analogue uses only the actual USED rectangles of $W$, not arbitrary unused windows. Each used rectangle contains a selected strip side. A spanning path in that rectangle is homotopic through the selected strip to its $P$-essential interval fiber, so every such span is essential relative to $P$, with the allowed endpoint tracks in the two actual $P$-end intervals. This premise is indispensable: doubling an arbitrary window with a $P$-inessential span can make the doubled $P$ compressible.

Double $M$ across exactly these used rectangles and call its reflection $j$. The doubled subsurface $P^*$ injects. To see this directly, take a null disk for a loop in $P^*$ transverse to the window seams. Remove circular preimages by replacing innermost mapped disks by disks in the contractible window, then pushing across its collar. An outermost remaining preimage arc cuts off a disk lying in one original half, with its other boundary path in $P$. If its seam endpoints are at opposite ends of the window, this disk is a forbidden $P$-inessential span. If at the same end, compare its $P$ path with the path in that end interval. Injection of $P$ into the original half makes the resulting loop null in $P$, so replace the cut-off disk by that $P$ null disk and remove the preimage arc. Finite repetition leaves a disk in one original half, proving the original loop null in $P^*$. Original interval fibers remain essential relative to $P^*$ by the actual fold map: send each labelled half of the window double identically onto $M$. The two maps agree pointwise on each glued window, and send $P^*$ into $P$. A homotopy of an original fiber into $P^*$ would fold to a homotopy into $P$ with the same endpoint tracks, contradicting its original essentialness. The used-window double is irreducible by the boundary-pocket sphere reduction above; here its seams are actual disks, so their group injection is automatic.

This is an embedded annulus whose core has infinite order: its two-piece graph word is reduced precisely by the original rectangle's essentialness relative to $W$. This infinite-core certificate replaces the $W$ marker in the annular-cap argument.

Apply that argument to a globally minimal eligible hole disk in $P^*$. Both cap disks are clean: another enclosure component trapped by them would carry its infinite core in the cap ball. ## Simultaneous rectangle placement in an actual augmented terminal ball

The terminal geometric operation has the following exact input; the finite disk tower reaching it is supplied in the following section. The target is a supplied parametrized PL ball, its relevant $P$ pieces are disjoint disks on the boundary sphere, its used $W$ pieces are rectangle bands between them, and the finite family consists of rectangle maps with $P$ edges in those disks and $W$ edges spanning their labelled bands. Every used band is essential relative to $P$, and every rectangle is essential relative to $W$. Endpoint tracks within the actual marked pieces are allowed; pointwise fixed boundary maps are not being asserted.

Collapse the $P$ disks to vertices and the used bands to edges of the boundary ribbon graph. A used edge joins distinct vertices: a span returning to one disk would be homotopic into that disk in the ball. A rectangle uses two distinct parallel edges, since its two $P$ ends are connected in the same disk at each end; using the same band would make it inessential relative to $W$. Two parallel-edge groups cannot interleave at a common vertex. Their alternating two-pair ribbon subgraph would be a punctured genus-one surface rather than a subsurface of the sphere. The finite noninterleaving cyclic orders therefore permit the incident $P$ disks to be split into disjoint marked subdisks, one for each relevant parallel-edge group. Construct these subdisks by successively cutting off an innermost noninterleaving block with an empty polygonal arc; each cut removes a finite block and leaves a disk. Enlarge each resulting group's two disks and all its bands to a connected boundary neighborhood $Y$; its two horizontal disks and bands form a planar surface whose finitely many frontier circles miss all the other such neighborhoods.

Here is a simultaneous, genuinely nested disk choice. Pick a point $p$ of the sphere outside all frontier circles. For each frontier circle $c$ let $D_c$ be the sphere disk avoiding $p$. These disks are disjoint or nested. In the supplied inward boundary collar choose a nonnegative PL roof function $\varphi_c$, zero precisely on $c$ and positive on the interior of $D_c$. Push $D_c$ to the graph of $\epsilon_c\varphi_c$. Choose the positive finite constants in outer-to-inner order: if $D_d$ is strictly inside $D_c$, its compact support has positive distance from $c$, so choose its entire roof below the positive minimum of the outer roof on $D_d$. For several ancestors take their finite minimum. All pushed disks are consequently disjoint, including the nested cases.

Cut the ball along these finitely many proper disks. The proper-disk Schoenflies and ball-window argument gives parametrized ball pieces by induction. Their contact patches on the original sphere are exactly the connected components of the frontier-circle complement. Thus every group neighborhood $Y$ bounds its own piece, even if other groups lie in holes of $Y$: the nested roofs place those other pieces above its caps, rather than swallowing them. Finite disk, annulus and ball-window parameters therefore give a product $D^2\times I$ making every one of these bands vertical. These product balls are disjoint.

Adjust each original rectangle's $P$ edge paths in their disks and its $W$ spanning paths in their rectangles to the corresponding product paths. Those adjustments retain every actual label. The remaining two fillings of the same boundary loop in the parametrized ball are homotopic relative to their boundary, since their glued sphere is null by the ball's explicit contraction. Each rectangle map is thus homotopic through the marked pairs into its group's product ball. Repeated maps with the same two band labels require no additional embedded copies. This establishes the finite-family terminal placement. Since the chamber has no essential annulus, A is boundary-parallel. In particular δ is isotopic THROUGH ∂M to the annular P core; if the target is a whole torus component, δ was already on that component. The accepted simultaneous boundary placement in the SUPER convention therefore puts the entire circle δ in P. This is a genuine boundary isotopy, not an inference that two coannular curves on different boundary components are boundary-isotopic.

The same conclusion holds in the annulus case in the exact form needed. If the chosen annulus F already has a P boundary circle, its core is peripheral on F itself, and the collar from either rim to that actual P end is retained as part of the source. If F has no P boundary circles and a cut-side shared word existed, the preceding reduction would again produce the proper injected annulus A from one δ of F to P. If A is essential it is an admissible annulus cut with at least one P end, contradicting the maximizing choice of F. If it is boundary-parallel, the permitted boundary isotopy puts δ in P while preserving essentialness of F, giving the same contradiction. Thus a maximizing annulus with no P end has NO such nontrivial cut-side shared word. The disk case has no nontrivial surface loop. These exhaust the selected cuts.

Consequently the cut-side all-loop reduction, for these actually CHOSEN hierarchy cuts, ends at a whole P rim of F (or at the explicit P end of the annulus F), not at an unspecified old mirror shore. This discharges the previously identified rooted-book coannular-rim obstruction at this step by cutting the coannular annulus FIRST, rather than asserting that all mixed chambers were SIMPLE to begin with.

In particular a word supported on square-window reflection colors can have trivial projection to the underlying chamber group; it must be handled by the actual corner/window disk reduction, not by the nontrivial cyclic-intersection argument. That is the remaining toric-window lift operation.


## The actual toric-window boundary normal form and its color exponent

Here the windows are kept as actual labelled pieces throughout. A torus window has no mirror sides. An annular window has its two entire rim circles on annular mirror collars, with no added corner on those rims. A square window has exactly four corners and four reflector sides. No refinement below adds corners to either type of old window. The finite color map is assumed injective on the local corner stabilizers, as in the explicit orthant construction; distinct nonincident sides need not have different colors.

Before lifting the chosen F, minimize its boundary intersections with the whole corner-edge pattern, using actual boundary isotopies and carrying its face/handle/peripheral tracks. On a square, an arc returning to one side cuts off a disk; choose it innermost relative to the finite F trace and push across that disk, decreasing two side crossings. An arc joining adjacent sides cuts off the unique intervening corner. An innermost such arc can be pushed across that corner on ∂M: its two old window-side crossings are replaced by one crossing of the edge between the adjacent mirror faces. Its total corner-edge count drops from two to one. Move all intervening parallel trace arcs together in their finite disk-window parameters if needed; no source circle or chosen port is deleted. Thus the minimal trace on a square has only opposite-side spans. Two disjoint opposite-side span families cannot use the two different directions of the square, since a left-to-right arc separates the bottom and top sides. All spans in one square are therefore parallel. These are boundary isotopies before defining the selected colored lift, not an assertion that an already prescribed upstairs rim may be changed without its marking.

On an annular window, every proper arc returning to one rim cuts off a disk, by the strip-cover polygon argument. Innermost disk pushes remove these arcs. The remaining arcs span its two rims and are parallel. A whole boundary circle contained in the annulus is its core. Such a circle cannot be disjoint from a spanning arc, so the two types do not coexist on one window. On a torus window the finite disjoint essential circles are parallel primitive slope circles, with their actual orientations and base paths retained. Inessential whole boundary circles are excluded for a nondisk injected F. Essential disks use their separate boundary-minimum choice; a boundary circle contained in an injected annular or torus window cannot bound such a disk.

These elementary normal forms compute the lifted cusp circles exactly. In a square use its literal affine rectangle coordinates. Write s₀,s₁ for the reflections in the two sides met by a span. They are opposite sides. Their product a=s₁s₀ is the translation through twice the width. Reflecting the span and reversing its parameter at each successive endpoint gives a proper embedded periodic line. Over one two-reflection period its transverse coordinate returns to its initial value; hence the period is a, with NO omitted transverse or longitudinal word. If ρ is the actual finite color map, let e be the least positive integer with ρ(a^e)=0. Since the color group is elementary two-torsion, e is one or two. The corresponding lifted boundary circle has based stabilizer <a^e>. It is primitive in the actual torus covering lattice: the intersection of that lattice with this line is exactly the kernel just computed. It need not be primitive in the downstairs translation lattice, and the exponent e must not be discarded. Parallel spans lift to disjoint parallel circles.

For an annular window write its supplied strip coordinates as (R/Z)×[0,1], with core generator z and literal endpoint reflections. Let a be the product of the two endpoint reflections. They preserve the circle coordinate, commute with z and translate the doubled strip coordinate. A span with any original longitudinal winding r is followed by the reversed reflected span; their longitudinal changes cancel exactly. Thus its lifted periodic line has generator a^e, with e defined by the same actual color kernel. The winding r is retained in the two marked half-spans and their endpoint paths, rather than falsely replaced by zero in a source annulus word. A whole annular core circle instead lifts in the z direction, primitive in the corresponding boundary torus. These are the actual two possible slope types; disjointness has already excluded their coexistence. A whole old torus window simply retains its supplied primitive slope.

Accordingly every normal window span gives an essential primitive circle on the FULL torus section, with its first face, corner colors, orientation, actual covering exponent and endpoint tracks specified. A same-side or corner-return arc would instead give respectively a removable fold or a contractible circle around a corner; it has been removed by the explicit pre-lift boundary isotopies, not silently counted as an essential cusp circle. This supplies the local pure-window certificate needed by the reflected surface construction. It does not by itself show that EVERY annulus word in the colored three-manifold is conjugate into one of these boundary circles. That last statement requires a global disk/annulus reduction across the old mirror panels, retaining the computed color exponents and the cut-side word factorization above.
