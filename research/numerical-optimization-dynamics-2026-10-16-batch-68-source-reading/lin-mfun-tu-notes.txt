Preface

Computing functions of matrices - one of the main problems in numerical linear algebra
- occurs in a wide variety of applications in science and engineering. The authoritative
textbook in this field is undoubtedly

N. J. Higham, Functions of Matrices, SIAM, 2008.

The following quote, which outlines the importance of the topic also comes from there.

“From their origin in pure mathematics, matrix functions have broadened
into a subject of study in applied mathematics, with widespread applications
in science and engineering. Research on matrix functions involves matrix
theory, numerical analysis, approximation theory, and the development of
algorithms and software, so it employs a wide range of theory and methods
and promotes an appreciation of all these important topics.”

This lecture notes complement the 10 ECTS advanced course Matrix Functions at
Technische Universit¨at Berlin, which I first taught in summer 2024.
I would like to
emphasize that this script is not a textbook, but it is intended as a supplementary
resource for following the lectures and should not replace attending them. Students who
plan to attend this course need a solid background in (numerical) linear algebra. In
addition, basic knowledge of complex analysis is helpful.

The very first version of this lecture notes were typed by Anna Barkova, Lennart
Helmst¨adter, and Andreas Hygrell in 2024.
They have not only made their work

5
Computation of f(A)

This chapter is devoted to study popular methods for the numerical computation of
f(A). To choose the right method (also in a more general setting), we should always ask
ourselves the following questions.

• Which operations can be performed?

(E.g., multiplication with A, multiplication with AH, solving a linear system with
system matrix A, matrix factorizations.)

• What is the size of the matrix?

• What is the desired accuracy?

In what follows, we assume that A is small/medium sized, i.e., we can use matrix
decompositions and direct methods for solving linear systems.

5.1
Techniques to Compute f(A)

We consider three approaches to compute f(A).

(1) Decomposition approach

• For normal A, we compute an eigendecomposition A = UDUH and can then
compute f(A) = Uf(D)UH.

• For a non-normal matrix A, we use the Schur decomposition A = UTUH and
then calculate f(A) = Uf(T)UH. For this, we need a method to compute
matrix functions of upper triangular matrices. This leads to the Schur-Parlett
algorithm that is discussed in Section 5.2.

(2) Iteration approach

We construct a map g : Cn×n →Cn×n and build a matrix sequence by setting

Xk+1 := g(Xk),
k ≥0,
(5.1)

for some X0 ∈Cn×n, such that

lim
k→∞Xk = f(A)
(5.2)

holds. Important examples of this approach are “Newton-like methods” which we
study in Section 5.3.

(3) Approximation approach

We approximate f by a polynomial p or a rational function r, e.g., by Taylor
approximation or Pad´e approximation, and then compute p(A) or r(A) as an
approximation for f(A), respectively.
This leads, for example, to the “Scaling
and Squaring methods” that we will see in Section 5.4.

63


Note, that the first approach leads to a general purpose method, while the latter
approaches are usually used for specific f.
One may wonder why the above approaches differ so much from the definitions in
Chapter 2, where we defined f(A) using the Jordan form, the Hermite interpolation
polynomial, the Taylor series, and the Cauchy integral formula. In fact, the first three
definitons do not immediately lead to well-suited methods for computing f(A), while
the last one can be used for the computation of f(A)b.

(1) Jordan form definition

As discussed in Remark 2.1, computing a Jordan form of A is not stable, and, in
particular, not continuous in the entries of A.

(2) Hermite interpolation definition

We are interested in methods for computing f(A) that use O(n3) flops. Since the
multiplication of two (full) (n × n) matrices requires 2n3 flops, the evaluation of
a polynomial of degree n in A requires O(n4) flops, which is too expensive. Note
that for any non-derogatory matrix, the Hermite interpolation polynomial is of
degree n.

(3) Taylor series definition

Inserting A in a truncated Taylor series, i.e., in a Taylor polynomial, has the
same performance problems as the Hermite interplation approach, since a good
approximation of f typically requires a Taylor polynomial of degree O(n), even if
all eigenvalues of A are located in a tight cluster.

(4) Cauchy integral definition

The Cauchy integral formula can be used in combination with quadrature formulas
for calculating f(A)b; see Chapter 6.

5.2

is considered to be the “state of the art” approach for the computation of f(A) for

(1) Compute a (block) Schur decomposition A = UTUH; see Subsection 5.2.3.

(2) Evaluate f(T); see Subsections 5.2.1 and 5.2.2.

(3) Return Uf(T)UH.

5.4
Scaling and Squaring Algorithm

Finally, we study exemplarily an approximation approach, where we approximate a given
function f by a simpler function g and evaluate g(A) instead of f(A). We already faced
this idea twice when considering the Hermite interpolation definition (Definition 2.9) and
the Taylor series definition (Definition 2.13) of matrix functions. Recall the downsides
of these approaches.

• The Hermite interpolation polynomial is hard to compute (e.g., we need to know
derivatives of f), unstable, and thus, badly suited for numerical computation.
Moreover, the evaluation is expensive.

• In Theorem 5.3, we saw that the m-th Taylor polynomial fm yields a fairly good
approximation, if all eigenvalues of A are in a small disk and if m ≈n. Of course,
this assumption is rather strong.

Another issue of polynomial approximation, that applies to both approaches above, is
that polynomials have a pole at infinity, i.e., they “explode” for |z| ≫1.
We overcome all these issues when computing the matrix exponential exp(A) and
the matrix logarithm log(A), using the following ideas.
