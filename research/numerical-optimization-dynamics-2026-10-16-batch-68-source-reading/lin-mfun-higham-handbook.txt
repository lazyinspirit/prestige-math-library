Matrix functions are used in many areas of linear algebra and arise in numerous applications
in science and engineering. The most common matrix function is the matrix inverse; it is
not treated specifically in this chapter, but is covered in Section 1.5 and Section 38.3. This
chapter is concerned with general matrix functions as well as specific cases such as matrix
square roots, trigonometric functions, and the exponential and logarithmic functions.
The specific functions just mentioned can all be defined via power series or as the solution
of nonlinear systems. For example, cos(A) = I −A2/2! + A4/4! −· · ·. However, a general
theory exists from which a number of properties possessed by all matrix functions can be
deduced and which suggests computational methods. This chapter treats general theory,
then specific functions, and finally outlines computational methods. More details of all
aspects of this chapter can be found in [Hig08].

When the function f is multivalued and A has a repeated eigenvalue occurring in more
than one Jordan block (i.e., A is derogatory), the Jordan canonical form definition has
more than one interpretation. Usually, for each occurrence of an eigenvalue in different
Jordan blocks the same branch is taken for f and its derivatives. This gives a primary
matrix function. If different branches are taken for the same eigenvalue in two different
Jordan blocks, then a nonprimary matrix function is obtained. A nonprimary matrix
function is not expressible as a polynomial in the matrix, and if such a function is obtained
from the Jordan canonical form definition (17.2) then it depends on the matrix Z. In most
applications it is primary matrix functions that are of interest. For the rest of this section
f(A) is assumed to be a primary matrix function, unless otherwise stated.

17.8
Computational Methods for General Functions

Many methods have been proposed for evaluating matrix functions. Three general ap-
proaches of wide applicability are outlined here. They have in common that they do not
require knowledge of Jordan structure and are suitable for computer implementation. More
details of methods can be found in [HAM10].

1. Polynomial and Rational Approximations:

Polynomial approximations

pm(X) =

�

to matrix functions can be obtained by truncating or economizing a power series represen-
tation, or by constructing a best approximation (in some norm) of a given degree. How to
most efficiently evaluate a polynomial at a matrix argument is a nontrivial question. Pos-
sibilities include Horner’s method, explicit computation of the powers of the matrix, and
a method of Paterson and Stockmeyer [Hig08, Sec. 4.2], [PS73], which is a combination of
these two methods that requires fewer matrix multiplications.
Rational approximations rmk(X) = pm(X)qk(X)−1 are also widely used, particularly
those arising from Pad´e approximation, which produces rationals matching as many terms
of the Taylor series of the function at the origin as possible. The evaluation of rationals at
matrix arguments needs careful consideration in order to find the best compromise between
speed and accuracy. The main possibilities are
• Evaluating the numerator and denominator polynomials and then solving a multiple
right-hand side linear system.
• Evaluating a continued fraction representation (in either top-down or bottom-up or-
der).
• Evaluating a partial fraction representation.
Since polynomials and rationals are typically accurate over a limited range of matrices,
practical methods involve a reduction stage prior to evaluating the polynomial or rational.

2. Factorization Methods:

Many methods are based on the property f(XAX−1) = Xf(A)X−1. If X can be found
such that B = XAX−1 has the property that f(B) is easily evaluated, then an obvious
method results. When A is diagonalizable, B can be taken to be diagonal, and evaluation
of f(B) is trivial. In finite precision arithmetic, though, this approach is reliable only if
X is well conditioned, that is, if the condition number κ(X) = ∥X∥∥X−1∥is not too
large. Ideally, X will be unitary, so that in the 2-norm κ2(X) = 1. For Hermitian A, or
more generally normal A, the spectral decomposition A = QDQ∗with Q unitary and D

3. Iteration Methods:

Several matrix functions f can be computed by iteration:

Xk+1 = g(Xk),
X0 = A,
(17.8)

where, for reasons of computational cost, g is usually a polynomial or a rational function.
Such an iteration might converge for all A for which f is defined, or just for a subset of
such A. A standard means of deriving matrix iterations is to apply Newton’s method to an
algebraic equation satisfied by f(A). The iterations most used in practice are quadratically
convergent, but iterations with higher orders of convergence are known.

4. Contour Integration:

The Cauchy integral definition (17.5) provides a way to compute or approximate f(A)
via contour integration. While not suitable as a practical method for all functions or all
matrices, this approach can be effective when numerical integration is preceded by a con-
formal mapping and done over a suitable contour using the repeated trapezium rule, whose
high accuracy properties for periodic functions integrated over a whole period are beneficial
[DH05], [HHT08].

17.9
Computational Methods for Specific Functions

Some methods specialized to particular functions are now outlined.

1. Matrix Exponential:

A large number of methods have been proposed for the matrix exponential, many of them
of pedagogic interest only or of dubious numerical stability. Some of the more computation-
ally useful methods are surveyed in [MVL03]. Probably the best general-purpose method
is the scaling and squaring method. In this method an integral power of 2, σ = 2s say, is
chosen so that A/σ has norm not too far from 1. The exponential of the scaled matrix is
approximated by an [m/m] Pad´e approximant, eA/2s ≈rmm(A/2s), and then s repeated
squarings recover an approximation to eA: eA ≈rmm(A/2s)2s. Symmetries in the Pad´e
approximant permit an efficient evaluation of rmm(A). The scaling and squaring method
was originally developed in [MVL78] and [War77], and it is the method employed by the
Matlab function expm. How best to choose σ and m is described in [ALHI09], [Hig09].

2. Matrix Logarithm:

The (principal) matrix logarithm can be computed using an inverse scaling and squaring
method based on the identity log A = 2k log A1/2k, where A is assumed to have no eigenval-
ues on R−
0 . Square roots are taken to make ∥A1/2k −I∥small enough that an [m/m] Pad´e
approximant approximates log A1/2k sufficiently accurately, for some suitable m. Then log A
is recovered by multiplying by 2k. To reduce the cost of computing the square roots and
evaluating the Pad´e approximant, a Schur decomposition can be computed initially so that
the method works with a triangular matrix. For details, see [ALHI12], [CHKL01], [Hig01],
or [KL89, App. A].

3. Matrix Cosine and Sine:

A method analogous to the scaling and squaring method for the exponential is the stan-
dard method for computing the matrix cosine. The idea is again to scale A to have norm
not too far from 1 and then compute a Pad´e approximant. The difference is that the scaling
is undone by repeated use of the double-angle formula cos(2A) = 2 cos2 A −I, rather than
by repeated squaring. The sine function can be obtained as sin(A) = cos(A −π

5. Arbitrary Matrix Power:

A matrix power As with s ∈R can be computed by the Schur–Pad´e algorithm [HL11b],
which exploits the relation As = (A1/2k)s·2k. It reduces A to Schur form, computes k
square roots of the triangular matrix T so that T 1/2k is sufficiently close to I, evaluates
a Pad´e approximant to the function (1 −x)s, then squares k times and undoes the Schur
transformation. This approach is superior in practice to evaluating As = es log A using
methods for exp and log.
For pth roots, several methods are available. The Schur method for square roots has been
extended to pth roots [Smi03]. A variety of other methods are available [BHM05]. For the
(inverse) Newton iteration, convergence conditions are nontrivial [BHM05], [Ian06], and, as
in the square root case, coupled iterations help to ensure numerical stability. By combining
a Newton iteration with a Schur decomposition an effective algorithm that can compute
either A1/p or A−1/p can be obtained [GH06].
