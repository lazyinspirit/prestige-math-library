## Statement

Assume full AC. Let $N$ be a compact oriented irreducible PL three-manifold with a supplied finite triangulation. Let $P\subset\operatorname{int}N$ be an embedded collared PL product $F\times[0,1]$, where $F$ is a closed connected oriented finite PL surface of positive genus and each horizontal face injects on fundamental groups into $N$. Let $S\subset P$ be a compact connected orientable two-sided properly embedded locally flat PL surface, with product collar, no sphere or disk component, and no compressing or boundary-compressing disk in $P$. There is an ambient PL isotopy of $P$ preserving its boundary setwise that takes $S$ either to a horizontal slice $F\times\{s\}$, $0<s<1$, or to a vertical annulus $\gamma\times[0,1]$, where $\gamma\subset F$ is a non-nullhomotopic embedded PL circle.

In particular such a surface with nonempty boundary cannot have its whole boundary on just one horizontal face. If a finite disjoint family of closed positive-genus surfaces in $N$ is injective on fundamental groups and pairwise nonparallel, then a product bounded by two other injective closed surfaces cannot contain a component of that family parallel to one of its boundary faces unless those two family components were already parallel in $N$. The classification also applies simultaneously to a finite family satisfying the stated incompressibility hypotheses; its horizontal sheets have a consistent finite height order.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The ambient finite PL manifold, injective collared product region and surface in the Statement.

[F1] Finite cuts along injective collared surfaces of nontrivial fundamental group preserve irreducibility. Ordinary incompressibility of a two-sided PL surface is equivalent to its injection on fundamental groups. [[lem-finite-pi1-injective-surface-cuts-preserve-three-manifold-irreducibility]] [[lem-finite-pl-two-sided-incompressibility-is-pi-one-injectivity]]

[F2] The finite normal-isotopy proof supplies three local constructions in a parametrized three-ball: proper disk cuts give a ball system with finite tree dual graph; the ball-capping stage converts an incompressible family to proper disk intersections without increasing edge weight; and repeated crossings of an edge by one disk boundary supply a returning-edge bigon. Inessential surgery disks are realized by empty-ball isotopies, using irreducibility and the boundary disk-ball property. These constructions use the ball parameter and its polygonal boundary, not the number four of tetrahedron faces. [[lem-finite-pl-incompressible-boundary-incompressible-surfaces-admit-normal-isotopies]]

[F3] Nullhomotopic simple PL circles on a finite oriented PL surface bound PL disks. Compact PL faces have collars, disk maps extend over parametrized balls, and balls glued along boundary disk windows are balls. [[lem-nullhomotopic-circles-on-finite-oriented-pl-surfaces-bound-pl-disks]] [[lem-compact-locally-collared-pl-subpolyhedron-has-pl-collar]] [[lem-parametrized-pl-balls-absorb-standard-boundary-windows]]

[A1] Full AC is retained for [F1]. [[def-axiom-of-choice]]

## Proof

**Given:** The product data; all subsequent isotopies refer to $P$ and preserve its boundary setwise.

1.1 The region $P$ is irreducible. It is a component of the result of cutting $N$ along its two horizontal faces, so [F1] applies; their groups are nontrivial by the positive genus and are injective by hypothesis. Each boundary face also injects into $P$, directly by the product retraction. Therefore every proper disk in $P$ has boundary nullhomotopic on its incident face, so this boundary bounds a disk there by [F3]. Such a proper disk cobounds a ball with that boundary disk: push their common seam a short distance into a boundary collar, take the interior sphere consisting of the trimmed proper disk and the inward copy of the boundary disk, and use irreducibility. The collar toward the original boundary is on the outside of that sphere's interior ball, because it connects to $\partial P$. Adjoining its disk collar ball to the interior ball gives a ball by [F3], with boundary the original two disks. Thus $P$ satisfies exactly the two ball hypotheses used in [F2]. [F1, F3, given, construct, algebra]

2.1 Triangulate the supplied $F$ and decompose $P$ into triangular product prisms. Their edges are of two kinds: vertical edges $v\times[0,1]$ and horizontal edges in $F\times\{0,1\}$. Put the finite surface family in PL general position with these cells, as in [F2]'s finite local vertex-push construction. Among all such ambiently isotopic positions choose a minimum of the pair $(w,u)$ in lexicographic order, where $w$ counts intersections with vertical edges and $u$ with horizontal edges. This minimum exists because both coordinates are nonnegative integers: minimize $w$ first, then $u$ among its minimizing positions. Apply [F2]'s finite ball-capping procedure in each prism. Its surgery disks avoid all prism edges; deleting the trivial sphere or boundary-parallel disk through an empty ball can only delete intersections. Hence neither weight increases, so minimality keeps the pair unchanged. The surface now meets each prism in finitely many proper disks. This use of ball-capping is valid for a convex triangular prism: innermost circles cap on its boundary sphere, nested processed caps are pushed into successively thinner boundary collars, and its proper disk cuts give the same ball pieces and dual tree as for a tetrahedron. [F2, F3, step 1.1, choose, construct]

3.1 No resulting disk boundary meets one prism edge twice. Otherwise the dual-tree returning-edge bigon of [F2] removes two intersections with that edge. For a vertical edge it is an interior-edge isotopy, decreasing $w$. For a horizontal edge it is a boundary-compressing disk unless the surface arc cuts off a disk in $S$; the former contradicts the hypothesis, and the latter is the empty boundary-ball isotopy of [F2]. It decreases $u$ and cannot increase $w$, since the replaced disk avoids all other edges and the discarded part only deletes crossings. After either move reapply the finite prism ball-capping step, which increases neither weight. This contradicts minimality. There are also no circular face intersections: after the disk stage, a circle on an interior rectangular face would join two incident surface disks into a whole sphere component, and a circle on a horizontal boundary face would be a whole disk component. Both component types are excluded. Hence every face intersection is an arc, and no arc has both ends on the same face edge. [F2, step 2.1, given, construct, algebra]

4.1 On a vertical rectangle there can be no arc joining a vertical edge to an adjacent horizontal edge. If such an arc exists, choose one whose triangle toward their common corner is innermost. Its interior then misses $S$: any arc there either returns to one edge, excluded in step 3.1, or is another adjacent-edge arc cutting off a smaller such triangle. In a small neighborhood of this triangle, push the surface strip across the corner, moving its boundary arc on $\partial P$ past the endpoint of the vertical edge. This is a finite PL ambient isotopy: take the triangle's disk collar, perform the vertex move in its half-ball charts, and cone the prescribed boundary disk motion over the two incident ball windows as in [F3]. The strip loses that one vertical-edge crossing; its horizontal-edge crossings may change, which is allowed because $w$ strictly decreases. No other vertical edge meets the support, and the empty triangular interior prevents another surface strip from being crossed. Reapply prism ball-capping if necessary. This again contradicts minimum $w$. It follows that every arc on a vertical rectangle joins opposite edges: it is horizontal, joining its two vertical sides, or vertical, joining its two horizontal sides. Arcs of these two types cannot coexist on one rectangle, because they would intersect. [F2, F3, step 3.1, step 2.1, construct, algebra]

5.1 A prism disk with a vertical-edge crossing has exactly three such crossings and no horizontal-edge crossing. At one end of a face arc on a vertical edge, step 4.1 forces it to end on the other vertical edge of that face; at that end the next arc must lie on the next vertical rectangle and again end on its opposite vertical edge. After the three rectangles this returns to the first edge, and step 3.1 forbids continuing to a second crossing there. Thus its boundary is a horizontal triangle. If no prism disk has a vertical-edge crossing, all rectangle arcs are vertical. On each horizontal triangle the surface arcs join different sides by step 3.1. Write $n_1,n_2,n_3$ for the numbers of endpoints on its three sides. The respective counts joining side pairs are uniquely $(n_1+n_2-n_3)/2$, $(n_1+n_3-n_2)/2$ and $(n_2+n_3-n_1)/2$. Vertical arcs on rectangle faces identify top and bottom endpoint counts and their linear orders. Consequently the top and bottom arc systems have the same pair types and matching orders; gluing them through the rectangles pairs each top arc with its corresponding bottom arc, producing a vertical rectangle disk. To justify the order assertion, arcs of any one pair type are nested parallel arcs, and arcs of different pair types occupy the separate corner intervals; their disjointness fixes the pairing on each side interval. No permutation of endpoints is possible through disjoint spanning arcs of a rectangle. Horizontal triangle disks and vertical rectangle disks cannot coexist in a prism: the triangle has a horizontal arc on every rectangular face, precluding the vertical face arcs required by a vertical disk. [step 3.1, step 4.1, construct, algebra]

6.1 Straighten each finite ordered face-arc system to the just described horizontal or vertical standard system by PL disk maps. The endpoint maps on common edges are the same on both incident faces. Extend these maps over all prism disk systems using the proper disk ball parameters of [F2]: cutting along the disks gives parametrized balls, and the matching boundary disk maps extend over those balls by [F3]. Pasting and restoring the cut disks gives a PL homeomorphism of each prism to its standard system. It is realized by an isotopy: first interpolate the ordered points along each edge and extend successively over its face collars; after fixing all faces, the residual ball maps are isotopic to the identity by coning and the Alexander interpolation in their ball parameters. These finite constructions agree on shared faces, so give an ambient PL isotopy of the product. In a connected family component the prism type cannot change across a face it meets: horizontal triangles meet a vertical face in horizontal arcs, and vertical rectangles meet it in vertical arcs. The triangles therefore glue to a finite covering of $F$ by horizontal sheets, while the rectangles glue to $\gamma\times[0,1]$ for a finite disjoint union of PL circles $\gamma$ on $F$. [F2, F3, step 5.1, construct]

7.1 The finite horizontal covering has no nontrivial permutation of sheets. Above each point of $F$, the finitely many sheet heights are strictly ordered. In an overlap of prism charts the first sheet must remain the first, the second the second, and so on, since changing that order forces an intersection. Thus each global ordered sheet is the graph of a PL height function on all of $F$. An order-preserving fiber isotopy takes these graphs to distinct constant heights: triangulate the graphs, send each ordered interval between consecutive graphs to the corresponding constant-height interval, and fill its finite prism cells by compatible coning. Connectedness of $S$ gives one sheet in the horizontal case. In the vertical case connectedness gives a single circle $\gamma$. It is non-nullhomotopic, since otherwise its middle circle would bound a disk in $F$ by [F3], contradicting injection of $\pi_1(S)$ into $P$ from [F1]. This proves the classification and excludes a surface with nonempty boundary only on one horizontal face. For the last assertion, a closed family component contained in such a region is incompressible there because its composite into $N$ is injective. It has no boundary-compression arcs. Its classified horizontal product exhibits its parallelism to each horizontal face in $N$; if one face is a different component of the given pairwise nonparallel family, this is precisely the forbidden parallel pair. The same finite sheet-order construction applies to all components at once. [F1, F3, step 6.1, given, construct, algebra] ∎
