# 1. `order-zorn-and-the-axiom-of-choice-examples` (B, foundations, 8 items)

Companion to round 1's `order-zorn-and-the-axiom-of-choice`. B pages are leaves:
nothing outside this page may depend on it, and it may cite its own earlier items.

1. **`ex-finite-choice-by-induction`** (example). Exhibit a choice function
   explicitly for a concrete three member family, say
   $\mathcal{F} = \{\{0,1\}, \{1,2\}, \{2,3\}\}$, by listing the picks, and note
   that the induction of `lem-finite-choice` is exactly this process iterated.
   The point: for finitely many sets no axiom is needed because the choices are
   made one after another, each from a set already known to be nonempty.
2. **`ex-canonical-choice-on-naturals`** (example). $S \mapsto \min S$ is a
   choice function on $\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{N}) \setminus \{\emptyset\}$, with no
   appeal to AC, because `thm-well-ordering-principle` supplies a least element
   of every nonempty subset and `lem-sup-unique`-style uniqueness is not even
   needed (least elements are unique by antisymmetry). Contrast with the next
   item: structure, not size, is what makes choice free.
3. **`ex-russells-socks`** (example). Russell's shoes and socks. From countably
   many pairs of shoes a choice function exists outright ("take the left shoe");
   from countably many pairs of socks it does not, in ZF. Be careful and honest:
   this is an *illustration* of why a rule is needed, and the sock statement is a
   genuine ZF independence result, not something proved here.
4. **`ex-powerset-is-chain-complete`** (example). $(\mathcal{P}(X), \subseteq)$
   is chain complete: every $\subseteq$-chain $\mathcal{D}$ has least upper bound
   $\bigcup \mathcal{D}$, and the empty chain has least upper bound $\emptyset$.
   Verify both the upper bound and the leastness.
5. **`ex-zorn-poset-of-chains`** (example). For any poset $P$, the set of chains
   of $P$ ordered by inclusion is chain complete, with $\bigcup$ as supremum:
   any two elements of a union of a $\subseteq$-chain of chains lie in a common
   member, hence are comparable. This is precisely the poset step 1.2 of
   `thm-zorn` builds, so this example is the worked engine of that proof.
6. **`cex-maximal-not-greatest`** (counterexample). In
   $P = \mathcal{P}(\{1,2\}) \setminus \{\{1,2\}\}$ ordered by inclusion, both
   $\{1\}$ and $\{2\}$ are maximal and neither is greatest, and $P$ has no
   greatest element at all. Refutes `fs-maximal-is-greatest`.
7. **`cex-zorn-hypothesis-fails`** (counterexample). $(\mathbb{N}, \le)$ is a
   nonempty poset with no maximal element; the hypothesis of Zorn fails because
   the chain $\mathbb{N}$ itself has no upper bound. Point out that the poset is
   even totally ordered and chain complete "except at the top", so what fails is
   exactly boundedness of chains, not anything exotic.
8. **`cex-progressive-map-without-fixed-point`** (counterexample). On
   $(\mathbb{N}, \le)$, $f(n) = n+1$ is progressive and has no fixed point.
   $\mathbb{N}$ is not chain complete (the chain $\mathbb{N}$ has no supremum),
   so `thm-bourbaki-witt` does not apply. This is the sharpest possible statement
   that chain completeness is not decoration.

# 2. `filters-and-ultrafilters` (A, foundations, 12 items)

**Convention (most widely adopted, Wikipedia as tiebreaker): a filter is
PROPER**, that is $\emptyset \notin \mathcal{F}$. Say so explicitly and note the
competing convention in a remark within `def-filter`.

1. **`def-filter`** (definition). $\mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ is a
   **filter on $X$** when: $X \in \mathcal{F}$; $\emptyset \notin \mathcal{F}$;
   $A, B \in \mathcal{F} \Rightarrow A \cap B \in \mathcal{F}$; and
   $A \in \mathcal{F}$, $A \subseteq B \subseteq X \Rightarrow B \in \mathcal{F}$.
   Remark on the improper filter $\mathcal{P}(X)$, on the intuition "large sets",
   and that $X \ne \emptyset$ follows.
2. **`def-filter-base`** (definition). $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ is
   a **filter base** when $\mathcal{B} \ne \emptyset$, $\emptyset \notin
   \mathcal{B}$, and for all $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ there is $B_3 \in
   \mathcal{B}$ with $B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$ (downward directed).
3. **`lem-filter-base-generates`** (lemma, direct). The upward closure
   $\langle \mathcal{B} \rangle = \{A \subseteq X : B \subseteq A$ for some
   $B \in \mathcal{B}\}$ of a filter base is a filter, it is the smallest filter
   containing $\mathcal{B}$, and every filter is a filter base generating itself.
   Check all four filter axioms; the intersection axiom is where directedness is
   used.
4. **`def-finite-intersection-property`** (definition). $\mathcal{A} \subseteq
   \mathcal{P}(X)$ has the **finite intersection property** when every
   intersection of finitely many members of $\mathcal{A}$ is nonempty. The empty
   intersection is $X$ by convention, so a family with the FIP forces
   $X \ne \emptyset$.
5. **`lem-fip-generates-filter`** (lemma, direct). If $\mathcal{A}$ has the FIP
   then the set of finite intersections of members of $\mathcal{A}$ is a filter
   base, so $\mathcal{A}$ generates a filter; conversely any $\mathcal{A}$
   contained in a filter has the FIP. Induction on the number of sets is not
   needed if you take "finite intersections" as the base directly, but be
   explicit that the family of finite intersections is closed under pairwise
   intersection.
6. **`def-ultrafilter`** (definition). An **ultrafilter** on $X$ is a filter that
   is a maximal element of the set of filters on $X$ ordered by inclusion, in the
   sense of `def-maximal-element`. Stress that maximal is not greatest
   (`fs-maximal-is-greatest`): there is no largest filter.
7. **`lem-union-of-chain-of-filters`** (lemma, direct). If $\mathcal{D}$ is a
   nonempty $\subseteq$-chain of filters on $X$ then $\bigcup \mathcal{D}$ is a
   filter on $X$. Upward closure and $X \in \bigcup\mathcal{D}$ are immediate;
   $\emptyset \notin \bigcup \mathcal{D}$ because no member contains it; the
   intersection axiom uses that $A \in F_1$ and $B \in F_2$ with $F_1 \subseteq
   F_2$ or $F_2 \subseteq F_1$ puts both in one member. **Nonemptiness of
   $\mathcal{D}$ is essential** and must be stated: the union of the empty chain
   is $\emptyset$, which is not a filter.
8. **`thm-ultrafilter-lemma`** (theorem, direct). LANDMARK. Every filter on $X$
   is contained in an ultrafilter on $X$. Fix a filter $\mathcal{F}_0$; let
   $P = \{\mathcal{F} : \mathcal{F}$ a filter on $X$, $\mathcal{F}_0 \subseteq
   \mathcal{F}\}$ ordered by $\subseteq$. $P$ is nonempty. Every chain in $P$ has
   an upper bound: the empty chain is bounded by $\mathcal{F}_0$, and a nonempty
   chain by its union via `lem-union-of-chain-of-filters`. Zorn gives a maximal
   $\mathcal{U} \in P$, and maximality in $P$ gives maximality among all filters
   because any filter above $\mathcal{U}$ is above $\mathcal{F}_0$. **Handle the
   empty chain explicitly**; that is exactly where `thm-zorn`'s nonemptiness
   hypothesis is used.
9. **`thm-ultrafilter-characterisation`** (theorem, direct). A filter
   $\mathcal{U}$ on $X$ is an ultrafilter **iff** for every $A \subseteq X$,
   either $A \in \mathcal{U}$ or $X \setminus A \in \mathcal{U}$ (and never
   both). Forward: if neither, then $\{B \subseteq X : B \supseteq C \cap A$ for
   some $C \in \mathcal{U}\}$ is a filter strictly larger than $\mathcal{U}$;
   check it is proper, which needs $C \cap A \ne \emptyset$ for every $C$, which
   holds precisely because $X \setminus A \notin \mathcal{U}$. Backward: a filter
   $\mathcal{G} \supsetneq \mathcal{U}$ contains some $A \notin \mathcal{U}$,
   hence $X \setminus A \in \mathcal{U} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$, so
   $\emptyset = A \cap (X\setminus A) \in \mathcal{G}$, contradiction.
10. **`lem-ultrafilter-prime`** (lemma, direct). If $\mathcal{U}$ is an
    ultrafilter and $A \cup B \in \mathcal{U}$ then $A \in \mathcal{U}$ or
    $B \in \mathcal{U}$; extend to finite unions by induction. Proof from the
    characterisation: if neither, both complements are in $\mathcal{U}$, so their
    intersection $X \setminus (A \cup B)$ is, contradicting properness.
11. **`rem-choice-strengths`** (remark). The ultrafilter lemma is a genuine
    choice principle, **strictly weaker than AC**: AC implies it (that is
    `thm-ultrafilter-lemma`, via `cor-ac-iff-zorn`), it is not provable in ZF,
    and it does not imply AC. It is the Boolean prime ideal theorem in disguise.
    **Stay in your lane:** do not mention the well-ordering theorem or ordinals,
    which live on a page being written in parallel with this one.
12. **`fs-every-ultrafilter-principal`** (false-statement). FALSE: every
    ultrafilter is principal, that is of the form $\{A : x \in A\}$. On a finite
    $X$ it is true, which is why the intuition survives; on $\mathbb{N}$ the
    Fréchet filter of cofinite sets extends to an ultrafilter by
    `thm-ultrafilter-lemma`, and that ultrafilter contains no finite set, so it
    is not principal. Be honest that the existence of a non-principal
    ultrafilter is not provable in ZF: this refutation consumes the ultrafilter
    lemma, and the false statement is *consistent* with ZF alone.

# 3. `ordinals-and-transfinite-recursion` (A, foundations, 23 items)

Ordinals arrive here and nowhere earlier. Round 1's foundations page is
deliberately **ordinal free**, which is exactly what Bourbaki–Witt buys, so
nothing here may be pushed back into it.

Throughout, "well-order" means strict: $(W, <)$ with $<$ a strict total order in
which every nonempty subset has a least element.

1. **`def-well-order`** (definition). A **well-order** on $W$ is a total order
   in which every nonempty $S \subseteq W$ has a least element. Note that
   `thm-well-ordering-principle` says $(\mathbb{N}, <)$ is one.
2. **`def-order-isomorphism`** (definition). An **order isomorphism**
   $(P,\le_P) \to (Q,\le_Q)$ is a bijection $f$ with $x \le_P y \iff f(x) \le_Q
   f(y)$. Note it is automatically an order embedding and that its inverse is one
   too. Define "$P$ and $Q$ are order isomorphic". Keep this self contained:
   bijections are ambient ZFC vocabulary here.
3. **`def-initial-segment`** (definition). For $a \in W$ write
   $W_{<a} = \{x \in W : x < a\}$, the **initial segment determined by $a$**. A
   subset $I \subseteq W$ is an **initial segment** when $x \in I$ and $y < x$
   imply $y \in I$. State the basic fact that a proper initial segment of a
   well-order is $W_{<a}$ for a unique $a$ (least element of the complement).
4. **`thm-transfinite-induction`** (theorem, contradiction). Let $(W,<)$ be a
   well-order and $S \subseteq W$ satisfy: for every $a \in W$, if
   $W_{<a} \subseteq S$ then $a \in S$. Then $S = W$. Proof: else $W \setminus S$
   is nonempty, take its least element $a$; then $W_{<a} \subseteq S$, so
   $a \in S$, contradiction. **This is a ZF theorem; no choice.**
5. **`thm-transfinite-recursion`** (theorem, direct). LANDMARK. Let $(W,<)$ be a
   well-order and let $G$ be a class function assigning a value to each function
   with domain a proper initial segment of $W$. Then there is a unique
   $F : W \to V$ with $F(a) = G(F \restriction W_{<a})$ for all $a$. Skeleton:
   call $h$ an **attempt** when $\mathrm{dom}(h)$ is an initial segment and
   $h(b) = G(h \restriction W_{<b})$ throughout; show any two attempts agree on
   the intersection of their domains by transfinite induction; show an attempt
   with domain $W_{<a}$ exists for every $a$ by transfinite induction (union of
   attempts below $a$, extended by $G$); take $F$ = union of all attempts.
   **Say plainly that this uses Replacement** and no choice. Uniqueness is
   another transfinite induction.
6. **`lem-well-order-rigid`** (lemma, contradiction). If $f : W \to W$ is order
   preserving and injective then $f(a) \ge a$ for all $a$; consequently a
   well-order has no order isomorphism onto a proper initial segment of itself,
   and the only order automorphism of a well-order is the identity. Proof of the
   first claim: else the set $\{a : f(a) < a\}$ has a least element $a$, and
   $f(f(a)) < f(a)$ contradicts minimality.
7. **`lem-well-order-comparability`** (lemma, direct). For any two well-orders
   $V, W$, exactly one of: $V \cong W$; $V \cong$ a proper initial segment of
   $W$; $W \cong$ a proper initial segment of $V$. Skeleton: define
   $f = \{(v,w) : V_{<v} \cong W_{<w}\}$; show it is a function, injective, order
   preserving, with domain and range initial segments, using
   `lem-well-order-rigid` for uniqueness of the witnessing isomorphisms; then
   rule out both domain and range being proper. Exclusivity from
   `lem-well-order-rigid`. **No choice.**
8. **`def-ordinal`** (definition). An **ordinal** is a transitive set that is
   strictly well ordered by $\in$. Recall from `lem-nat-transitive-irreflexive`
   that each natural number is transitive, and that $\mathbb{N} = \omega$ is an
   ordinal. Notation $\alpha < \beta$ for $\alpha \in \beta$.
9. **`lem-ordinal-basics`** (lemma, direct). Every element of an ordinal is an
   ordinal; $\alpha \notin \alpha$; $\alpha^{+} = \alpha \cup \{\alpha\}$ is an
   ordinal; an intersection of a nonempty set of ordinals is an ordinal; a union
   of a set of ordinals is an ordinal; for ordinals, $\alpha \subseteq \beta$
   iff $\alpha \in \beta$ or $\alpha = \beta$.
10. **`lem-ordinal-trichotomy`** (lemma, direct). For ordinals $\alpha, \beta$
    exactly one of $\alpha \in \beta$, $\alpha = \beta$, $\beta \in \alpha$
    holds, and every nonempty set of ordinals has an $\in$-least element. So
    the ordinals are linearly ordered and every set of them is well ordered.
    Skeleton: $\alpha \cap \beta$ is an ordinal and is an initial segment of
    both; if it were a proper subset of each it would be a member of each, hence
    of itself.
11. **`thm-burali-forti`** (theorem, contradiction). There is no set of all
    ordinals. If $\Omega$ were one, then by `lem-ordinal-basics` and
    `lem-ordinal-trichotomy` $\Omega$ is transitive and well ordered by $\in$,
    hence an ordinal, hence $\Omega \in \Omega$, contradicting irreflexivity.
12. **`thm-mostowski-collapse`** (theorem, direct). Every well-order $(W,<)$ is
    order isomorphic to a unique ordinal, its **order type**. Define
    $F(a) = \{F(b) : b < a\}$ by `thm-transfinite-recursion`; show by transfinite
    induction that each $F(a)$ is an ordinal and $F$ is order preserving and
    injective; the image is transitive, hence an ordinal. Uniqueness from
    `lem-well-order-rigid`. **Uses Replacement, not choice.**
13. **`def-limit-ordinal`** (definition). $\alpha$ is a **successor ordinal** if
    $\alpha = \beta^{+}$ for some $\beta$; a **limit ordinal** if it is neither
    $0$ nor a successor. Equivalently a limit ordinal is a nonzero $\alpha$ with
    $\alpha = \bigcup \alpha$.
14. **`lem-omega-least-limit-ordinal`** (lemma, direct). $\omega = \mathbb{N}$ is
    a limit ordinal, and it is the least one. Uses
    `lem-nat-nonzero-is-successor` for "every smaller nonzero ordinal is a
    successor" and `lem-omega-smallest-inductive` for the fact that
    $\omega$ is not a successor.
15. **`thm-hartogs`** (theorem, contradiction). LANDMARK, and **choice free**.
    For every set $A$ there is an ordinal that does not inject into $A$; the
    least such is the **Hartogs number** $\aleph(A)$. Skeleton: by Separation on
    $\mathcal{P}(A) \times \mathcal{P}(A \times A)$ collect the pairs $(S, R)$
    with $R$ a well-order of $S \subseteq A$; by `thm-mostowski-collapse` and
    Replacement the set $H$ of their order types exists; $H$ is transitive and
    well ordered, hence an ordinal; and $H$ does not inject into $A$, since an
    injection would make $H$ the order type of a well-ordered subset of $A$ and
    so put $H \in H$. **Emphasise that this needs no choice**, which is exactly
    why it is the ZF substitute for cardinal comparability.
16. **`thm-well-ordering-theorem`** (theorem, direct). Assume AC. Every set can
    be well ordered. Skeleton via `thm-zorn`: let $P$ be the set of pairs
    $(A, <_A)$ with $A \subseteq X$ and $<_A$ a well-order of $A$, ordered by
    **end extension** ($(A,<_A) \preceq (B,<_B)$ when $A$ is an initial segment
    of $B$ and $<_B$ restricts to $<_A$). $P$ is nonempty ($(\emptyset,
    \emptyset)$). A chain has the union as an upper bound, and end extension is
    exactly what makes the union a well-order: a nonempty $S$ meets some $A$, and
    its least element there is least overall. Zorn gives a maximal
    $(M, <_M)$; if $x \in X \setminus M$ then appending $x$ on top end extends
    it, contradicting maximality; so $M = X$. **Where is AC?** Only inside
    `thm-zorn`. Say so.
17. **`thm-well-ordering-implies-ac`** (theorem, direct). If every set can be
    well ordered then AC holds: well order $\bigcup \mathcal{F}$ and send each
    $S \in \mathcal{F}$ to its least element.
18. **`cor-ac-iff-well-ordering`** (corollary, direct). Over ZF, AC, Zorn's
    lemma and the well-ordering theorem are equivalent. Compose the two previous
    items with `cor-ac-iff-zorn`.
19. **`def-cardinal`** (definition). A **cardinal** is an ordinal not
    equinumerous with any smaller ordinal (an initial ordinal). Under AC every
    set has a cardinality, namely the least ordinal equinumerous with it, which
    exists by `thm-well-ordering-theorem` and `lem-ordinal-trichotomy`. Note
    honestly that without AC "cardinality" as an ordinal is unavailable in
    general, and `thm-hartogs` is what survives.
20. **`fs-ordinals-form-a-set`** (false-statement). FALSE, by `thm-burali-forti`.
    Explain the appeal: every *initial segment* of the ordinals is a set, so the
    class looks locally set sized. Separation gives sets only as subsets of an
    existing set, which is precisely what is missing here.
21. **`fs-every-set-well-orderable-in-zf`** (false-statement). FALSE: the
    well-ordering theorem is not a theorem of ZF. Same conditional discipline as
    `fs-zorn-provable-in-zf`: what is proved is that ZF does not prove it,
    *assuming ZF is consistent*, by Cohen's models. Do not state anything
    unconditionally here.
22. **`fs-transfinite-induction-needs-choice`** (false-statement). FALSE:
    transfinite induction and transfinite recursion are theorems of ZF, using
    Replacement, and no choice. The confusion comes from the fact that *applying*
    them often involves choosing at each stage, and it is the choosing, not the
    recursion, that costs an axiom.
23. **`rem-choice-ledger`** (remark). The equivalents of AC over ZF: Zorn,
    well-ordering, Tychonoff, every vector space has a basis, cardinal
    comparability. Strictly weaker: the ultrafilter lemma / BPI, dependent
    choice, countable choice. **Do not wikilink the ultrafilter page**, which is
    being written in parallel; name the principle in prose. Choice free
    substitutes: `thm-hartogs`, `lem-well-order-comparability`.

# 4. `suprema-and-infima-examples` (B, real analysis, 8 items)

Companion to round 1's `suprema-and-infima`. Every item is a concrete
computation carried out in full; nothing is asserted by inspection.

1. **`ex-sup-of-open-interval`** (example). $\sup(0,1) = 1$ and $\inf(0,1) = 0$,
   and neither is attained. Verify $1$ is an upper bound; verify leastness by
   `lem-sup-epsilon`, exhibiting for given $\varepsilon > 0$ an explicit
   $s \in (0,1)$ with $s > 1 - \varepsilon$, for instance
   $s = \max\{1/2, 1 - \varepsilon/2\}$.
2. **`ex-sup-of-closed-interval`** (example). $\sup[0,1] = 1 = \max[0,1]$, via
   `lem-max-is-sup`. The contrast with item 1 is the content: the same supremum,
   attained in one case and not the other.
3. **`ex-inf-of-reciprocals`** (example). $\inf\{1/n : n \ge 1\} = 0$, not
   attained, and $\sup = 1$, attained. Leastness of the infimum is exactly
   `thm-of-archimedean`: for $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $n$ with $1/n <
   \varepsilon$.
4. **`ex-sup-rationals-below-sqrt-two`** (example).
   $S = \{q \in \mathbb{Q} : q > 0, q^2 < 2\}$ has $\sup S = \sqrt{2}$ in
   $\mathbb{R}$ by `thm-of-square-roots` and `lem-sup-epsilon`, and has **no**
   supremum inside $\mathbb{Q}$. The second half is the whole reason $\mathbb{R}$
   exists; cite `fs-rationals-complete` and `fs-sqrt2-rational`.
5. **`ex-sup-of-sum-of-sets`** (example). With $S = (0,1)$ and $T = (2,3)$,
   $S + T = (2,4)$ and $\sup(S+T) = 4 = \sup S + \sup T$, illustrating
   `lem-sup-sum`. Prove the set identity $S + T = (2,4)$, do not assert it.
6. **`cex-sup-not-attained`** (counterexample). Refutes `fs-sup-belongs-to-set`:
   $\sup(0,1) = 1 \notin (0,1)$. Keep it minimal and point at item 1 for the
   computation rather than repeating it.
7. **`cex-unbounded-set-has-no-sup`** (counterexample). Refutes
   `fs-every-set-has-sup`: the canonical copy of $\mathbb{N}$ inside
   $\mathbb{R}$ has no upper bound at all, by `thm-of-archimedean`, hence no
   supremum. Use `lem-of-naturals-positive` for the canonical embedding.
8. **`cex-empty-set-has-no-sup`** (counterexample). Also refutes
   `fs-every-set-has-sup`, for the opposite reason: every real is an upper bound
   of $\emptyset$, and there is no least real, so $\sup \emptyset$ does not exist
   in $\mathbb{R}$. Connect to `rem-sup-conventions`, which records the
   extended real convention $\sup \emptyset = -\infty$ and why this library does
   not adopt it silently.

# 5. `roots-and-rational-powers` (A, real analysis, 22 items)

Everything is inside the complete ordered field $\mathbb{R}$. **No exponential,
no logarithm, no limits, no continuity**: none of them exist yet. Exponents are
integers and then rationals, full stop.

1. **`def-finite-sum`** (definition). By `thm-recursion`: for $a : \mathbb{N} \to
   \mathbb{R}$ define $\sum_{k=0}^{-1} a_k$ as the empty sum $0$ and
   $\sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k = \left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} a_k\right) + a_n$; likewise
   $\prod$ with empty product $1$. Fix the notation $\sum_{k=m}^{n}$ for a
   general lower index. State clearly that this is a definition by recursion and
   name the recursion theorem, since that is what makes it legitimate.
2. **`lem-finite-sum-laws`** (lemma, induction). Linearity
   $\sum (a_k + b_k) = \sum a_k + \sum b_k$ and $\sum c\,a_k = c \sum a_k$;
   splitting $\sum_{k=0}^{n} = \sum_{k=0}^{m} + \sum_{k=m+1}^{n}$; monotonicity,
   if $a_k \le b_k$ for all $k$ then $\sum a_k \le \sum b_k$; and the telescoping
   identity $\sum_{k=0}^{n}(c_{k+1} - c_k) = c_{n+1} - c_0$. All by induction on
   $n$. These are used everywhere below, so state them once and cite.
3. **`def-integer-power`** (definition). $a^0 = 1$, $a^{n+1} = a^n \cdot a$ for
   $n \in \mathbb{N}$; and $a^{-n} = (a^n)^{-1}$ for $a \ne 0$. Note $0^0 = 1$ is
   the convention adopted here (it is the one that makes the empty product and
   the binomial theorem work) and say so explicitly.
4. **`lem-power-laws`** (lemma, induction). For $a, b \ne 0$ and $m, n \in
   \mathbb{Z}$: $a^{m+n} = a^m a^n$, $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$, $(ab)^n = a^n b^n$.
   Prove for $\mathbb{N}$ by induction, then extend to $\mathbb{Z}$ by the
   definition of negative powers and `lem-of-inverse-unique`.
5. **`lem-power-monotone`** (lemma, induction). For $n \ge 1$ and
   $0 \le a < b$: $a^n < b^n$. Hence $x \mapsto x^n$ is strictly increasing and
   injective on $[0,\infty)$. Also: $a > 1 \Rightarrow a^n$ strictly increasing
   in $n$; $0 < a < 1 \Rightarrow$ strictly decreasing.
6. **`lem-bernoulli-inequality`** (lemma, induction). For $x \ge -1$ and
   $n \in \mathbb{N}$: $(1+x)^n \ge 1 + nx$, with equality iff $n \le 1$ or
   $x = 0$. The induction step multiplies by $1 + x \ge 0$ and discards
   $nx^2 \ge 0$; be explicit that $1 + x \ge 0$ is what licenses the
   multiplication (`lem-of-sign-rules`).
7. **`lem-power-difference-factorisation`** (lemma, direct). For $n \ge 1$,
   $b^n - a^n = (b-a)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} a^k b^{n-1-k}$. Prove by telescoping
   (`lem-finite-sum-laws`) rather than by a separate induction. Corollary to
   state here, because `thm-nth-roots-exist` needs it: if
   $0 \le a \le b \le M$ then $b^n - a^n \le n M^{n-1} (b - a)$.
8. **`thm-nth-roots-exist`** (theorem, contradiction). LANDMARK. For every
   $a \ge 0$ and every integer $n \ge 1$ there is a unique $s \ge 0$ with
   $s^n = a$; write $s = a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}$. Skeleton, the standard
   least-upper-bound argument: let $S = \{x \ge 0 : x^n \le a\}$, nonempty
   ($0 \in S$) and bounded above (by $1 + a$, since $x > 1+a$ gives
   $x^n \ge x > a$ using `lem-power-monotone`); set $s = \sup S$. Rule out
   $s^n < a$ and $s^n > a$ using the Lipschitz-style estimate of item 7 to choose
   an explicit $h > 0$ with $(s+h)^n < a$ respectively $(s-h)^n > a$, each
   contradicting a property of the supremum. Uniqueness from
   `lem-power-monotone`. **Generalises the published `thm-of-square-roots`; cite
   it and say so rather than pretending the $n=2$ case is new.**
9. **`def-rational-power`** (definition). For $a > 0$ and $r = m/n \in
   \mathbb{Q}$ with $n \ge 1$, define $a^r = (a^{1/n})^m$. Restrict to $a > 0$
   and say why (item 21). Well-definedness is `lem-rational-power-well-defined`,
   which must be recorded in `justified_by`, not `deps`.
10. **`lem-rational-power-well-defined`** (lemma, direct). If $m/n = p/q$ with
    $n, q \ge 1$ then $(a^{1/n})^m = (a^{1/q})^p$ for $a > 0$. Skeleton: both
    sides are nonnegative and their $nq$-th powers agree, by `lem-power-laws`;
    conclude by injectivity of $x \mapsto x^{nq}$ on the nonnegatives
    (`lem-power-monotone`).
11. **`lem-rational-power-laws`** (lemma, direct). For $a, b > 0$ and
    $r, s \in \mathbb{Q}$: $a^{r+s} = a^r a^s$, $(a^r)^s = a^{rs}$,
    $(ab)^r = a^r b^r$, $a^{-r} = 1/a^r$. Reduce to `lem-power-laws` and
    `thm-nth-roots-exist` over a common denominator.
12. **`lem-rational-power-monotone`** (lemma, cases). For $a > 0$ fixed:
    $r < s \Rightarrow a^r < a^s$ when $a > 1$, $a^r = a^s$ when $a = 1$,
    $a^r > a^s$ when $0 < a < 1$. And for $r > 0$ fixed, $a \mapsto a^r$ is
    strictly increasing on $(0,\infty)$. Use single-token case labels: `big`,
    `one`, `small`.
13. **`lem-triangle-inequality-finite`** (lemma, induction).
    $\left|\sum_{k=0}^n a_k\right| \le \sum_{k=0}^n |a_k|$, by induction from
    `lem-of-triangle-inequality`.
14. **`thm-am-gm`** (theorem, induction). For $a_0, \dots, a_{n-1} \ge 0$,
    $\left(\prod a_k\right)^{1/n} \le \frac{1}{n}\sum a_k$, with equality iff all
    $a_k$ are equal. Proof by **Cauchy forward-backward induction**, and state
    that name: (i) $n = 2$ from $(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b})^2 \ge 0$; (ii) $n
    \Rightarrow 2n$ by grouping; (iii) $n \Rightarrow n-1$ by taking the $n$-th
    entry to be the arithmetic mean of the other $n-1$. Precheck wants a single
    `induction` skeleton, so present (ii) and (iii) as two phases inside the
    inductive step and be explicit in prose that the induction is over the
    doubling scheme, not over $n$ directly.
15. **`thm-weighted-am-gm-rational`** (theorem, direct). For $x_k > 0$ and
    **rational** weights $w_k \ge 0$ with $\sum w_k = 1$:
    $\prod x_k^{w_k} \le \sum w_k x_k$. Skeleton: write $w_k = m_k / N$ over a
    common denominator, apply `thm-am-gm` to the list in which $x_k$ is repeated
    $m_k$ times, and translate back with `lem-rational-power-laws`. **The
    restriction to rational weights is not laziness**: real weights need real
    exponents, which need the exponential function. Say so.
16. **`thm-young-inequality`** (theorem, direct). For $a, b \ge 0$ and rational
    $p, q > 1$ with $1/p + 1/q = 1$: $ab \le a^p/p + b^q/q$. Immediate from item
    15 with weights $1/p, 1/q$ applied to $a^p, b^q$. Handle $a = 0$ or $b = 0$
    separately since item 15 assumed positivity.
17. **`thm-cauchy-schwarz-finite`** (theorem, direct). $\left(\sum a_k
    b_k\right)^2 \le \left(\sum a_k^2\right)\left(\sum b_k^2\right)$, with the
    root form $\left|\sum a_k b_k\right| \le \sqrt{\sum a_k^2}\sqrt{\sum b_k^2}$,
    and equality iff the vectors are proportional. Skeleton: $0 \le \sum (a_k t +
    b_k)^2 = At^2 + 2Bt + C$ for every real $t$; if $A = 0$ then all $a_k = 0$
    and the claim is trivial; otherwise put $t = -B/A$ to get $B^2 \le AC$. Note
    this proof needs no root at all, and the root form is a restatement via
    `lem-of-square-monotone`.
18. **`thm-holder-finite`** (theorem, direct). For rational $p, q > 1$ conjugate:
    $\sum |a_k b_k| \le \left(\sum |a_k|^p\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum
    |b_k|^q\right)^{1/q}$. Normalise: if either factor is $0$ all terms vanish;
    otherwise divide through and apply `thm-young-inequality` termwise, then sum.
19. **`thm-minkowski-finite`** (theorem, direct). For rational $p \ge 1$:
    $\left(\sum |a_k + b_k|^p\right)^{1/p} \le \left(\sum
    |a_k|^p\right)^{1/p} + \left(\sum |b_k|^p\right)^{1/p}$. Skeleton: $p = 1$ is
    `lem-triangle-inequality-finite`; for $p > 1$ split $|a_k + b_k|^p \le
    |a_k||a_k+b_k|^{p-1} + |b_k||a_k+b_k|^{p-1}$ and apply Hölder to each piece
    with conjugate exponent $q = p/(p-1)$, **which is rational exactly because
    $p$ is**. Divide by the common factor, handling the zero case.
20. **`fs-negative-has-square-root`** (false-statement). FALSE: every real has a
    real square root. Refutation: squares are nonnegative
    (`lem-of-square-positive`), so $x^2 = -1$ is impossible in any ordered field.
    Note that odd roots of negatives DO exist and give the true statement:
    $x \mapsto x^n$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{R}$ for odd $n$.
21. **`fs-rational-power-of-negative-base`** (false-statement). FALSE:
    $a^{m/n}$ extends to negative $a$ by the same formula. Refutation:
    $(-8)^{1/3} = -2$ but $1/3 = 2/6$ and $(-8)^{2/6}$ would be
    $((-8)^{1/6})^{2}$, and $(-8)^{1/6}$ does not exist in $\mathbb{R}$; so the
    value depends on the representative and `lem-rational-power-well-defined`
    genuinely fails. This is why `def-rational-power` insists on $a > 0$.
22. **`rem-real-exponents-deferred`** (remark). What is available so far is
    $a^r$ for rational $r$ only, monotone in $r$ by
    `lem-rational-power-monotone`. Defining $a^x$ for real $x$ as
    $\sup\{a^r : r \in \mathbb{Q}, r \le x\}$ is possible in principle and gives
    the right object, but proving the power laws for it needs limits, and the
    library's route to general powers is through the exponential and the
    logarithm, developed much later. **State this in prose with no wikilink**;
    the target page is seventeen rounds away.

# 6. `countability-and-uncountability` (A, real analysis, 19 items)

**$\mathbb{R}$ is proved uncountable by Cantor's 1874 nested-interval argument,
not by the decimal diagonal.** Decimal expansions are infinite series, which do
not exist in this library until much later, so the diagonal proof would be
circular here. The pure diagonal survives, on power sets, as item 13.

Ordinals are being written in parallel: **do not mention or cite them.**

1. **`def-injection-surjection-bijection`** (definition). $f : A \to B$ is
   **injective** when $f(x) = f(y) \Rightarrow x = y$; **surjective** when every
   $b \in B$ is $f(x)$ for some $x$; **bijective** when both. Composition of
   injections is an injection, and similarly for surjections; a bijection has a
   two-sided inverse. Add a short remark that functions, ordered pairs and
   Cartesian products are ambient ZFC vocabulary in this library, exactly as
   "subset" and "union" are, and that this item only fixes terminology.
2. **`def-equinumerous`** (definition). $A \approx B$ when a bijection
   $A \to B$ exists; $A \preceq B$ when an injection $A \to B$ exists. Note
   $\approx$ is reflexive, symmetric and transitive **on any set of sets** (it is
   not a relation on a set of all sets, which does not exist), and $\preceq$ is
   reflexive and transitive.
3. **`def-countable`** (definition). $A$ is **finite** when $A \approx n$ for
   some $n \in \mathbb{N}$ (von Neumann natural, so $n = \{0,\dots,n-1\}$);
   **countably infinite** when $A \approx \mathbb{N}$; **at most countable** when
   finite or countably infinite; **uncountable** otherwise. Record that this
   library's convention is that "countable" alone means "at most countable" and
   flag the competing convention in a remark.
4. **`thm-schroder-bernstein`** (theorem, direct). If $A \preceq B$ and
   $B \preceq A$ then $A \approx B$. **Choice free**, and say so. Skeleton with
   $f : A \to B$, $g : B \to A$ injective: define $C_0 = A \setminus g[B]$ and
   $C_{n+1} = g[f[C_n]]$ by `thm-recursion`, put $C = \bigcup C_n$, and set
   $h(x) = f(x)$ for $x \in C$, $h(x) = g^{-1}(x)$ otherwise; check $g^{-1}$ is
   defined off $C$ and that $h$ is a bijection.
5. **`lem-subset-of-countable`** (lemma, direct). Every subset of an at most
   countable set is at most countable. **Choice free**, and this is the point:
   the canonical enumeration comes from `thm-well-ordering-principle`, taking
   least elements, never from arbitrary picks. Handle the finite and infinite
   cases and note the subset of an infinite countable set may be finite.
6. **`lem-countable-iff-surjection-from-n`** (lemma, direct). A nonempty $A$ is
   at most countable **iff** there is a surjection $\mathbb{N} \to A$. Backward
   direction: given a surjection $s$, the map $a \mapsto \min s^{-1}(\{a\})$ is
   an injection $A \to \mathbb{N}$, by `thm-well-ordering-principle`; then apply
   item 5. **No choice is used**, precisely because "min" is canonical.
7. **`lem-finite-subsets-listable`** (lemma, induction). Every nonempty finite
   $F \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ can be written $\{a_0, \dots, a_n\}$ for some
   $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and some $a_0, \dots, a_n \in \mathbb{R}$, and conversely.
   Induction on the $n$ with $F \approx n+1$. **This item exists to discharge the
   one stipulation left open in round 1's `lem-finite-set-has-max`**, which
   assumed exactly this bridge because no definition of finiteness existed yet.
   Say so in a remark, and point back at that lemma.
8. **`thm-n-cross-n-countable`** (theorem, direct). $\mathbb{N} \times
   \mathbb{N} \approx \mathbb{N}$. Use the explicit bijection
   $(m,n) \mapsto 2^m(2n+1) - 1$ and prove it is a bijection from unique
   factorisation into a power of two times an odd number, which follows from
   `thm-strong-induction`; or use the Cantor pairing polynomial. Either way
   **exhibit the map and prove bijectivity**; do not wave at "the diagonal
   enumeration".
9. **`thm-product-of-countable`** (theorem, direct). If $A$ and $B$ are at most
   countable then so is $A \times B$; by induction, so is any finite product.
   From items 6 and 8 by composing surjections.
10. **`def-countable-choice`** (definition). **AC$_\omega$**: every countable
    family of nonempty sets has a choice function. Record that it is strictly
    weaker than AC and strictly stronger than ZF, and that dependent choice sits
    between them. This is the honest home for the qualification the next item
    needs.
11. **`thm-countable-union-of-countable`** (theorem, direct). Assume
    AC$_\omega$. A countable union of at most countable sets is at most
    countable. **The use of choice must be flagged at the exact step**: choosing,
    for each $n$, one surjection $\mathbb{N} \to A_n$ out of the many that exist.
    Then $(n,k) \mapsto s_n(k)$ is a surjection $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} \to
    \bigcup A_n$ and item 8 finishes. See item 16 for what happens without it.
12. **`thm-rationals-countable`** (theorem, direct). $\mathbb{Q}$ is countably
    infinite. **Choice free**, because `lem-rat-positive-denominator` gives every
    rational a canonical representative: reduce to lowest terms with positive
    denominator, inject into $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{N}$, and use items 8 and
    5 with $\mathbb{Z} \approx \mathbb{N}$. Infinite because
    $n \mapsto n$ is injective.
13. **`thm-cantor-powerset`** (theorem, contradiction). There is no surjection
    $A \to \mathcal{P}(A)$; hence $A \not\approx \mathcal{P}(A)$ while
    $A \preceq \mathcal{P}(A)$. The diagonal set $D = \{x \in A : x \notin
    f(x)\}$; if $D = f(d)$ then $d \in D \iff d \notin D$. Note this is the
    diagonal argument in its non-circular form, and that it needs nothing about
    $\mathbb{R}$.
14. **`thm-r-uncountable`** (theorem, contradiction). LANDMARK. $\mathbb{R}$ is
    uncountable. Skeleton, Cantor 1874: suppose $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$
    is a surjection. Build nested closed intervals $[a_n, b_n]$ with $a_n < b_n$
    and $f(n) \notin [a_{n+1}, b_{n+1}]$ as follows: given $[a_n,b_n]$ of length
    $d$, the three closed thirds $[a_n, a_n + d/3]$, $[a_n + d/3, a_n + 2d/3]$,
    $[a_n + 2d/3, b_n]$ cannot all contain $f(n)$; take the **first one in that
    fixed order** which does not. **This deterministic rule is essential**: it
    makes the construction an application of `thm-recursion` with no choice at
    all, and a version that says "pick one avoiding $f(n)$" would be quietly
    using dependent choice. Then $(a_n)$ is increasing and bounded above by
    $b_0$, so $x = \sup\{a_n\}$ exists; show $a_n \le x \le b_n$ for every $n$,
    hence $x \in [a_n,b_n]$ for all $n$, hence $x \ne f(n)$ for every $n$,
    contradicting surjectivity. **Call out the choice-freeness explicitly**; it
    is the whole reason for the thirds.
15. **`cor-irrationals-uncountable`** (corollary, contradiction).
    $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ is uncountable. If it were at most
    countable then $\mathbb{R}$, the union of two at most countable sets, would
    be at most countable by a **two-set** union (which needs no choice at all,
    unlike item 11), contradicting item 14. Be explicit that only the finite case
    of the union theorem is used here.
16. **`fs-countable-union-theorem-of-zf`** (false-statement). FALSE: "a countable
    union of countable sets is countable" is a theorem of ZF. It is not: it is
    consistent with ZF that $\mathbb{R}$ is a countable union of countable sets
    (Feferman and Levy, 1963). Keep the statement conditional on the consistency
    of ZF, in the style of `fs-zorn-provable-in-zf`. The correct reading of item
    11 is that AC$_\omega$ is genuinely being spent.
17. **`fs-infinite-has-countable-subset-in-zf`** (false-statement). FALSE: every
    infinite set has a countably infinite subset, in ZF. The proof everyone
    reaches for picks $a_0, a_1, \dots$ in turn, which is dependent choice. In
    ZF there may exist infinite sets with no countably infinite subset
    (amorphous sets, in Fraenkel-Mostowski style models and their ZF
    counterparts). Again conditional on consistency. Note that with AC$_\omega$
    the statement is true, which is why it feels obvious.
18. **`fs-uncountable-contains-interval`** (false-statement). FALSE: every
    uncountable subset of $\mathbb{R}$ contains an interval. Refuted by
    $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$, uncountable by item 15, containing no
    interval because $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense (`lem-rat-embeds-dense`), so every
    interval of positive length meets $\mathbb{Q}$.
19. **`rem-continuum-hypothesis`** (remark). By item 13,
    $\mathbb{N} \prec \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})$; CH asserts nothing sits strictly
    between $|\mathbb{N}|$ and $|\mathbb{R}|$. Independent of ZFC: Gödel 1940
    (consistency of CH), Cohen 1963 (consistency of its negation). Note honestly
    that the library has **not** yet proved $\mathbb{R} \approx
    \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})$, because the natural proof runs through binary
    expansions, which are series. State that in prose with no wikilink.

# 7. `sequences-and-limits` (A, real analysis, 18 items)

**Convergence and Cauchy for real sequences are ALREADY defined** by the
published `def-real-limit`, exactly as the supremum was already defined for round
1. **Do not redefine them.** Cite `def-real-limit` and build the toolkit.

Note its phrasing: it quantifies over **rational** $\varepsilon > 0$. That loses
nothing, by `lem-rat-embeds-dense`, and the remark in `def-real-limit` says so.
Where a real $\varepsilon$ is more convenient, say once that the two formulations
agree and cite that remark, rather than silently switching.

Monotone convergence, Bolzano-Weierstrass and Cauchy completeness of $\mathbb{R}$
belong to the **next** page, `monotone-sequences-and-cauchy-completeness`. Do not
prove them here, and do not link them.

1. **`def-sequence`** (definition). A **sequence** of reals is a function
   $x : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$, written $(x_k)$. Define: **bounded**
   sequence; a property holding **eventually** (for all $k \ge K$) and
   **frequently** (for infinitely many $k$); a **subsequence** $(x_{n_k})$ given
   by a strictly increasing $n : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$; the **tail**
   $(x_{k+K})_k$. State explicitly that convergence and Cauchyness are
   `def-real-limit` and are not redefined here.
2. **`lem-limit-of-tail`** (lemma, direct). $(x_k)$ converges to $x$ iff some
   tail does iff every tail does. Convergence depends only on the tail. Short,
   and it is what makes "eventually" usable in every later proof.
3. **`lem-limit-unique`** (lemma, contradiction). A sequence has at most one
   limit. If $x \ne y$ were both limits, take $\varepsilon$ rational with
   $0 < 2\varepsilon < |x - y|$, available by `thm-of-archimedean` and
   `lem-rat-embeds-dense`, and derive $|x - y| < 2\varepsilon$ from the triangle
   inequality. This licenses the notation $\lim x_k$.
4. **`lem-convergent-implies-bounded`** (lemma, direct). A convergent sequence is
   bounded. Take $\varepsilon = 1$, get $K$; the first $K$ terms form a finite
   set with a maximum by `lem-finite-set-has-max`; bound by
   $\max\{|x_0|,\dots,|x_{K-1}|, |x|+1\}$. **This is the item that consumes
   `lem-finite-set-has-max`, so cite it rather than reproving it.**
5. **`lem-convergent-implies-cauchy`** (lemma, direct). Convergent implies
   Cauchy, by the triangle inequality with $\varepsilon/2$. The **converse is
   completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ and belongs to the next page**; say so in prose
   without a wikilink, or note that the published `thm-reals-cauchy-complete`
   already proves it for the Cauchy construction and cite that.
6. **`lem-null-times-bounded`** (lemma, direct). If $x_k \to 0$ and $(y_k)$ is
   bounded then $x_k y_k \to 0$. Note this does **not** need $(y_k)$ to converge,
   which is exactly why it is stated separately, and it is the workhorse of the
   product rule.
7. **`thm-algebra-of-limits`** (theorem, direct). LANDMARK. If $x_k \to x$ and
   $y_k \to y$ then $x_k + y_k \to x + y$; $c x_k \to cx$;
   $x_k y_k \to xy$; and, provided $y \ne 0$ and $y_k \ne 0$ for all $k$,
   $x_k / y_k \to x / y$. Skeletons: sum by $\varepsilon/2$; product by
   $x_ky_k - xy = x_k(y_k - y) + y(x_k - x)$ with `lem-convergent-implies-bounded`
   and item 6; quotient by first proving $1/y_k \to 1/y$, for which you must
   first show $|y_k| \ge |y|/2$ eventually. **The quotient case is where proofs
   usually cheat; do the eventual lower bound explicitly.**
8. **`lem-limit-preserves-order`** (lemma, contradiction). If $x_k \le y_k$
   eventually and both converge then $\lim x_k \le \lim y_k$. In particular
   $x_k \ge 0$ eventually gives $\lim x_k \ge 0$. Contradiction: if
   $\lim y_k < \lim x_k$, take $\varepsilon$ a third of the gap.
9. **`thm-squeeze`** (theorem, direct). If $a_k \le x_k \le b_k$ eventually and
   $a_k \to L$ and $b_k \to L$ then $x_k \to L$. Direct from the definition;
   no need to route through item 8.
10. **`lem-limit-abs`** (lemma, direct). $x_k \to x \Rightarrow |x_k| \to |x|$,
    by `cor-of-reverse-triangle`. Note the converse fails, and that
    $|x_k| \to 0 \iff x_k \to 0$ is the one case where it does not.
11. **`def-divergence-to-infinity`** (definition). $x_k \to +\infty$ when for
    every $M$ there is $K$ with $x_k > M$ for all $k \ge K$; dually $-\infty$.
    Stress that this is **divergence**, that $+\infty$ is not a real number and
    no limit in the sense of `def-real-limit` is being claimed, and that the
    notation is an abbreviation.
12. **`lem-reciprocal-of-null-diverges`** (lemma, direct). For $x_k > 0$:
    $x_k \to 0 \iff 1/x_k \to +\infty$. Uses `lem-of-inverse-positive` and
    `prop-of-reciprocal-order`. Note positivity is essential and give the reason
    (without it, $(-1)^k / k$).
13. **`lem-index-map-grows`** (lemma, induction). A strictly increasing
    $n : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ satisfies $n_k \ge k$ for all $k$. Induction
    with `lem-nat-discrete`. Small, and every subsequence argument in the library
    will cite it.
14. **`lem-subsequence-inherits-limit`** (lemma, direct). Every subsequence of a
    convergent sequence converges to the same limit, via item 13. Consequently a
    sequence with two subsequences converging to different limits diverges, which
    is the standard divergence test.
15. **`fs-bounded-implies-convergent`** (false-statement). FALSE. Refuted by
    $x_k = (-1)^k$: bounded, and by item 14 it cannot converge since its even and
    odd subsequences have limits $1$ and $-1$. The true statement in this
    direction is Bolzano-Weierstrass, on the next page; mention it in prose
    without a wikilink.
16. **`fs-limit-preserves-strict-inequality`** (false-statement). FALSE:
    $x_k < y_k$ for all $k$ implies $\lim x_k < \lim y_k$. Refuted by
    $x_k = 0$, $y_k = 1/(k+1)$, whose limits are both $0$, using
    `thm-of-archimedean`. Item 8 with $\le$ is the correct statement, and the
    reason is that the order is not open at the limit.
17. **`fs-subsequence-convergence-implies-convergence`** (false-statement).
    FALSE: if some subsequence converges then the sequence converges. Refuted by
    $(-1)^k$ again, whose even subsequence converges. The correct statement needs
    **every** subsequence, or a Cauchy hypothesis.
18. **`rem-sequence-conventions`** (remark). Indexing from $0$ versus $1$ and why
    it never matters (item 2); "eventually" and "frequently" as the negations of
    each other applied to the complement; the convention that $\lim$ is written
    only after uniqueness (item 3) is known; and the fact that
    `def-real-limit` quantifies over rational $\varepsilon$.
