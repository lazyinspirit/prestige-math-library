Abstract

In a (1+1)-dimensional QFT with non-invertible symmetry, the fusion rules of topolog-
ical lines are often the most accessible data, yet they do not fully determine whether the
symmetry is anomaly-free, i.e. whether it admits trivially gapped phases. Such phases are
characterized by fiber functors of the corresponding fusion category. Building on Tannaka
duality, we extract fiber functors from the fusion ring alone by solving simultaneously for
monoidal structure maps and tensor automorphisms. This allows us to find categorifications
admitting a fiber functor without solving the pentagon equations directly. We test this pro-
cedure on the known Tambara–Yamagami ring of Z2 × Z2. Our main result concerns the
rank-11 fusion ring shared by the representation categories of the two extraspecial groups of
order 27, which has a multiplicity 3 channel and is relevant to self-triality. In both cases we
find more than one fiber functor, i.e. several non-invertible SPT phases. We use the explicit
data of these fiber functors to derive the F-symbols of the two representation categories
in a compact form depending on a nondegenerate alternating bicharacter and an invertible
object ϵ. Using the reconstructed data, we compute the anomalous algebra on the interface
between two different SPT phases.

Introduction

Discrete internal bosonic symmetries of two-dimensional quantum field theories (QFTs) are im-
plemented by topological line defects and are mathematically described by fusion categories [1–3].
The fusion of simple lines is encoded by the fusion ring K(C) of the symmetry category C. A
symmetry is non-invertible when at least one simple line has no fusion inverse; otherwise it is an
ordinary, possibly anomalous, group-like symmetry. The fusion ring does not specify the sym-
metry completely. A fusion category also includes F-symbols, which encode the associativity of
line fusion. Inequivalent choices of F-symbols, modulo changes of basis and automorphisms of
the fusion ring, therefore define distinct categorifications of the same fusion ring.
A symmetry category C is called anomaly-free if it admits a monoidal fiber functor [4–6]

F : C →Vec .

The existence of this functor depends both on the fusion ring K(C) and on a compatibility
condition between the F-symbols of C and the monoidal structure of the functor F. Every fusion
category admitting a fiber functor is equivalent to Rep(H) for a finite-dimensional semisimple
Hopf algebra H [7]. When H is noncommutative, Rep(H) contains non-invertible simple objects.
In 1+1 dimensions, a monoidal fiber functor of C corresponds to a C-symmetric gapped phase
with a unique bulk vacuum [4, 8]. Conversely, the absence of a fiber functor is an obstruction
to the existence of such a phase. A given symmetry may admit several inequivalent symmetric
gapped phases that cannot be connected by a symmetry-preserving deformation without crossing
a phase transition. These are symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phases [9–14]. For a unitary,
bosonic symmetry group G, two-dimensional G-SPT phases are classified by the second group
cohomology H2(G, U(1)) [13]. For a non-invertible symmetry, this classification is generalized
by fiber functors: the monoidally inequivalent fiber functors on C label the distinct C-symmetric
SPT phases. Explicit realizations of non-invertible SPT phases have been constructed in lattice
models based on cluster states, anyon chains, and tensor networks [15–21]. In many of these con-
structions, the monoidal structure maps of the fiber functor are crucial for explicitly constructing
the corresponding C-symmetric state.
The relation between fusion rules, associators, and fiber functors leads to a concrete recon-
struction problem: starting from the fusion rules alone, can one reconstruct the categorifications

2


that admit a fiber functor, including their associators, and determine their inequivalent fiber
functors? In this work we develop and apply a bootstrap that answers this question and thereby
determines the associated non-invertible SPT phases for the categories we reconstruct.
The
only categorical input is the fusion ring: neither a categorification C nor a representative of its
F-symbols is assumed. This makes the bootstrap physically useful: fusion rules can often be
obtained directly from the composition of topological lines, whereas F-symbols require access
to their junction data and are typically computed in more controlled settings, such as rational
conformal field theories and free theories [22–27]. The procedure is a constructive application of
Tannaka duality [28].1 Standard Tannaka reconstruction starts from a specified fusion category
C, including its monoidal structure, together with the forgetful fiber functor, and reconstructs
the corresponding finite-dimensional semisimple Hopf algebra. When C = Rep(G), the finite
group G is recovered as the group of tensor automorphisms of the forgetful functor. In this
setting, the monoidal structure maps of the forgetful functor are the Clebsch–Gordan maps, or
3j-symbols, while the F-symbols are the corresponding 6j-symbols and can be reconstructed
from the monoidal structure maps.
We reverse this logic. Starting from the fusion ring alone, we treat the monoidal structure
maps and their tensor automorphisms as unknowns and solve the coupled consistency squares.
When the square equations leave part of the monoidal structure undetermined, we additionally
require that the maps admit a consistent set of F-symbols, whose existence is otherwise guar-
anteed. Solving this full system modulo the allowed changes of basis determines all compatible
fiber functors. The resulting monoidal structure maps determine the F-symbols, and Tannaka
reconstruction yields the corresponding finite group or Hopf algebra.
When the tensor automorphisms form a group of the maximal order allowed by the fusion ring,
the solutions reconstruct a category Rep(G). For the group representation categories considered
below, the resulting numbers of monoidally inequivalent fiber functors agree with the classifi-
cation of [32], and we determine explicitly how the different solutions are related by monoidal
equivalences.
We apply the procedure to three fusion rings. The fusion ring of Rep(S3) provides a first check:
the bootstrap reproduces the expected unique solution and reconstructs S3 as its group of tensor
automorphisms. We then consider the Tambara–Yamagami fusion ring associated with Z2 × Z2,
which appears physically in self-duality symmetries [33, 23, 34–36]. The solutions satisfying the
maximality condition give four labeled parametric forms: three reconstruct Rep(D8) and one
reconstructs Rep(Q8). At fixed associator, they yield three and one inequivalent fiber functors,
respectively. Relaxing maximality also recovers Rep(H8) and its unique fiber functor, with H8
the Kac–Paljutkin Hopf algebra. This shows that the method recovers representation categories

3
Bootstrapping fiber functors

As reviewed in the previous section, standard Tannaka reconstruction starts from a fusion cat-
egory equipped with a fiber functor. In the case of Rep(G), after choosing bases, the monoidal
structure maps Jab are the Clebsch–Gordan decomposition maps. Once these maps are known,
the group of tensor automorphisms is reconstructed by solving the square equations (2.24).
Here we reverse this logic. We start from a fusion category C, assuming that only its fusion
rules and the existence of at least one fiber functor are known. We take neither a choice of
F-symbols for C nor the monoidal structure maps of a fiber functor as input. The dimensions
of the vector spaces Va are determined by requiring compatibility with the fusion rules, while
both the monoidal structure maps Jab and the components τa of the tensor automorphisms are
treated as unknown matrices. We then solve the square equations (2.24) for every allowed fusion
channel, requiring the full maps Jab and the matrices τa to be invertible.

The monoidal structure maps Jab, the tensor automorphisms τ = {τa}a∈I, and the square

The square equations, together with the requirement that Eq. (2.19) admit F-symbols —

a pair. Different solutions may nevertheless describe equivalent pairs, so we must compare the
resulting presentations. For this comparison, we use the equivalences described in Section 2.2.
Fusion-space basis transformations change the gauge representative of the associator, whereas
basis transformations in the Va identify monoidally isomorphic fiber functors.
Accordingly, after reconstructing the F-symbols associated with each parametric form, we
first determine which of them are related by fusion-space basis transformations and relabelings
of the simple objects induced by automorphisms of the fusion ring. Parametric forms whose F-
symbols are not related in this way define different fusion categories. When they are related, we
bring their F-symbols to a common representative and then compare the corresponding maps Jab
modulo changes of basis in the spaces Va. This determines the monoidal-isomorphism classes of
fiber functors on the fixed category. Transformations that leave the chosen F-symbols invariant
define monoidal autoequivalences of the category and may permute these classes.

5.3
Reconstruction of the F-symbols and gauge invariants

As in Section 4.3, we derive the F-symbols from the eighteen parametric forms of the J-maps.
We identify two gauge invariants of the resulting associators and show that the free parameters
can be chosen so that all F-symbols admit a uniform expression in terms of these invariants.

From parametric J-maps to associators.
For any invertible choice of the J-maps within

• It corresponds to the monoidal functor mapping the UV symmetry C to the trivial IR

topological line under this RG flow. The monoidal maps ensure that the associativity of
defect fusion in the UV is compatible with the (trivial) associativity in the IR.

understood through anomaly inflow: the interface carries the relative anomaly determined by
the two bulk phases. This principle applies in any spacetime dimension.

7
Conclusions and outlook

The examples studied in this work show how the passage from fusion rules to non-invertible SPT
phases can be carried out explicitly. The bootstrap determines both the associators and their
compatible fiber functors, so that the dependence of the SPT classification on data beyond the
fusion ring can be examined directly.
For the Tambara–Yamagami ring associated with Z2
2, the reconstruction agrees with the
known classification of categorifications admitting a fiber functor.
The example of Rep(H8)
shows that relaxing the maximality condition can allow the bootstrap to recover fiber functors
for categories that are not representation categories of finite groups.
For the extraspecial fusion ring, the eighteen parametric forms obtained under the maximality
condition describe two representation categories up to fusion-ring relabelings. This classification
must be distinguished from that of fiber functors within each category, which label distinct SPT
phases. The fiber functors compatible with the reconstructed associator are labeled by characters
satisfying ϑ(ϵ) = 1, yielding nine classes for Rep(Esp3) and three for Rep(Esp9), in agreement
with the group-theoretical classification.
The invariants (ϵ, ι) give a uniform description of all reconstructed F-symbols, including the
multiplicity-three blocks. For ϵ = 1, these reproduce the triality associator of Ref. [45] up to
gauge. For ϵ ̸= 1, we obtain explicit F-symbols for Rep(Esp9), including all multiplicity-three
blocks, a case not covered in that work. We verify the pentagon equations exactly for all eighteen
labeled forms.

68


We use the monoidal structure maps to explicitly compute the algebras M3(C)⊕3 acting
on interfaces between distinct SPT phases.
Each algebra has three irreducible sectors, each
supporting a symmetry-protected qutrit of states. The explicit representations determine the
invertible charges of these states and the action of the non-invertible symmetry operators.
These results leave several questions for future work. A first direction is to extend the explicit
bootstrap analysis to extraspecial groups of order p3, with p an odd prime greater than three.
The p = 3 results suggest that the forms satisfying the maximality condition are parametrized by
(ϵ, ι) ∈Γp ×F∗
p, where Γp ≃Z2
p is the group of invertible objects. Although the group-theoretical
fiber-functor counts are known, deriving this parametrization from the bootstrap equations would
explain how the reconstruction extends to arbitrary odd primes. A further goal is to extract ϵ
from the associator and establish the parametrization of compatible fiber functors by characters
satisfying ϑ(ϵ) = 1.
A second direction is to study systematically the solutions obtained by relaxing the maximal-
ity condition. The H8 example motivates a search for further Hopf-algebra categorifications of
the extraspecial fusion ring, guided by the classification of semisimple Hopf algebras of dimension
p3. The case p = 3 will be treated in a forthcoming paper. Applying the same procedure to other
integral fusion rings would also clarify the scope and computational efficiency of the bootstrap,
both in separating categorifications and in detecting obstructions to a fiber functor.
A further physical objective is to realize the reconstructed data in lattice models and matrix-
product-operator constructions. For Rep(Esp3) and Rep(Esp9), a common microscopic realiza-
tion of their nine and three SPT phases, respectively, would allow their interfaces to be studied
within a single framework. The triplet degeneracies and explicit symmetry action derived here
provide concrete predictions for such models, connecting the categorical reconstruction to ob-
servable properties of non-invertible SPT phases.
