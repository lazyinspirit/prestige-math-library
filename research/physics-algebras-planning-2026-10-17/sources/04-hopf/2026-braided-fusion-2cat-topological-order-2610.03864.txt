Realizing Braided Fusion 2-Categories as (3+1)D Mixed-State Topological Orders

Ryohei Kobayashi,1, • Abhinav Prem,2, ↓and Matthew Yu3, ⇓

1Department of Applied Physics, University of Tokyo, Bunkyo-ku, Tokyo 113-8656, Japan
2Physics Program, Bard College, 30 Campus Road, Annandale-on-Hudson, New York 12504, USA
3School of Natural Sciences, Institute for Advanced Study, 1 Einstein Drive, Princeton, NJ 08540, USA

We construct large families of mixed-state topological orders in (3+1)D, including intrinsically
mixed-state topological orders. We realize these theories by relating the data parameterizing braided
fusion 2-categories to the strong higher-form symmetries and their anomalies that characterize
mixed-state topological order. We focus here on bosonic theories, which include theories which
admit an emergent fermion in the spectrum of line operators. Our general framework is exempli-
fied on the lattice, where we present concrete models and identify how the data of braided fusion
2-categories are physically encoded. We then formalize this data and show that every braided fusion
2-category arises from a general construction (decoherence followed by strong and weak gauging)
as the category of topological operators of a (3+1)D mixed state, with intrinsically mixed-state
topological orders distinguished by the presence of strong higher-form symmetries whose anomalies
cannot be realized in any (3+1)D gapped ground state. Notably, we show that (3+1)D admits a new
mechanism with no (2+1)D counterpart: intrinsically mixed-state order whose topological operators
braid non-degenerately, but which carries a gravitational anomaly.

I.
INTRODUCTION

Topological states of matter provide canonical re-
sources for fault-tolerant quantum information stor-
age and processing. Their recent preparation on pro-
grammable quantum simulators [1–7] has rendered the
fate of topological pure states under noise a question of
immediate practical importance. It is becoming increas-
ingly clear, however, that decoherence does not merely
degrade patterns of entanglement present in pure initial
states: rather, the resulting density matrices can host

new forms of order without pure-state counterpart. In-
deed, the distinction between “strong” and “weak” sym-
metries for mixed states [8, 9] opens the door to strong-
to-weak spontaneous symmetry breaking (SWSSB) [10–
14] and intrinsically average symmetry protected topo-
logical (iASPT) mixed-state phases [15–23].
From the perspective of quantum error correction, a
fundamental question is understanding how logical in-
formation stored in topologically ordered pure states gets
corrupted by noise. For the two dimensional toric code,
for instance, the encoded information remains recover-
able for noise below a certain threshold, beyond which
it is no longer accessible, signaling a phase transition
in the decodable information encoded in the decohered
mixed state [24, 25]. Such transitions mark the destruc-
tion of the parent topological order; more surprisingly,
decoherence can also generate new forms of topologi-
cal order. Indeed, recent work has shown that subject-
ing (2+1)D topologically ordered states to local noise
can produce intrinsically mixed-state topological order
(imTO), which is a form of topological order that can-
not arise in the ground states of any 2D gapped local
Hamiltonian [26–28]. Such mixed states are character-
ized in terms of their strong one-form symmetries and
their corresponding self/mixed anomalies, and admit a
partial classification in terms of non-modular braided fu-
sion categories [26, 27, 29].
Here, the anomalies of strong higher-form symme-
tries1 play a central role: they underpin the quantum

belonging to the same phase when they are related by
(distinct) finite-depth local quantum channels acting in
both directions. Since a state invariant under anomalous
strong higher-form symmetries cannot be generated from
a trivial state via a finite-depth local quantum chan-
nel, these symmetries and their anomalies furnish ro-
bust data characterizing mixed-state phases [29, 32, 33].
In fact, anomalous strong higher-form symmetries en-
force intrinsic long-range entanglement: any state invari-
ant under such anomalous symmetries has exponentially
small overlap with every short-range entangled state [29].

While mixed-state topological order remains far from
well-understood in general, one systematic approach
is to subject pure-state topological orders (arising in
ground states of gapped local Hamiltonians) to deco-
herence channels and analyze the symmetries and their
anomalies of the resulting mixed states. We can then
distinguish between two kinds of mixed-state topologi-
cal orders. In the first case, the mixed state is only a
“weakly” deformed version of the parent pure state and
the strong symmetries and their anomalies remain in-
tact. The mixed state is then two-way channel connected
to the parent pure state and, in the current taxonomy,
is referred to as simply carrying mixed-state topologi-
cal order (mTO). In contrast, the mixed state could re-
sult from subjecting a gapped ground state to strong
decoherence such that the resulting mixed state cannot
be two-way channel connected to the parent pure state.
For instance, some of the initial strong symmetries can
be broken to weak symmetries, with the resulting alge-
bra of remaining strong symmetry anomalies impossible
in any gapped ground state. Mixed states carrying such
strong higher-form symmetry anomalies are said to re-
alize imTO. This distinction can be re-stated physically
in terms of remote detectability: incoherently proliferat-
ing an excitation can leave behind operators which braid
trivially with all others. When such “transparent” op-
erators are themselves anomalous (e.g., carry fermionic
self-statistics), they obstruct a pure-state realization out-
right, since the anomaly prevents two-way channel con-
nectivity to a trivial state and since remote detectability
(or braiding nondegeneracy) is expected to hold in the
ground state of any gapped local Hamiltonian in any
dimension. In (2+1)D, this breakdown is precisely the
failure of the anyon theory to be modular [26, 27]. Nei-
ther our definition of a mixed-state phase nor the prin-
ciple whose violation defines imTO makes reference to
spatial dimension, however, and both accordingly carry
over unchanged to higher dimensions.

In this paper, we propose a general framework for
characterizing mixed-state topological order in (3+1)D,
and use it to construct imTOs whose excitations are ei-
ther all bosons or contain an emergent fermion in the
spectrum of line operators. Our starting point is the

ansatz that the topological operators of a (3+1)D mTO
form a braided fusion 2-category which need not be non-
degenerate. This allows for the presence of transpar-
ent operators i.e., operators with trivial mutual braid-
ing with every operator in the theory. Such an operator
need not be trivial on its own: it can still carry non-
trivial self-statistics, as with a fermionic particle or a
fermionic loop. Such anomalous transparent operators
violate the principle of remote detectability, prevent two-
way channel connectivity to a pure-state TO, and enforce
long-range entanglement. This ansatz converts a ques-
tion about density matrices into one about categorical
data: mathematically, mTO in (3+1)D (two-way channel
connected to a pure state in our taxonomy) corresponds
to a non-degenerate braided fusion 2-category D, where
a faithful symmetry grading can be forgetfully removed
to recover pure-state topological data. imTO in (3+1)D
arises in two distinct ways. Generalizing the (2+1)D con-
struction, it is represented by a degenerate braided fu-
sion 2-category B whose transparent operators generate
anomalous strong symmetries; these obstruct two-way
channel connectivity to pure-state topological order and
enforce intrinsic long-range entanglement [29, 32, 33]. Al-
ternatively, B may be non-degenerate but carry a grav-
itational anomaly that no strictly (3+1)D ground state
can realize; this provides a novel route to imTO that has
no counterpart in (2+1)D. Note that degeneracy alone is
insufficient: transparent operators that are anomaly-free
bosons arise from essentially classical ensembles, which
are two-way connected to trivial states.
In (3+1)D, topological excitations are both point-like
and loop-like, such that topological operators organize
into a braided fusion 2-category rather than a braided
fusion category, opening more mechanisms for generat-
ing imTO via decoherence than are available in (2+1)D.
For instance, the decohered object in (3+1)D can be a
loop and, since loops in a purely bosonic theory can carry
nontrivial self-statistics (and are thus dubbed fermionic
loops) [34–37], decohering the bosonic Z2 toric code al-
ready yields a nontrivial imTO [29]. Most strikingly,
we construct an imTO of a distinct kind: it hosts a
fermionic particle and a fermionic loop with nontriv-
ial mutual braiding, so that every operator is detected
by braiding and remote detectability holds2. It realizes
the (3+1)D Z2 gauge theory with a global gravitational
anomaly [35, 36], which is believed not to admit a pure-
state realization in strictly three spatial dimensions with-
out coupling to a four-dimensional bulk. This theory is
thus intrinsically mixed not because of transparent oper-
ators, but because of its gravitational anomaly. Note that
since fusion 2-categories with no nontrivial line operators
are necessarily grouplike [38], every theory we encounter
can be obtained by gauging symmetries of a parent gauge

We exemplify our construction on the lattice, start-
ing with a ZN toric code and applying an error channel
that incoherently proliferates loops carrying an ’t Hooft
anomaly, resulting in a degenerate theory with transpar-
ent operators. We then gauge the 0-form symmetry G
of the degenerate theory, such that the non-anomalous
part N ⊂G of the gauged 0-form symmetry acts strongly
while the quotient H = G/N acts weakly. Note that even
if the left H action on the density matrix is anomalous,
one can still gauge H as a weak symmetry; gauging a
weak symmetry is also called “classical gauging,” which
amounts to taking a classical ensemble of gauge field con-
figurations [27]. Categorically, we show that the data ob-
tained by this procedure is exactly the data parametriz-
ing braided fusion 2-categories as presented in Theorem
1: a finite abelian group E together with a class β spec-
ifying the proliferated loops, a faithfully graded crossed
extension encoding the strong symmetry N, and a group
H = G/N acting by braided autoequivalences encoding
the weak one. In this language, pure-state (3+1)D TOs
arise from faithfully graded G-crossed braided fusion 2-
categories, for which equivariantization reinstates the
line operators and preserves nondegeneracy; mixed-state
TOs unlock symmetry structures with non-faithful grad-
ing, enabled by gauging weak symmetries with anoma-
lous left/right action.
We stress that this does not amount to a classifica-
tion of (3+1)D mixed-state phases under the two-way
channel equivalence relation (or the finer classification
under the finite-Markov length condition [39]). Our re-
sults rest on the Ansatz 1, which we motivate but do
not prove; we restrict throughout to bosonic theories
(but allow for emergent fermions); and, we do not es-
tablish that distinct categorical data necessarily label
states inequivalent under two-way channels, nor do we
capture chiral mixed-state phases or multi-loop braid-
ings. Our main result is to establish that every braided
fusion 2-category in the two families above is realized via
a single construction – incoherently proliferating possi-
bly anomalous excitations of an Abelian gauge theory,
followed by strong and weak 0-form gauging – provid-
ing a physical realization for this categorical data. We
implement this construction explicitly on the lattice for
representative examples, including a new imTO with no
(2+1)D counterpart.
This paper is organized as follows: In Sec. II, we in-
troduce (3+1)D imTOs from the point of view of lat-
tice models. In particular, we cover the constructions of
some bosonic theories that contain only bosonic point-
like excitations as well as emergent fermions. In Sec. III,
we abstract from these examples a general construction.
In Sec. IV, we study (3+1)D mTOs as braided fusion
2-categories, obtain the data parametrizing all such the-
ories, and show that it coincides with the data produced
by the construction of Sec. III. We collect some rele-

vant categorical facts about fusion 2-categories in Ap-
pendix A.

II.
LATTICE MODELS

We begin by example, describing various lattice con-
structions for (i)mTO in (3+1)D. The examples here
serve as the starting point from which we will develop the
general prescription for encoding every braided fusion 2-
category as the (i)mTO of some (3+1)D mixed state.
The key new example contained in this Section is an
imTO that hosts an emergent fermion and an emergent
fermionic loop, following which we present the general
construction in Sec. III.

The maximally decohered state cannot be two-way
connected to the ground state of any gapped local Hamil-
tonian in three spatial dimensions. In such a ground
state, remote detectability requires every nontrivial line
operator to be detected by braiding with some sur-
face operator. Here, however, the anomalous fermion
line braids trivially with all strong operators. Categor-
ically, the strong operators form the degenerate cat-
egory 2SVect, whose transparent fermion carries the
anomaly (3). This is precisely the situation in which
we expect two-way channel connectivity to a pure state
to fail, and it can be made sharp using the refined no-
tion of phase equivalence of Ref. [45]: locally reversible
channels preserve strong and weak higher-form symme-
tries together with their anomalies. In the fermionic toric
code, the fermion line has a mutual anomaly with the
strong membrane M(Σ), whereas here it braids nontriv-
ially only with the weak M(Σ). Hence, the maximally
decohered state realizes an imTO. It is the simplest mem-
ber of the family with emergent fermions: in the language
of Theorem 2 it corresponds to E, N, and H all trivial.
It also serves as the building block for the FcFl theory
of Sec. II D, whose layer A is the state constructed here.

Finally, note that Lf(γ) is an exact strong symmetry
for all p, so the anomaly-enforced long-range entangle-
ment persists throughout 0 ≤p ≤1. What distinguishes
weak from maximal decoherence is the fate of M(Σ).
For p sufficiently close to 1, we expect that the fermions
can be recovered by a quasi-local decoder, so that Nρ0
remains two-way connected to the fermionic toric code
(mTO). The imTO phase should then be separated from
it by a decoding transition at some finite threshold.

C.
Mixed topological order with a non-Abelian
fermionic loop

III.
GENERAL CONSTRUCTION OF (3+1)D
MIXED TOPOLOGICAL ORDER

The above lattice model examples are constructed by
the following procedure:

1. We first start with a pure state for copies of
ZN toric codes (with bosonic/fermionic quasipar-
ticles), and turn on an error channel to obtain
a mixed state ρ with strong ZN 1-form/2-form
symmetries. The strong symmetry of the result-
ing mixed state forms a braided fusion 2-category
which is either degenerate or non-degenerate but
anomalous. For instance, the strong symmetry of
the model in Sec. II A consists of a single fermionic
quasiparticle, and that in Sec. II B consists of a
single fermionic loop, both of which are transpar-
ent and form a degenerate theory. Meanwhile, the
strong symmetry of the model in Sec. II D forms
the non-degenerate but anomalous theory T .

2. The above mixed state ρ then has a 0-form sym-
metry, which may be strong or weak. We obtain
a new imTO by gauging these strong/weak 0-form
symmetries. For instance, the model in Sec. II C
is obtained by gauging the weak Z2 SWAP sym-
metry of the two copies of intrinsically mixed Z2
toric codes, producing a theory with a non-Abelian
fermionic loop.

The above construction motivates a general procedure
to achieve imTOs in (3+1)D. In fact, a suitable gen-
eralization, considering all algebraic structures of finite
strong symmetries in (3+1)D TOs, yields a general con-
struction which, as we show in Sec. IV, produces all
braided fusion 2-categories.

IV.
MIXED TOPOLOGICAL ORDERS REALIZE
ALL BRAIDED FUSION 2-CATEGORIES

The theories presented thus far reveal a close connec-
tion between the topological operators of imTOs and
braided fusion 2-categories. By adopting the following
ansatz for mTOs, we place this relationship on a sys-
tematic footing, enabling more general statements about
their characterization using category theory. This will be
the focus of the remainder of the paper.

Ansatz 1. The strong topological operators of a (3+1)D
mixed state topological order form a braided fusion 2-
category.

Notation. In this Ansatz, and for all the examples in
this paper, we are only concerned with bosonic topolog-
ical orders, which amounts to the fact that the theory
does not need to couple to a spin structure to be de-
fined. Even though the topological order is bosonic, there
can still be emergent fermions in the spectrum of oper-
ators as discussed in Sec. II. To distinguish whether or
not a theory has an emergent fermion we use the follow-
ing terminology. In the case when all line operators of
a bosonic topological order are bosons (loops may still
carry fermionic statistics), the theory will be described
by a “bosonic” braided fusion 2-category. In the case
when the spectrum contains emergent fermions, the the-
ory will be described by a “emergently-fermionic” (EF)
braided fusion 2-category. We will not be concerned with
inherently fermionic (i)mTOs in this paper, which re-
quire a spin structure to be defined.

V.
CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE
DIRECTIONS

In this paper, we have developed a general frame-
work for constructing and characterizing mixed-state
topological order in (3+1)D. Motivated by lattice con-
structions, we have proposed an ansatz under which
the topological operators of a (3+1)D mTO organize
into a braided fusion 2-category, generalizing the pro-
posed correspondence between (2+1)D mTO and non-
modular braided tensor categories. Within this ansatz,
we have provided a general recipe, built from an Abelian
gauge theory subjected to decoherence and followed by
weak/strong 0-form gauging, whose output exhausts all
bosonic braided fusion 2-categories (including those with
emergent fermions), and we have implemented it ex-
plicitly on the lattice for representative examples. Ev-
ery such category hence admits a mixed-state realization
along the lines we have described. We have further iden-
tified when these categories describe intrinsically mixed-
state order: either the transparent operators carry a non-
trivial anomaly, or the category is non-degenerate but
carries the gravitational anomaly π
�
w2w3, as for the


13

FcFl theory (crucially, degeneracy alone does not suf-
fice). Neither is expected in the ground state of a gapped
local Hamiltonian in three spatial dimensions. This gives
a proposed categorical criterion for distinguishing imTO
from mTO in (3+1)D.
Our work raises a number of open questions and fu-
ture directions. The most pressing open question is the
extent to which the data of Theorems 1 and 2 furnish
a classification of (3+1)D mixed-state topological or-
der, rather than a means of construction. Our central
ansatz asserts that the operators of an mTO organize
into a braided fusion 2-category, but we have not shown
that every operator algebra realizing a given category
must arise this way, nor have we addressed when two
sets of data (E, β, N, H, ρ) (or their fermionic counter-
parts (E, κ, ς, N, H, ρ)) describe the same mixed-state
phase i.e., are related by finite-depth local quantum
channels acting in both directions. Settling this would
require fully understanding the equivalence relation that
two-way channel connectivity induces on the categori-
cal data, alongside a complete classification of braided
fusion 2-categories in their own right.
One can also ask a more restricted question. Rather
than classifying all mTOs consistent with Ansatz 1, one
could aim to classify only those that arise from decoher-
ing a (3+1)D topological order. Here, one starts from
a pure-state topological order and views local decoher-
ence as the process that can remove remote detectabil-
ity. Physically, this amounts to classifying pairs of a
parent pure state and a noise channel up to a suit-
able notion of equivalence; categorically, it should cor-
respond to classifying the braided fusion 2-categories of
Theorems 1 and 2 up to braided and monoidal equiv-
alence, presumably a more tractable problem than the
unconstrained classification discussed above. Relatedly,
while our construction produces all braided fusion 2-
categories at the level of categorical data, explicit lat-
tice realizations for general data (for instance, arbitrary
loop anomalies β ∈H5(B2E; C×), or weak gauging with
anomalous actions) remain to be constructed.
More broadly, we can relax the conditions on the
ground states from which our construction starts. In
(2+1)D, gapped ground states of local Hamiltonians are
expected to realize only ordinary topological order; in
(3+1)D, by contrast, gapped ground states can also re-
alize fracton order, which lies outside the conventional
TQFT paradigm altogether. Decohering a fracton phase
can likewise generate new forms of mixed-state order:
since braiding non-degeneracy is expected to hold even
for fracton phases, mixed-state fracton orders that vio-
late this principle will encode intrinsically mixed forms
of fracton order. Formalizing this first requires an alge-
braic theory of braiding and fusion for fracton phases,
on which there has been partial recent progress [61–63].
Separately, rather than obtaining mTOs by decohering
a pure ground state, one may ask whether (3+1)D (i)

mTOs can instead arise as steady states of open-system
dynamics, extending recent work on steady-state SPT
and topological order [64–66].
A further natural question concerns chirality. As re-
cently emphasized in the (2+1)D setting [67, 68], mixed
states that appear chiral under certain definitions of chi-
rality can fail to be chiral under other definitions, and
the usual diagnostics of chirality for pure-state topo-
logical order do not carry over unmodified to mTOs.
This question is sharpened in (3+1)D by the fact that,
while (3+1)D topological orders always admit a gapped
boundary, this need not be true for a general mTO: since
a generic braided fusion 2-category need not arise as
the center of any fusion 2-category, it need not admit
a gapped boundary at all. Understanding chirality for
mTOs in a precise physical sense, and, relatedly, classi-
fying which mTOs admit boundaries at all (gapped or
otherwise) and what data characterizes them, will re-
quire ideas beyond those available for pure states.
Finally, it is worth revisiting notions of orientability
for mixed-state theories. For pure-state (3+1)D topo-
logical order, framed theories correspond to nondegener-
ate braided fusion 2-categories, while oriented theories
require the additional data trivializing the associated
anomaly (the categorical analogue of the distinction be-
tween nondegenerate braided fusion categories and mod-
ular tensor categories in (2+1)D). Since decoherence de-
forms what is a priori a framed pure-state theory, it is
natural to ask whether the resulting mTOs should like-
wise be regarded as framed, and if so, what plays the
role of a framing anomaly in this setting. Formalizing
the notion of a mixed-state TQFT along the lines of
Ref. [69] and using it to settle this question would help
place mTOs on the same functorial footing as their pure-
state counterparts.

Appendix A: Topological Order and Fusion
2-Categories

In order to describe the operators of a (3+1)D mTO,
or pure-state topological order, we use the language of
fusion 2-categories. A fusion 2-category categorifies a
fusion 1-category, the latter which is relevant in the
study of symmetries and topological order in (2+1)D
TOs. In recent years, the study of fusion 2-categories
has led to advancements of our understanding of many
important areas including: the study of finite symme-
tries in (2+1)D, (3+1)D and (4+1)D topological or-
der [51, 54, 58, 70–78], anomalous (3+1)D symmetry
enriched topological order, non-invertible duality sym-
metry in (3+1)D, and how to prove the existence of
a minimal non-degenerate extension for braided fusion
1-categories. Furthermore, their classification has been
given in [79], with the main result revealing that the
purely 2-categorical part is easier to deal with than
one might think: it just reduces to group theoretical
and cohomological data. In this Appendix, we introduce
the mathematical formalisms and background regarding
both bosonic and emergent fermionic braided fusion 2-
categories. For the purposes of conveying physical intu-
ition, we start with only bosonic fusion 2-categories and
we relegate the discussion of how the cohomological data
changes when there is an emergent fermion in the spec-
trum for Sec A 4.

1.
Details on Fusion Categories

We give a quick account of the details and terminology
associated to fusion n-categories (over C). For the reader
that wants an account of how to rigorously define fusion
n-categories in general we recommend [74, Section 3.1],
and [60, 80, 81].

• An n-category C is called locally finite semisimple
if for every object X ∈C, ΩXC := EndC(X) is
a multifusion (n −1)-category. In particular, every
k-morphism for 0 < k < n has both a left and
a right adjoint. We will denote the looping at the
identity object in C by ΩC.

• An n-category C is called semisimple if it is lo-
cally finite semisimple, and it is Cauchy complete
in the sense of [80], that is, every n-condensation
monad splits and direct sums for objects exist.

Cauchy completion is the statement of conden-
sation completion: i.e. we include all possible n-
dimensional topological defects that can be con-
structed by condensing/gauging topological de-
fects living inside an n-dimensional topological de-
fect X, which forms the multifusion (n −1) cate-
gory ΩXC.

• An object X of a semisimple n-category C is called
simple if ΩXC is fusion.

This means that there are no genuine topological
point-like defects on X except for multiples of the
identity local operator.

• Two simple objects are called connected if there
exists a non-zero 1-morphism between them. If C
is locally finite, being connected defines an equiv-
alence relation on the set of equivalence classes of
simple objects of C, and we write π0(C) for the
corresponding quotient.

Physically, two n-dimensional topological defects
are connected if there exists a non-zero topological
interface between them. Two topological defects
that are connected are also related by condensa-
tions, or in other words either of the two defects
can be obtained by gauging topological sub-defects
living on the other defect. Thus, π0(C) charac-
terizes gauge inequivalent classes of topological n-
dimensional defects. We note that having multi-
ple objects which live in the same component is
not a feature of fusion 1-categories. This is readily
seen, as there are no nontrivial morphisms between
anyons of different type in a (2+1)D TO. The next
definition categorifies the notion of finiteness.

• A finite semisimple n-category is a semisimple n-
category such that π0(C) is finite. For n = 1, con-
nected components are exactly (equivalence classes
of) simple objects. For n = 2, every connected
component contains only finitely many simple ob-
jects [82].


18

• A multifusion n-category C is a monoidal finite
semisimple n-category that is rigid, i.e. every ob-
ject has a left and a right dual.

Physically, such an n-category describes topologi-
cal defects that can be bent into ∪and ∩shapes.

• A fusion n-category is a multifusion n-category
whose monoidal unit is simple.

Fusion n-categories are useful for describing and clas-
sifying topological orders, up to gapped boundary, due
to the axiomatization that topological order in (n + 1)D
are fusion n-categories with trivial center, enforcing re-
mote detectability. In terms of braided categories, one
can say that (n + 1)D TOs are given by nondegenerate
braided fusion (n −1)-categories. In (3+1)D, the con-
strained structure of braided fusion 2-categories makes
possible a systematic classification of (m)TOs, whereas

the analogous question in (2+1)D seems out of reach.

we now must contend with a 2Rep(G) action. Because
Rep(G) is symmetric monoidal, we can always take it-
