Algebra of free fermions: Classifying spaces, Hamiltonians, and computation

Tian Yuan∗and Yang Qi†

State Key Laboratory of Surface Physics and Department of Physics, Fudan University, Shanghai 200433, China
(Dated: September 4, 2026)

Research on topological phases of matter is a core field in modern condensed matter physics.
Free fermion systems, such as topological insulators and superconductors, have been studied using
the “tenfold way” and K-theory. Building on Kitaev’s idea of Ω-spectrum and classifying space, as
well as Freed–Moore’s K-theory, this work demonstrates that free fermionic systems form a genuine
G-Ω-spectrum and clarifies its connection to several distinct classification schemes appearing in
the physical literature.
By introducing the Z2-graded algebra AV
sym, the classification problem
for systems with general symmetries, including antilinear symmetries, antisymmetries, projective
representations, and point group symmetries, is turned into an extension problem in representation
theory. To solve this, a computational method for the Z2-graded Wedderburn–Artin decomposition
of AV
sym is developed. This decomposition not only yields a classification but also enables the explicit
construction of the corresponding Dirac Hamiltonian. Furthermore, a GAP programming package
has been developed to automate these calculations.

I.
INTRODUCTION

Topological phases of matter play a central role in
modern condensed matter physics. Among these, gapped
free fermionic systems encompassing topological insula-
tors and superconductors [1–14] constitute a fundamen-
tal and rigorously understood class. With the theoreti-
cal development of topological insulators and supercon-
ductors, a systematic topological classification of free
fermionic systems has become an urgent necessity. Al-
tland and Zirnbauer proposed the well-known tenfold
symmetry classes [15, 16].
Subsequently, the “tenfold
way” was connected to free fermionic systems via time-
reversal (T), particle-hole (C), and chiral (P) symme-
tries [17], leading to the establishment of the well-known
topological periodic table. Kitaev [18] synthesized these
phenomena using Clifford algebras and K-theory, thereby
introducing homotopy theory—a cornerstone of modern
mathematics—into the study of topological phases of
matter.
Despite the tremendous success of the existing pe-
riodic table, it becomes inadequate when more com-
plex symmetries—such as crystalline symmetries [19] and
magnetic space group symmetries [20]—must be taken
into account. Building upon Kitaev’s work, Freed and
Moore generalized the K-theoretic framework to incor-
porate arbitrary symmetries [21].
Their approach can
handle general antilinear symmetries and antisymmetries
(i.e., symmetries that anticommute with the Hamilto-
nian). Furthermore, it allows for projective representa-
tions of symmetries (e.g., in spin- 1

2 systems) and accom-
modates nontrivial symmetry actions on the spatial lat-
tice. For other related works, we refer the reader to Refs.
[22–25].
For systems with general symmetries, computing the
classification is highly nontrivial. For strong topological

rived from a generalized Clifford algebra extension prob-

Furthermore, strong topological phases
serve as the “root states” for computations in more gen-
eral settings; determining their classification paves the
way for calculating the general cases. For a specific class
of space groups, the complete classification problem can
be reduced to the classification of strong phases [27]. For
general crystalline symmetries, physicists typically em-
ploy symmetry-based indicator theory [28–30] (for frag-
ile topology beyond symmetry indicators, see [31]). How-
ever, the classification obtained via this method is incom-
plete. A complete classification requires the application
of the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence (AHSS) in
either real space or momentum space [32–34].
These previous works lack a method to explicitly
construct a model Hamiltonian, and require case-by-
case derivation to obtain the topological classification of
Dirac Hamiltonians.
To solve this problem, we intro-
duce an algorithm to explicitly calculate the Z2-graded
Wedderburn–Artin decomposition of the Z2-graded alge-
bra AV
sym, which serves as the algebra of Dirac Hamil-
tonians. As a byproduct of this decomposition, we can
obtain both the model Hamiltonians and the topological
classification of Dirac Hamiltonians for strong topologi-
cal insulators/superconductors in the most general set-
tings. This algorithm is implemented as a GAP package
[35], which is our main computational result and will be
heavily used in our upcoming work [36] on the boundary
states of topological insulators/superconductors.
Classification represents the lowest-order information
of topological phases of matter; however, many physi-
cal problems require more sophisticated data. In his pi-
oneering talks [37–39], Kitaev proposed a more refined
approach to characterize invertible topological orders,
which encompasses free fermionic systems as a special
case. Kitaev’s ideas were developed in the subsequent
works [40, 41]. His core idea is to utilize an Ω-spectrum
to study the classifying space of invertible topological
orders. An Ω-spectrum is a sequence of spaces Xn in-

ΩXn+1 ≃Xn.
From the perspective of modern ho-

an “Abelian group”[43]; that is, it is a homotopy type
endowed with a commutative multiplication structure.
Physically, Xn represents the space of n-dimensional

group structure on this space physically corresponds to
invertible stacking.
This perspective makes the valid-

The
difficulty in applying this approach lies in identifying a
concrete Ω-spectrum to characterize a specific type of in-
vertible topological order. In homotopy theory, there is a
one-to-one correspondence between Ω-spectra and gener-
alized cohomology theories. For many physical systems of
interest, their classifying spaces are not directly specified
by an Ω-spectrum, but rather by a generalized cohomol-
ogy theory.
Examples include interacting bosonic sys-
tems [44, 45], interacting fermionic systems [46], invert-
ible stabilizer codes [47], quantum cellular automata [48],
and invertible topological quantum field theories [49, 50].
For free fermionic systems, the classifying space corre-
sponds to the space of gapped Hamiltonians. Mathemat-
ically, this is precisely the Ω-spectrum associated with
the K-theory mentioned earlier. In the presence of sym-
metries, we need to consider the genuine G-Ω-spectrum
[27, 51]. A genuine G-Ω-spectrum is no longer a sequence
of spaces indexed by integers; rather, it consists of G-
spaces XV indexed by virtual real representations V of
the group G, satisfying the equivalence ΩV XV ⊕W ≃XW .
When G is the trivial group, its real representations are
determined solely by their dimensions, and we recover
the sequence of spaces indexed by integers.
Here, the
real representation V (or the virtual real representation
−V ) of G can be interpreted as the real space (or the mo-
mentum space). An ordinary Ω-spectrum can be viewed
as a mathematical structure that packages systems of all
dimensions together, whereas a genuine G-Ω-spectrum
further incorporates the different actions of symmetries
on space.

A.
Conventions and notations

We use K to denote a field; in this work, K is always
R or C. Clp,q is the Clifford algebra over R, and Clp,q or
Clp is the Clifford algebra over C, as defined in Sec. III D.
Asym = C[G, ω, s] is a Z2-graded algebra with grading
h, as defined in Sec. II B.
A vector space V that has an action of an algebra A is
called an A module. The corresponding representation of
A on V is the algebra homomorphism ρV : A →End(V ).
Module and representation are equivalent concepts in
principle; however, we distinguish them by referring to
the vector space V as the A module and to the algebra
homomorphism ρV as the representation of A.
When we use the word “space”, we refer to a topolog-
ical space or a homotopy type, and more generally a G
space or a G-homotopy type depending on the context.
∼= of spaces stands for (G-)homeomorphism, ≃of spaces
stands for (G-)homotopy equivalence. We use ΩX to de-
note the loop space of a space X with a basepoint. If V is
a G-representation over R, SV is the one-point compact-
ification of V with the point at infinity as the basepoint.
Similarly, we use ΩV X to denote the mapping space from
SV to X preserving the basepoint.

A.
Symmetries of free fermionic systems

In quantum mechanics, the symmetry of a system is
often described by group theory and group representa-
tion. In free fermionic systems, the symmetry behaves in
a more complicated way: we need to consider antilinear
symmetries, projective representations, and anticommu-
tation with the Hamiltonian. Thus, it is inconvenient to

use only the language of group theory; we need to gener-
alize the group to an algebra.

An algebra is a vector space with an associative and
unital multiplication. For each group G, there is an al-
gebra C[G] called the group algebra.
C[G] is spanned
by the elements g ∈G as a vector space, and the mul-
tiplication is given by g · h = gh.
The representation
theory of G is equivalent to the representation theory of
C[G]; therefore, we can replace G by C[G] in the study
of symmetries in quantum mechanics.

As a symmetry, C[G] only describes the linear (or uni-
tary) symmetry of the system. In general, the symmetry
can be antilinear (or antiunitary). Furthermore, the mul-
tiplication rule g · h = gh is also violated in many cases;
for example, the rotation symmetry of a spin- 1

2 system
only satisfies this multiplication rule up to a sign.

To describe these symmetries, we generalize C[G] to
a new algebra C[G, ω, s]. The group homomorphism s :
G →Z2 is called a Z2-grading on G. G0 = s−1(0) is
the subgroup of linear symmetries, and G1 = s−1(1) is
the subset of antilinear symmetries. ω is a two-cocycle,
i.e., [ω] ∈H2(G, U(1)). The coefficient group U(1) of the
cohomology has a G-action that is given by ga = a for
g ∈G0, and ga = ¯a for g ∈G1, where a ∈U(1), and ¯a is
the complex conjugate of a. The generators of C[G, ω, s]
are still g ∈G, but we have g·h = ω(g, h)gh for g, h ∈G,
and gi = −ig for g ∈G1.

Notice that the algebra C[G, ω, s] is not an algebra
over C.
The multiplication of a C algebra should be
C-bilinear, but we have gi = −ig for g ∈G1.
Thus,
C[G, ω, s] is actually an algebra over R: i ∈C is con-
sidered as an independent generator of this R algebra,
rather than just a scalar.

B.
(0 + 1)-dimensional gapped free fermionic
systems

A 0+1-d gapped free fermionic system is a vector space
with a Hamiltonian and a symmetry acting on it. The
requirement of a gap partitions the vector space into two
subspaces, of the occupied states and of the unoccupied
states. Since we only care about the topology, we should
focus on the partition itself but not the specific value of
energy. Thus, we assume the energy of all occupied states
to be −1, and the energy of all unoccupied states to be
1.
Under the assumption above, the Hamiltonian of the
gapped system is a linear map H such that H2 = 1.
If the system has symmetry, the vector space should be
a representation of the algebra Asym := C[G, ω, s]. To-
gether with the Hamiltonian H, we form a new algebra
Cl0,1 ⊗C[G, ω, s], where Cl0,1 is the Clifford algebra that
has one generator ˜e1 and satisfies the relation ˜e2
1 = 1.
There are symmetries anticommuting with the Hamil-
tonian H, for example, the chiral symmetry. Therefore,
we need to introduce a new grading on G that is given
by h : G →Z2. With this grading, C[G, ω, s] becomes a
Z2-graded algebra. A Z2-graded algebra is a Z2-graded
vector space equipped with a unital and associative mul-
tiplication which preserves the grading. There is a theory
of Z2-graded algebras similar to that of ordinary algebras,
as will be discussed in Sec. III. [53]
Now the algebra of the symmetry and the Hamilto-
nian becomes Cl0,1 ˆ⊗C[G, ω, s].

product of Z2-graded algebras; the generator ˜e1 ∈Cl0,1

has grading 1. The ordinary tensor product A ⊗B has
the property of a ⊗1 commuting with 1 ⊗b for a ∈A
and b ∈B. The super tensor product ˆ⊗has the property
of aˆ⊗1 anticommuting with 1ˆ⊗b for a ∈A1 and b ∈B1,

1ˆ⊗b.
A 0+1-d free fermionic system with the symmetry
given by the Z2-graded algebra C[G, ω, s] is a represen-
tation of Cl0,1 ˆ⊗C[G, ω, s]. Cl0,1 ˆ⊗C[G, ω, s] is a semisim-
ple algebra, which means that any representation can
be decomposed into a direct sum of irreducible represen-
tations.
Moreover, as a consequence of semisimplicity,
Cl0,1 ˆ⊗C[G, ω, s] can be decomposed into a product of
matrix algebras over division algebras, which is called
the Wedderburn–Artin decomposition. Then the repre-
sentation theory of Cl0,1 ˆ⊗C[G, ω, s] reduces to the rep-
resentation theory of the matrix algebras over division
algebras, which is easy to study.

C.
Classifying spaces

To classify quantum systems, let us imagine a space
K of all quantum states (with some properties, for ex-
ample, 0+1-d gapped free fermionic systems), which is
called the classifying space. A point in the classifying

space K is a quantum state.
Given two points in K,
a path between them should be considered as an equiv-
alence between two quantum states.
Given two paths
in K, the homotopy between them should be considered
as an equivalence between two different equivalences be-
tween quantum states. This keeps going to higher ho-
motopies; therefore, the classifying space is a homotopy
type.
What is the equivalence of two quantum systems? For
different types of quantum systems, there should be dif-
ferent kinds of equivalence. For example, for the gapped
free fermionic systems, the classifying space is the space
of parameters of the gapped Hamiltonians, which is a
topological space, and the equivalences are the paths and
higher homotopies in the topological space. The physical
meaning of this equivalence is the adiabatic deformation
of the Hamiltonian. Another example is gapped inter-
acting bosonic systems, where the equivalences are given
by invertible gapped domain walls and by domain walls
between domain walls.
To get the classifying space for 0+1-d gapped fermionic
systems, let us denote the Z2-graded algebra of symme-
try C[G, ω, s] by Asym. Cl0,1 ˆ⊗Asym is the algebra of the
symmetry together with the Hamiltonian. There is a sub-
tlety that, to get a meaningful classification, we need to
consider an infinite-dimensional quantum system for two
reasons: (1) the quantum systems in the real world have
infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, and (2) it is generally
allowed to add new degrees of freedom to the system in
the study of topological phases.
In the language of representation theory, the infinite-
dimensional system is given by a representation ρ of
Cl0,1 ˆ⊗Asym; the Asym module corresponding to ρ is de-
noted by U(Cl0,1 ˆ⊗Asym).
ρ is a (countably) infinite-
dimensional representation of Cl0,1 ˆ⊗Asym; if we de-
compose it into irreducible representations, we require
that the direct sum components contain all species
of irreducible representations, and that for each irre-
ducible representation there are infinitely many copies.
U(Cl0,1 ˆ⊗Asym) is called the universe of Cl0,1 ˆ⊗Asym.
The Hamiltonian of the system is ρ(˜e1 ˆ⊗1) = H0. H0
is chosen to be the trivial system.
Other symmetric
Hamiltonians of the system also form a representation
of Cl0,1 ˆ⊗Asym. The action of Asym is the same as ρ, but
the Hamiltonian H is different from H0. We require H
to be different from H0 only on a finite-dimensional sub-
space of U(Cl0,1 ˆ⊗Asym). The reason is that we do not
allow infinitely many high-energy states to be occupied
and infinitely many low-energy states to be unoccupied.
Now we define the classifying space. We first define
the classifying space[54] of (not necessarily symmetric)
Hamiltonians.

For (0 + 1)-d gapped free fermionic systems with
symmetry Asym, the classifying space for (not nec-
essarily symmetric) Hamiltonians is K(Asym), which
is the space of linear maps on U(Cl0,1 ˆ⊗Asym) such

1.
Z2-graded division algebra

We review the classification of Z2-graded division al-
gebras [64]. If D is a Z2-graded division algebra, then
D0 is a division algebra. When D1 = 0, the classification
reduces to the classification of ordinary division algebras.
Thus, we assume D1 ̸= 0 in what follows.
Let θ ∈D1 be an invertible element.
We denote
θ2 ∈D0 by a. θ induces an algebra automorphism of D0,
which is denoted by f with f(d) = θdθ−1. It is straight-
forward to show that f 2(d) = ada−1 and f(a) = a.
Conversely, given (D0, f, a) such that f 2(d) = ada−1

and f(a) = a, we can construct a Z2-graded algebra D ∼=
D0 ⊕D0θ. An element of D has the form d or dθ for
d ∈D0. The multiplication is induced by the rules θ2 = a
and θdθ−1 = f(d).
We have shown that the classification of Z2-graded di-
vision algebras is equivalent to the classification of the
data (D0, f, a) satisfying f 2(d) = ada−1 and f(a) = a.
When D0 is central simple, we have the following lemma:

Lemma III.4. If D0 is a central simple algebra over K,
the Z2-graded division algebra constructed from (D0, f, a)

structed from (D0, f ′, a′) such that a′ ∈K and f ′ = id.

Proof. Since D0 is a central simple algebra, according to
the Skolem–Noether theorem, any automorphism of D0
is an inner automorphism. Thus, we have f(d) = bdb−1

for some b ∈D0.
If we set θ′ = b−1θ, then we have

since d = (f ′)2(d) = a′d(a′)−1 for d ∈D0.

For Z2-graded division algebras over C, D0 = C is a
central simple algebra. Therefore, the only choice of D
is (C, id, 1) up to isomorphism. Notice that if a ̸= 1, we
can take θ′ to be a−1

For Z2-graded division algebras over R, when D0 = R
or H, the only two choices are (D0, id, ±1) up to isomor-

group of C over R is Z2; hence, the only two choices of

the only two choices are (C, (−), ±1) up to isomorphism.
In summary, there are two isomorphism classes of Z2-

(C, id, 1). There are ten isomorphism classes of Z2-graded
division algebras over R, which are R, C, H, (R, id, 1),
(R, id, −1), (C, id, 1), (C, (−), 1), (C, (−), −1), (H, id, 1),

V.
CONCLUSIONS

In this work, we introduce the classifying spaces of
free fermionic systems. Starting from the symmetries of
free fermionic systems, we generalize these symmetries
from groups to algebras.
Subsequently, we introduce
the classifying spaces for free fermionic systems in ar-
bitrary dimensions endowed with such algebraic symme-
tries, and demonstrate that they are G-homotopy equiv-
alent to the classifying spaces of Dirac Hamiltonians.
Based on these classifying spaces, we then address the
classification problem. We consider four distinct classifi-
cation schemes prevalent in the physics literature, along
with the canonical isomorphisms among them, noting
that each scheme possesses its own specific applications
and physical significance.
Among these four schemes,
the algebraic classification is the most amenable to com-
putation.
Consequently, we investigate the Z2-graded
Wedderburn–Artin decomposition of the Z2-graded al-
gebra AV
sym obtained from Dirac Hamiltonians, and we
have developed a GAP package to facilitate these com-
putations. The GAP package can be used to obtain the
classification, the graded Wedderburn–Artin decomposi-
tion of AV
sym, the Dirac Hamiltonians, and so on.

Characters and great orthogonality theorem

We first prove that the Frobenius–Schur indicator can
distinguish the three D types. It is well known that there
is a G-invariant inner product on any C[G] module, since
we can start with an arbitrary inner product and then
average it over G. The inner product induces an isomor-
phism ¯V ∼= V ∗, where V ∗is the dual module.
Thus,
we can replace V ∼= ¯V by V ∼= V ∗. The isomorphism
V ∼= V ∗is equivalent to the existence of a G-invariant
nondegenerate bilinear form B. Notice that B is unique
up to a scalar, because of the Schur lemma. B can be
decomposed into S + A, where S is symmetric and A is
skew symmetric; both S and A are G invariant. Thus, B
must equal either S or A. It can be proved that if ¯α ◦α
is positive, then B = S. If ¯α ◦α is negative, then B = A.

Appendix E: Karoubi’s K-Theory

follows, we assume that the fundamental theorem for the
general case is correct, and leave the proof to the ex-

case has been used implicitly in physics-related works,
for example, Refs. [20, 27].

can be studied by a Hamiltonian on the Brillouin zone
(Kitaev recently proposed a rigorous method to classify

this point. The periodic boundary conditions in each di-
rection on the torus come from translation invariance in
that direction. If we consider a translation-noninvariant
system, at least the periodic boundary conditions should
be broken. The spherical Brillouin zone is a way to break
the periodic boundary conditions, but it retains part of
the “translation invariance” in the sense that it does not
have q translation-invariant directions, but is translation
invariant in a “uniform” way. We hope that the Hamilto-
nians on the spherical Brillouin zone are a good approxi-
mation to translation-noninvariant systems whose Hamil-
tonians vary slowly in real space. Then we could study
each small region of the real space by the Hamiltonian
on the spherical Brillouin zone. Finally, for any Hamil-
tonian on the spherical Brillouin zone, we can construct
a Hamiltonian on the torus via the map T q →Sq that
maps the periodic boundary of the torus to one point.
