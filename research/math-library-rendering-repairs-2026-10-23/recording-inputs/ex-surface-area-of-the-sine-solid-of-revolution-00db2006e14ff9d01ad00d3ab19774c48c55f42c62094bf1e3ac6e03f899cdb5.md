## Example

Rotate the graph $y=\sin x$, $0\le x\le\pi$, about the $x$-axis. The resulting
surface has area

$$A=2\pi\left(\sqrt2+\operatorname{arsinh}1\right)=2\pi\left(\sqrt2+\log(1+\sqrt2)\right).$$

The profile is positive in the parameter interior and vanishes only at its
endpoints, where the generating curve meets the axis of revolution.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The radius function $r(x)=\sin x$ on $[0,\pi]$ and the surface obtained by rotating its graph about the $x$-axis.

[F1] Under the hypotheses of the scalar surface-integral theorem for a surface of revolution, the rotated surface has area $2\pi\int_a^b r(s)\sqrt{1+r'(s)^2}\,ds$ ([[cor-surface-area-of-revolution-formula]]).

[F2] Those hypotheses require $a<b$ and $r:[a,b]\to[0,\infty)$ to be $C^1$ on a neighbourhood of $[a,b]$, positive on $(a,b)$, and allowed to vanish only at the endpoints ([[thm-scalar-surface-integrals-on-a-surface-of-revolution]]).

[L1] $\sin\pi=0$, and $\sin x>0$ for every $x$ with $0<x<\pi$; thus $\pi$ is the first positive zero of sine, in particular $\pi>0$ ([[cor-pi-is-the-first-positive-sine-zero]]).

[L2] The functions $\sin$ and $\cos$ are differentiable on $\mathbb R$, with $(\sin x)'=\cos x$ and $(\cos x)'=-\sin x$; also $\sin0=0$ and $\cos0=1$ ([[thm-sine-and-cosine-derivatives]]).

[L3] For a real-valued function on $A\subseteq\mathbb R$, differentiability at a limit point $c\in A$ implies continuity there ([[cor-differentiable-implies-continuous]]).

[L4] The function $\sinh:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ is odd, strictly increasing, and onto; $\cosh$ is positive; $\cosh^2v-\sinh^2v=1$; and $(\sinh v)'=\cosh v$ ([[thm-hyperbolic-identities-and-derivatives]]).

[L5] Let $I\subseteq\mathbb R$ be order-convex with at least two elements, let $f:I\to\mathbb R$ be continuous and injective, and let $g:f[I]\to I$ be its inverse. If $f$ is differentiable at $c\in I$ with $f'(c)\ne0$, then $g$ is differentiable at $f(c)$ and $g'(f(c))=1/f'(c)$ ([[thm-derivative-of-an-inverse]]).

[L6] For every real $u$, $\operatorname{arsinh}u=\log(u+\sqrt{u^2+1})$ ([[thm-inverse-hyperbolic-logarithm-formulas]]).

[L7] For every real $\alpha$, $u\mapsto u^\alpha$ is continuous and differentiable on $(0,\infty)$, with derivative $\alpha u^{\alpha-1}$ ([[thm-real-power-continuity-and-derivatives]]).

[L8] If $a>0$ and $q\in\mathbb Q$, then the real power $a^q$ agrees with the rational power; in particular this holds for $q=1/2$ ([[thm-real-power-agrees-with-rational-exponent]]).

[L9] Every nonnegative real $a$ has a unique nonnegative square root $\sqrt a$ ([[thm-of-square-roots]]).

[L10] If real functions $g$ and $h$ are differentiable at the relevant points, then $(g\circ h)'=(g'\circ h)h'$ ([[thm-chain-rule]]).

[L11] Products, sums, and scalar multiples obey their usual derivative rules ([[thm-algebra-of-derivatives]]).

[L12] Every continuous real-valued function on a nondegenerate closed interval is Riemann integrable there ([[thm-continuous-implies-integrable]]).

[L13] If $a<b$, $G:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ is differentiable at every point of $[a,b]$, and $G'$ is integrable, then $\int_a^bG'=G(b)-G(a)$ ([[thm-ftc-second-part]]).

[L14] $\cos0=1$ and $\cos\pi=-1$ ([[thm-quarter-turn-values-and-shift-formulas]]).

## Verification

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 Facts [L1], [L2], and [L3] verify the hypotheses in [F2], so [F1] gives $A=2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin x\sqrt{1+\cos^2x}\,dx$. [F1, F2, L1, L2, L3]

1.2 Let $u\in\mathbb R$ and $v=\operatorname{arsinh}u$. Facts [L3], [L4], and [L5] give $(\operatorname{arsinh}u)'=1/\cosh v=1/\sqrt{1+u^2}$, where positivity of $\cosh v$ and uniqueness in [L9] select the nonnegative square root. Since $\sinh$ is an odd bijection, its inverse is odd, so $\operatorname{arsinh}(-u)=-\operatorname{arsinh}u$. [L3, L4, L5, L9, algebra]

1.3 Since $\pi>0$ by [L1], the interval $[0,\pi]$ is nondegenerate. The function $x\mapsto\sin x\sqrt{1+\cos^2x}$ is continuous on this interval and hence integrable there. [L1, L2, L3, L7, L8, L9, L12, algebra]

2.1 Define $G(x)=-\tfrac12\left(\operatorname{arsinh}(\cos x)+\cos x\sqrt{1+\cos^2x}\right)$. Using step 1.2, the derivative of the positive square root, and the chain and product rules gives $G'(x)=\sin x\sqrt{1+\cos^2x}$. [step 1.2, L2, L7, L8, L9, L10, L11, algebra]

3.1 By the fundamental theorem, step 1.3, and step 2.1, the integral in step 1.1 is $G(\pi)-G(0)=\sqrt2+\operatorname{arsinh}1$: fact [L14] gives the cosine endpoint values, and the oddness in step 1.2 changes $\operatorname{arsinh}(-1)$ to $-\operatorname{arsinh}1$. Fact [L6] also gives $\operatorname{arsinh}1=\log(1+\sqrt2)$. [step 1.2, step 1.3, step 2.1, L6, L9, L13, L14, algebra]

4.1 Substituting step 3.1 into the area formula of step 1.1 gives $A=2\pi(\sqrt2+\operatorname{arsinh}1)=2\pi(\sqrt2+\log(1+\sqrt2))$. [step 1.1, step 3.1, algebra] ∎

## Remarks
