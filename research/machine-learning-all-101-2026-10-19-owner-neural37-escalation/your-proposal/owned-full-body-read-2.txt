
===== def-physics-informed-neural-network-loss =====


## Definition

Let $U\subset\mathbb R^d$ be a bounded Lipschitz domain, let $\Gamma\subseteq\partial U$ be closed, let $L$ be a differential operator on $U$, and let $B$ be a boundary operator on $\Gamma$. Let $f:U\to\mathbb R$ and $g:\Gamma\to\mathbb R$ be target data. For a trial function $h$, define its residuals by $r_h(x)=L[h](x)-f(x)$ and $q_h(x)=B[h](x)-g(x)$. For $0<\alpha\le1$ and a function $v$ on a set $E$, define its Hölder seminorm by
$$[v]_{\alpha;E}=\sup_{x\ne y\in E}\frac{|v(x)-v(y)|}{|x-y|^\alpha}.$$
Only trial functions for which $L[h]$ on $U$ and $B[h]$ on $\Gamma$ are pointwise defined and finite are admitted. For the population loss, require $f,r_h$ to be measurable with respect to $(U,\mu_r)$ and $g,q_h$ to be measurable with respect to $(\Gamma,\mu_b)$. For positive fixed weights $\lambda_r,\lambda_b$ and probability measures $\mu_r$ on $U$, $\mu_b$ on $\Gamma$, the population loss is
$$\operatorname{Loss}^{\mathrm{PINN}}(h;\lambda)=\lambda_r\int_U|r_h|^2\,d\mu_r+\lambda_b\int_\Gamma|q_h|^2\,d\mu_b.$$
These nonnegative population integrals take values in $[0,+\infty]$, so they may be infinite. The Hölder seminorms below may likewise be infinite.

For $d\ge2$, given positive integer counts $m_r,m_b$, residual collocation points $x_r^1,\ldots,x_r^{m_r}$, and boundary collocation points $x_b^1,\ldots,x_b^{m_b}$, the empirical loss is
$$\operatorname{Loss}^{\mathrm{PINN}}_{m}(h;\lambda)=\frac{\lambda_r}{m_r}\sum_{i=1}^{m_r}|r_h(x_r^i)|^2+\frac{\lambda_b}{m_b}\sum_{j=1}^{m_b}|q_h(x_b^j)|^2.$$
For $d=1$, a bounded Lipschitz domain has finite boundary, so $\Gamma$ is finite. When every point of $\Gamma$ is available deterministically, define its exact full-boundary contribution by
$$\operatorname{Loss}^{\mathrm{PINN},b}(h;\lambda_b)=\lambda_b\sum_{y\in\Gamma}\mu_b(\{y\})|q_h(y)|^2=\lambda_b\int_\Gamma|q_h|^2\,d\mu_b,$$
and, for a positive integer count $m_r$ and residual collocation points $x_r^1,\ldots,x_r^{m_r}$, set
$$\operatorname{Loss}^{\mathrm{PINN}}_{m}(h;\lambda)=\frac{\lambda_r}{m_r}\sum_{i=1}^{m_r}|r_h(x_r^i)|^2+\operatorname{Loss}^{\mathrm{PINN},b}(h;\lambda_b).$$
For $d\ge2$, its Hölder-regularized version is
$$\operatorname{Loss}_{m}(h;\lambda,\lambda_m^R)=\operatorname{Loss}^{\mathrm{PINN}}_m(h;\lambda)+\lambda^R_{r,m}[L[h]]_{\alpha;U}^{2}+\lambda^R_{b,m}[B[h]]_{\alpha;\Gamma}^{2}.$$
For $d=1$, the Hölder-regularized version is
$$\operatorname{Loss}_{m}(h;\lambda,\lambda_m^R)=\operatorname{Loss}^{\mathrm{PINN}}_m(h;\lambda)+\lambda^R_{r,m}[L[h]]_{\alpha;U}^{2};$$
it uses the exact full-boundary contribution above, has no random boundary sample, and has no boundary seminorm penalty. Require the regularization coefficients appearing in either version to be nonnegative; a zero coefficient contributes zero even when its seminorm is infinite. For finite realized sample lists the empirical sums are finite; the regularized objective is allowed to take the value $+\infty$. These are population and empirical functionals; they do not specify an optimization algorithm or assert that training reaches a global minimizer.

===== def-shin-elliptic-divergence-form-operator =====


## Definition

Let $U\subset\mathbb R^d$ be open. For coefficients $a_{ij},b_i,c_i,d$ on $U$, define the positive divergence-form operator
$$L[u]=\sum_{i,j=1}^d D_i(a_{ij}D_j u)+\sum_{i=1}^dD_i(b_i u)+\sum_{i=1}^d c_iD_i u+du.$$
When the coefficients are continuously differentiable where needed, its nondivergence expansion is
$$L[u]=\sum_{i,j=1}^d a_{ij}D_iD_j u+\sum_{j=1}^d\widetilde b_jD_j u+\widetilde c\,u,$$
with
$$\widetilde b_j=\sum_{i=1}^dD_i a_{ij}+b_j+c_j,\qquad \widetilde c=\sum_{i=1}^dD_i b_i+d.$$
The **positive divergence convention** here means that the principal part is $+\sum_{i,j}D_i(a_{ij}D_j u)$. This sign is part of the definition.

===== def-shin-parabolic-operator-and-boundary =====


## Definition

Let $U\subset\mathbb R^d$ be bounded, let $T>0$, and set $Q=U\times(0,T)$ and
$$\Gamma_T=(\bar U\times\{0\})\cup(\partial U\times[0,T]).$$
The final-time face $U\times\{T\}$ is excluded from $\Gamma_T$. For time-dependent coefficients define
$$L_t[u]=\sum_{i,j=1}^dD_i(a_{ij}(x,t)D_j u)+\sum_{i=1}^dD_i(b_i(x,t)u)+\sum_{i=1}^d c_i(x,t)D_i u+d(x,t)u.$$
Where the coefficient derivatives exist, write its nondivergence expansion as
$$L_t[u]=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}D_iD_j u+\sum_i\widetilde b_iD_i u+\widetilde c\,u,$$
where $\widetilde b_i=\sum_jD_j a_{ji}+b_i+c_i$ and $\widetilde c=\sum_iD_i b_i+d$. For symmetric $a$, the drift also equals $\sum_jD_j a_{ij}+b_i+c_i$, the form used by the symmetric source consumers. The initial-boundary value problem uses the sign convention
$$-u_t+L_t[u]=f\quad\hbox{in }Q,\qquad u=\varphi\quad\hbox{on }\partial U\times[0,T],\qquad u=g\quad\hbox{on }\bar U\times\{0\},$$
with compatibility $g(x)=\varphi(x,0)$ on $\partial U$. Its residual for a trial function $h$ is $f+h_t-L_t[h]$, and its prescribed-data error is measured on $\Gamma_T$.

For the source notation $C_{2+\alpha}(Q)$, use the parabolic distance
$$d_p((x,t),(y,s))=(|x-y|^2+|t-s|)^{1/2}.$$
Let $B=U\times\{0\}$ and $S_\tau=\partial U\times[0,\tau]$. For $P=(x,\tau)\in Q$, set $\delta(P)=\inf_{R\in B\cup S_\tau}d_p(P,R)$ and $\delta_{P,Q}=\min\{\delta(P),\delta(Q)\}$. For an integer $m\ge0$, define
$$|v|_{m,\alpha}=\sup_{P\in Q}\delta(P)^m|v(P)|+\sup_{P\ne Q\in Q}\delta_{P,Q}^{m+\alpha}\frac{|v(P)-v(Q)|}{d_p(P,Q)^\alpha}.$$
The class $C_{2+\alpha}(Q)$ consists of functions for which
$$|u|_{0,\alpha}+\sum_i|D_i u|_{1,\alpha}+\sum_{i,j}|D_{ij}u|_{1,\alpha}+|u_t|_{2,\alpha}<\infty,$$
with the indicated derivatives continuous in $Q$. This fixes the source's weighted parabolic Hölder convention.

===== ex-fourier-neural-operator-layer-and-claim-boundary =====


## Example

On the one-dimensional torus, let $v(x)=\sin(2\pi x)$ and use a Fourier cutoff $K\ge1$. If the spectral matrices satisfy $R_1=R_{-1}=1$ and all other retained matrices vanish, then the spectral part of the layer returns $v$ exactly. If the cutoff is $K=0$, its spectral part is zero because $\widehat v(0)=0$. These finite-mode computations illustrate the layer definition and make no approximation or universality claim for the full Fourier neural-operator architecture.

## Example references
[Kovachki et al., §5.3: finite Fourier-mode parameterization](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.08481)

[Kovachki et al., §5.3: Fourier-layer convention](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.08481)

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** $v(x)=\sin(2\pi x)$ on $\mathbb T=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ and the Fourier coefficient convention in the Fourier neural-operator layer definition.

[F1] The Fourier coefficient of $e^{2\pi i kx}$ at index $j$ is $1$ if $j=k$ and $0$ otherwise, by integrating the exponential over one period.

## Verification

**Proof strategy:** compute the two nonzero Fourier coefficients.

1.1 Using $\sin(2\pi x)=(e^{2\pi ix}-e^{-2\pi ix})/(2i)$ and [F1], $\widehat v(1)=1/(2i)$, $\widehat v(-1)=-1/(2i)$, and every other coefficient is zero. [given, F1, algebra]

2.1 With $R_1=R_{-1}=1$, the retained sum is $e^{2\pi ix}/(2i)-e^{-2\pi ix}/(2i)=v(x)$. At cutoff zero, the sum has only the $k=0$ term, which vanishes because $\widehat v(0)=0$. [step 1.1, algebra] ∎


===== ex-neural-operator-grid-refinement-vs-fixed-grid-network =====


## Example

On the open domain $D=(0,1)$, use the continuous representatives $a\in C([0,1])$ and $G(a)\in C([0,1])$, and define $G(a)(x)=\int_0^1a(y)\,dy$ for every $x\in[0,1]$. Its midpoint-mesh realization with $n$ cells is the constant output $G_n(a)(x)=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n a((j-\frac12)/n)$. For a fixed continuous input $a$, these outputs converge uniformly in $x$ to $G(a)$. By contrast, the identity map $F_n:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n$ on a length-$n$ vector is defined on a different input and output space at each $n$; without an additional reconstruction rule it is not a single function-space operator that accepts arbitrary meshes and can be queried at arbitrary output points.

## Example references
[Kovachki et al., Definition 4, Theorem 8, and §1.2: continuum operators versus fixed-grid maps](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.08481)

[Kovachki et al., Appendix E: uniform Riemann-sum convergence for operator layers](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.08481)

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A continuous function $a:[0,1]\to\mathbb R$, the integral operator $G$, and the midpoint quadrature rule in the Example.

[F1] A continuous function on the compact interval $[0,1]$ is uniformly continuous.

## Verification

**Proof strategy:** estimate each midpoint cell error by the modulus of continuity.

1.1 Let $I_j=[(j-1)/n,j/n]$ and define $\omega_a(\delta)=\sup\{|a(x)-a(y)|:x,y\in[0,1],\ |x-y|\le\delta\}$. For each $y\in I_j$, $|y-(j-\frac12)/n|\le1/(2n)$, so $$\left|\int_{I_j}a(y)\,dy-\frac1n a((j-\tfrac12)/n)\right|\le\frac1n\omega_a(1/(2n)).$$ Summing over $j$ gives $|G(a)(x)-G_n(a)(x)|\le\omega_a(1/(2n))$ for every $x$. [given, algebra]

2.1 By [F1], $\omega_a(\delta)\to0$ as $\delta\downarrow0$. The bound in step 1.1 is independent of $x$, so $G_n(a)\to G(a)$ in $C([0,1])$. The dimension of $F_n$ changes with $n$ and its definition returns only a length-$n$ vector, so no arbitrary-point output or common function-space domain follows from $F_n$ alone. [step 1.1, F1, algebra] ∎


===== ex-non-global-training-stationary-point =====


## Example

For the scalar objective $J(\theta)=(\theta^2-1)^2$, the global minimum value is $0$ and is attained at $\theta=\pm1$, while $\theta=0$ is stationary with $J(0)=1$. Gradient descent $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta J'(\theta_k)$ initialized at $\theta_0=0$ remains at $0$ for every step size $\eta>0$. Thus a theorem about global minimizers does not by itself imply that a gradient-based training procedure finds one.

## Example references
[Shin et al., Introduction: the analysis idealizes global minimizers and does not prove optimizer convergence](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.01806)

[Shin et al., Introduction: no general optimizer guarantee is asserted](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.01806)

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The polynomial objective and gradient-descent update in the Example.

[F1] $J'(\theta)=4\theta(\theta^2-1)$ by polynomial differentiation.

## Verification

**Proof strategy:** compare objective values and evaluate the update at zero.

1.1 Since $J(\theta)$ is a square, $J(\theta)\ge0$, with equality exactly when $\theta^2=1$. Also $J(0)=1$. Hence $0$ is not a global minimizer. [given, algebra]

2.1 By [F1], $J'(0)=0$. If $\theta_k=0$, the update gives $\theta_{k+1}=0-\eta J'(0)=0$; induction from $\theta_0=0$ yields $\theta_k=0$ for all $k$. The iterates therefore never reach either global minimizer. [given, F1, algebra, induction] ∎


===== ex-pinn-small-scale-cube-sampling-has-instances =====


## Example

For every integer $D\ge2$, take $U=(0,1)^D$ and $\Gamma=\partial[0,1]^D$. Uniform volume probability on $U$ and uniform surface probability on $\Gamma$ satisfy the corrected PINN sampling conditions for every $0<\ell\le1$. One may take $c_r=2^{-D}$ and $c_b=(2D)^{-1}2^{-(D-1)}$ for the lower cell-mass constants. The upper ball-mass constants may be taken as the volumes of the unit balls in $\mathbb R^D$ and $\mathbb R^{D-1}$, respectively, at every radius. Thus the small-scale condition has actual probability-measure instances, whereas its all-positive-scale predecessor does not.

## Example references

[[def-lebesgue-measure-and-the-lebesgue-sigma-algebra]]

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The unit cube, its full boundary, and the indicated uniform probabilities. Let $\omega_s$ denote the $s$-dimensional Lebesgue measure of the Euclidean unit ball.

[F1] Lebesgue measure of a rectangular cell is the product of its side lengths, and dilation by a factor $\rho$ multiplies $s$-dimensional volume by $\rho^s$ ([[def-lebesgue-measure-and-the-lebesgue-sigma-algebra]]).

## Verification

1.1 Fix $0<\ell\le1$ and put $N=\lceil1/\ell\rceil$. Since $1/\ell\le N<1/\ell+1\le2/\ell$, one has $\ell/2\le1/N\le\ell$. Partition each coordinate interval into $N$ intervals of length $1/N$, assigning endpoints by a fixed half-open convention and intersecting with $U$. Their product cells lie in side-$\ell$ cubes centered at their midpoints, which belong to the cells. Each has volume $N^{-D}\ge2^{-D}\ell^D$. On each of the $2D$ boundary faces perform the same $(D-1)$-dimensional partition. Assign shared face edges to the lowest-index incident face and then to a lowest-index neighboring cell; the grid vertices and edges have surface measure zero and lie in the closures of those cells. Each cell remains in its same closed ambient cube and its midpoint belongs to it. Its normalized surface mass is $(2D)^{-1}N^{-(D-1)}\ge(2D)^{-1}2^{-(D-1)}\ell^{D-1}$. [given, F1, algebra]

2.1 Every residual ball intersection has volume at most $\omega_D\rho^D$. On each face its surface intersection is contained in a $(D-1)$-dimensional ball of radius $\rho$, of measure at most $\omega_{D-1}\rho^{D-1}$. Summing over $2D$ faces and dividing by total surface area $2D$ gives boundary probability at most $\omega_{D-1}\rho^{D-1}$. The probability densities are the positive constants $1$ and $1/(2D)$ on their respective supports. These are the required upper and lower bounds with the stated small-scale range. [step 1.1, F1, algebra] ∎


===== ex-sparse-pinn-collocation-aliases-residual =====


## Example

On $U=(0,1)$ take the residual operator $L[h]=h'$, target $f=0$, boundary operator given by endpoint restriction, and boundary data $g=0$. For each integer $m\ge2$, set
$$h_m(x)=\frac{1-\cos(2\pi mx)}{2\pi m}.$$
At the residual collocation points $x_j=j/m$ for $1\le j<m$, the residual $L[h_m](x_j)$ is zero; the exact endpoint values are also zero. Nevertheless, for uniform population measure, the population residual loss is $\int_0^1|L[h_m](x)|^2dx=1/2$. This example concerns unregularized sparse collocation and does not claim that $h_m$ minimizes the regularized objective.

## Example references
[Shin et al., equations (2.4)–(2.6): empirical and population PINN losses](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.01806)

[Shin et al., equation (2.5): the population residual loss integrates squared residuals](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.01806)

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The operator, data, collocation set, and trial functions in the Example.

[F1] The derivative of $h_m$ is $h_m'(x)=\sin(2\pi mx)$.

[F2] For every positive integer $m$, $\int_0^1\sin^2(2\pi mx)dx=1/2$, by $\sin^2 z=(1-\cos(2z))/2$ and direct integration.

## Verification

**Proof strategy:** evaluate the derivative at the grid points and integrate its square.

1.1 Differentiating the displayed formula gives [F1]. At $x_j=j/m$, $h_m'(x_j)=\sin(2\pi j)=0$. Also $h_m(0)=h_m(1)=0$ since $\cos(0)=\cos(2\pi m)=1$. Thus the empirical residual and exact boundary errors vanish. [given, F1, algebra]

2.1 The population residual loss is $\int_0^1|h_m'(x)|^2dx=1/2$ by [F2]. Therefore exact collocation fit at these sample points does not by itself force the population residual loss to be small. [step 1.1, F2, algebra] ∎


===== fs-shin-global-holder-dirichlet-claim =====


## Statement

Let $0<\alpha<1$ and let $U\subset\mathbb R^d$ be bounded and satisfy an exterior sphere condition at every boundary point. Let $a_{ij}=a_{ji}\in C^{1,\alpha}(U)$ be uniformly elliptic with minimum eigenvalue at least $\lambda_0>0$, let $b_i\in C^{1,\alpha}(U)$ and $c_i,d\in C^\alpha(U)$, and define
$$L[u]=\sum_{i,j=1}^dD_i(a_{ij}D_j u+b_i u)+\sum_{i=1}^d c_iD_i u+du.$$
Assume $\sum_{i,j}|a_{ij}(x)|^2\le\Lambda$ and $\lambda_0^{-2}(\|b(x)\|^2+\|c(x)\|^2)+\lambda_0^{-1}|d(x)|\le v^2$ on $U$, and use the expanded coefficients $\widetilde b_j=\sum_iD_i a_{ij}+b_j+c_j$ and $\widetilde c=\sum_iD_i b_i+d\le0$. For every $f\in C^{0,\alpha}(U)$ and $g\in C^0(\partial U)$, the source assertion is that the Dirichlet problem $L[u]=f$ in $U$, $u=g$ on $\partial U$ has a unique solution $u\in C^{2,\alpha}(U)\cap C^0(\bar U)$. For any $h\in C^0(U)$ with $L[h]\in C^{0,\alpha}(U)$ and $h\in C^{0,\alpha}(\partial U)$, the source further asserts
$$\|u-h\|_{C^0(U)}\le C\bigl(\|f-L[h]\|_{C^0(U)}+\|g-h\|_{C^0(\partial U)}\bigr),$$
where $C$ depends only on the domain and the displayed coefficient bounds. The global existence part of this assertion is false under the library's bounded global Hölder convention.

## Statement references

[Shin et al., Theorem D.1 and Lemma D.2, PDF p.26: source assertion retained here](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.01806)

[[cex-shin-elliptic-corner-regularity-on-exterior-sphere-domain]]

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The source assertion in the Statement, including its printed divergence indexing. The refutation uses $b=c=d=0$, so the indexing discrepancy does not affect it.

[A1] Assume for contradiction that the asserted global existence conclusion holds for every admissible datum.

[F1] The sector $U=\{0<r<1,0<\theta<2\pi/3\}$ is bounded Lipschitz and satisfies the exterior-sphere condition. With $L=\Delta$, $g=0$ and $u=\chi(r)r^{3/2}\sin(3\theta/2)$, its smooth forcing $f=\Delta u$ is globally Hölder, and $u$ is classical and continuous on the closure, but has unbounded second derivatives ([[cex-shin-elliptic-corner-regularity-on-exterior-sphere-domain]]).

[F2] The zero-boundary classical homogeneous Dirichlet problem is unique by comparison ([[lem-shin-elliptic-residual-stability]]).

[F3] The global bounded Hölder norm includes the supremum of every second derivative ([[def-holder-spaces-c-k-alpha-and-their-scaled-norms]]). The positive-principal convention is fixed by [[def-shin-elliptic-divergence-form-operator]].

## Refutation

1.1 Assume for contradiction the claimed global existence assertion [A1]. Use the sector and data from [F1]. The coefficient matrix is the identity and all lower coefficients vanish; uniform ellipticity holds with $\lambda_0=1$, the pointwise source bounds hold with $\Lambda=2$ and $v=0$, and the expanded zero-order coefficient is zero. Thus these data satisfy every hypothesis of the claimed existence assertion. [assume-contra, F1, F3]

1.2 Assumption [A1] gives a global bounded $C^{2,\alpha}(U)$ solution $u^*$ continuous on the closure. The explicit $u$ from [F1] is a second classical continuous Dirichlet solution. Their difference is zero-boundary harmonic, so [F2] implies $u^*=u$. [A1, F1, F2]

2.1 Along $\theta=\pi/3$ and $r<1/4$, $\partial_{rr}u=(3/4)r^{-1/2}$ is unbounded. A globally bounded Cartesian Hessian would bound this unit-direction second derivative. Therefore [F3] excludes $u$ from the asserted global class, contradicting step 1.2. The global existence claim is false; its failure does not refute the separately proved classical residual estimate. [step 1.2, F1, F3, discharge-contradiction] ∎

## Remarks

The printed source formula also repeats $b_i u$ inside the double divergence sum, although its stated expanded drift contains $b$ only once. This defect is retained visibly in the historical assertion above. The native Laplacian sector refutation is independent of that discrepancy. The repaired existence theorem uses the intended single $b$ divergence and preserves the correct local-interior and conditional smooth-boundary branches.

===== lem-ap-finite-dimensional-factorization-of-continuous-maps =====


## Statement

Let $X,Y$ be real Banach spaces with the approximation property, $T:X\to Y$ continuous, $K\subset X$ compact, and $\varepsilon>0$. There are integers $J,s\ge1$, bounded linear maps $E:X\to\mathbb R^J$ and $B:\mathbb R^s\to Y$, and continuous $h:\mathbb R^J\to\mathbb R^s$ such that $\sup_{x\in K}\|T(x)-Bh(E(x))\|_Y<\varepsilon$. In particular $E(x)=(\ell_j(x))_{j=1}^J$ for bounded linear functionals $\ell_j$, and $Bv=\sum_{i=1}^s v_i\beta_i$ for finitely many $\beta_i\in Y$.

## Statement references

[Kovachki et al., Lemma 22](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.08481)

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The Banach spaces with AP, the continuous map $T$, compact $K$, and error $\varepsilon>0$.

[F1] AP approximates the identity uniformly on any compact set by bounded finite-rank linear maps ([[def-approximation-property-and-bounded-approximation-property]]).

[F2] A chosen finite basis has bounded linear coordinate maps in both directions ([[thm-coordinate-map-for-a-finite-dimensional-normed-space]]).

## Proof

1.1 If $K$ is empty use zero maps. Otherwise consider all pairs $(x,\rho)$ with $x\in K$ and $\rho>0$ such that $\|T(z)-T(x)\|_Y<\varepsilon/4$ whenever $\|z-x\|_X<3\rho$. Continuity ensures that the balls $B(x,\rho)$ from this family cover $K$; take a finite subcover $B(x_i,\rho_i)$. Put $\delta=\min_i\rho_i>0$. For $x\in K$ and $z\in X$ with $\|z-x\|_X<\delta$, select $i$ with $\|x-x_i\|_X<\rho_i$; then both $x,z$ lie within $3\rho_i$ of $x_i$, so $\|T(z)-T(x)\|_Y<\varepsilon/2$. No compact neighborhood of $K$ is asserted or needed. [given, choose, construct, algebra]

2.1 Apply AP in $X$ to choose bounded finite-rank $P$ with $\sup_K\|Px-x\|_X<\delta$. By step 1.1, $\sup_K\|T(Px)-T(x)\|_Y<\varepsilon/2$. The set $T(P(K))$ is compact; apply AP in $Y$ to obtain bounded finite-rank $Q$ with $\sup_{x\in K}\|QT(Px)-T(Px)\|_Y<\varepsilon/2$. Thus $QT P$ approximates $T$ on $K$ within $\varepsilon$. This makes only two AP selections and does not require a sequence, an infinite union of compact sets, or a choice principle for sequences. [step 1.1, F1, choose, algebra]

3.1 Choose a finite basis $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_J$ of $P(X)$. Its coordinate functionals are bounded by [F2]; hence $Px=\sum_j\ell_j(x)\alpha_j$ with $\ell_j$ bounded on $X$, by composing those functionals with $P$. Write $Rz=\sum_jz_j\alpha_j$, $E(x)=(\ell_j(x))_j$. Choose a finite basis $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_s$ of $Q(Y)$, with bounded coordinate functionals $c_i$, and put $h_i(z)=c_i(QT(Rz))$ and $Bv=\sum_i v_i\beta_i$. Each $h_i$ is continuous on all $\mathbb R^J$, and $BhE=QTP$. For a zero-dimensional range use one zero basis vector and the zero coefficient map instead of an actual basis. These formulas prove the stated factorization and complete the proof. [step 2.1, F2, construct, algebra] ∎
