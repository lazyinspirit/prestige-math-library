## Statement

Let $W$ and $\mathcal C(W)$ be as in
[[def-finite-window-spatial-observation]], and let
$f:W\to[0,\infty)$ be finite-valued and Borel measurable. For
$\eta=\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{x_i}\in\mathcal C(W)$, counting repeated locations
with their multiplicities and taking the empty product to be $1$, define
$$G_f(\eta):=\prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i).$$
Then $G_f:\mathcal C(W)\to[0,\infty)$ is measurable.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The finite-window configuration space, a finite-valued Borel
function $f$, and the product $G_f$ defined in the Statement.

[F1] The sigma-algebra on $\mathcal C(W)$ is generated by the evaluation maps
$\eta\mapsto\eta(B)$ for Borel $B\subseteq W$; each configuration is a
finite sum of Dirac masses, with repetitions allowed.
([[def-finite-window-spatial-observation]])

[F2] A nonnegative measurable function is the pointwise increasing limit of
nonnegative simple measurable functions.
([[thm-increasing-simple-approximation-of-a-nonnegative-measurable-function]])

[F3] If $u,v$ are nonnegative measurable functions, then $uv$ is measurable:
for $a\ge0$,
$$\{uv>a\}=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb Q,\ q>0}\{u>q\}\cap\{v>a/q\},$$
and for $a<0$ the superlevel set is the whole space. Iterating this fact gives
measurability of finite products. The identity uses the extended-product
convention $0\cdot(+\infty)=0$.
([[def-extended-real-valued-measurable-function]])

## Proof

**Given:** $f$ is finite-valued and Borel measurable on $W$.

1.1 First suppose $f$ is simple, with value $c_j\ge0$ on each member $B_j$ of a finite Borel partition of $W$. Then $G_f(\eta)=\prod_j c_j^{\eta(B_j)}$, using $0^0=1$; every exponent is an evaluation map. For each fixed $c_j$, the map $m\mapsto c_j^m$ on $\mathbb N_0$ is measurable: the inverse image of any Borel set is a union of the countable level sets $\{\eta(B_j)=m\}$. This also covers $c_j=0$ with $0^0=1$. The finite product is measurable by [F3]. [given, F1, F3, construct]

2.1 For general $f$, choose simple functions $s_m\uparrow f$ by [F2]. If $\eta=\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{x_i}$, then $G_{s_m}(\eta)=\prod_{i=1}^n s_m(x_i)\uparrow\prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i)=G_f(\eta)$; the limit holds because the product has finitely many nonnegative factors, and it also holds for the empty configuration. Thus $G_f$ is a pointwise limit of measurable functions from step 1.1 and is measurable. [given, step 1.1, F2, algebra] ∎
