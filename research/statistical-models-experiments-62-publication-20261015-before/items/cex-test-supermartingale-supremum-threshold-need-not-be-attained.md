## Statement refuted

Assume AC. For every null family $\mathcal P_0$, every nonnegative test
supermartingale $M$ for $\mathcal P_0$, every $P\in\mathcal P_0$, and every
$\alpha\in(0,1)$, the identity
$$P\!\left(\sup_{t\ge0}M_t\ge1/\alpha\right)=P(\tau_\alpha<\infty),\qquad\tau_\alpha=\inf\{t\ge0:M_t\ge1/\alpha\},$$
holds.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Assume AC as in the library's test-supermartingale and supermartingale definitions. We construct one probability law and a discrete nonnegative martingale $M$ with $\mathbb E M_0\le1$ for which the two events have different probabilities at $\alpha=1/2$.

[F1] [[def-probability-measure]]: a probability measure is a countably additive measure of total mass one.

[F2] [[def-conditional-expectation-given-a-sigma-algebra]]: a conditional-expectation version is measurable with respect to the conditioning sigma-algebra and has the same integral as the original variable over every event in that sigma-algebra.

[F3] [[def-conditional-expectation-as-an-ae-class]]: conditional expectations are unique almost-sure classes, so equalities of conditional expectations mean almost-sure equality.

[F4] [[def-expectation-of-a-nonnegative-or-integrable-random-variable]]: expectation is the integral against the probability measure; a bounded nonnegative measurable random variable has finite expectation.

[F5] [[prop-order-and-scalar-rules-for-the-nonnegative-integral]]: the nonnegative integral is monotone and is homogeneous for positive scalars.

[F6] [[def-martingale-submartingale-and-supermartingale]]: an integrable adapted process is a martingale when $\mathbb E[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=M_n$ almost surely for every $n$.

[F7] [[def-nonnegative-test-supermartingale]]: under each null law, an adapted integrable nonnegative supermartingale with initial expectation at most one is a nonnegative test supermartingale; the definition assumes AC.

[F8] [[thm-discrete-ville-inequality]]: under AC, a nonnegative test supermartingale satisfies both the supremum-event and first-crossing probability bounds by $\alpha$.

[A1] AC is inherited from the conditional-expectation class, martingale and test-supermartingale definitions, and the Ville theorem ([F3], [F6]–[F8]); the probability space, filtration, conditional means, and process below are explicit ([[def-axiom-of-choice]]).

## Counterexample

1.1 Set $p=1/4$, $c=2$, and $\Omega=\{a\}\cup\{b_k:k\ge1\}$ with $\mathcal F=2^\Omega$, $P(\{a\})=p$, and $P(\{b_k\})=(1-p)2^{-k}$. The geometric series makes $P$ a probability measure. For $n\ge0$, let $T_n=\{a\}\cup\{b_k:k>n\}$, let $\mathcal F_n$ be generated by the partition $\{\{b_1\},\ldots,\{b_n\},T_n\}$ (at $n=0$ this is the trivial partition), and put $m_n=P(T_n)=p+(1-p)2^{-n}$. These partitions refine as $n$ increases, so $(\mathcal F_n)$ is a filtration. [F1, given, construct, algebra]

2.1 Define $M_n=0$ on each singleton $\{b_j\}$ with $j\le n$, and set $M_n=cp/m_n$ on $T_n$. This is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable and satisfies $0\le M_n<c$. By [F1], [F4], and [F5], $\mathbb E_PM_n\le cP(\Omega)=c<\infty$, so it is integrable. [given, F1, F4, F5, step 1.1, algebra]

3.1 On every revealed singleton $\{b_j\}$, $j\le n$, both $M_n$ and $M_{n+1}$ are zero. The remaining atom $T_n$ splits into $\{b_{n+1}\}$, of mass $(1-p)2^{-(n+1)}$, and $T_{n+1}$, of mass $m_{n+1}$. The conditional mean of $M_{n+1}$ on $T_n$ is $$\frac{m_{n+1}(cp/m_{n+1})+(1-p)2^{-(n+1)}\cdot0}{m_n}=\frac{cp}{m_n}=M_n,$$ since the two parts' masses sum to $m_n$. These equalities on the finitely many atoms of $\mathcal F_n$ give a conditional-expectation version by [F2]; [F3] gives its almost-sure class, and [F6] makes $M$ a martingale. [F2, F3, F6, A1, step 1.1, step 2.1, algebra]

4.1 $M_0=cp=1/2$, so $\mathbb E_PM_0=cpP(\Omega)=1/2\le1$. With step 2.1 and step 3.1, [F7] shows that $M$ is a nonnegative test supermartingale for the singleton null family $\{P\}$. [F1, F4, F7, A1, step 2.1, step 3.1]

5.1 Step 2.1 shows $M_n<c=2$ at every finite time, so $\tau_{1/2}=\infty$ everywhere. On $a$, $M_n=cp/[p+(1-p)2^{-n}]\to c$, so $\sup_nM_n=c$; on each $b_k$, $M_n=0$ for $n\ge k$ and the finitely many earlier values are all below $c$. Hence $\{\sup_nM_n\ge2\}=\{a\}$, with probability $1/4$, whereas $P(\tau_{1/2}<\infty)=0$. Both are at most $\alpha=1/2$, as [F8] requires, but they are not equal. [given, F8, A1, step 2.1, step 4.1, algebra] ∎
