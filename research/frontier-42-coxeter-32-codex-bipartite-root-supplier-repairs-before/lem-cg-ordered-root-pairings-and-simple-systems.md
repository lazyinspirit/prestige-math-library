## Statement

Let $(W,S)$ be an irreducible Coxeter system of finite type with $S$ finite of cardinality $n\ge2$, with the data $\alpha_i$, $\beta_i$, $s_i$, $R_i$, $a$, $b$, $c$, $h$, $(\rho_i)$, $(\mu_i)$ and the linear map $\mu$ of [[def-cg-bipartite-coxeter-element-and-root-recursion]], and assume the conclusions of [[lem-cg-steinberg-bipartite-root-enumeration]] (so that $\Phi_+=\{\rho_1,\dots,\rho_{nh/2}\}$ and $\mu(\rho_i)=\mu_i$). Let $\ell_T$, $\le_T$, $M$, $F$ be reflection length, absolute order and the moved and fixed spaces, and let $P_\sigma:=\{\alpha\in\Phi_+:t_\alpha\le_T\sigma\}$ for $\sigma\le_Tc$, where $t_\alpha\in T$ is the reflection with normal $\alpha$ ([[thm-cg-root-inversion-formulas-and-strong-exchange]] (1), [[def-cg-reflection-length-absolute-order-and-moved-space]], [[thm-cg-carter-reflection-length-and-absolute-order]]). Then:

**(1) The basic identities.** For every $i\ge1$: $(c-\mathrm{id})\mu_i=-2\rho_i$, $\mu_i\cdot\rho_i=1$, and $\mu_i\in F(R(\rho_i)c)$, i.e. $R(\rho_i)c\,\mu_i=\mu_i$; moreover $F(R(\rho_i)c)$ is one dimensional and $\mu_i$ is its unique vector satisfying $\mu_i\cdot\rho_i=1$.

**(2) Sign and vanishing identities.** (a) $\mu_i\cdot\rho_j=-\mu_{j+n}\cdot\rho_i$ for all $i,j\ge1$; (b) $\mu_i\cdot\rho_j\ge0$ whenever $1\le i\le j\le nh/2$; (c) $\mu_{i+t}\cdot\rho_i=0$ for $1\le t\le n-1$ and all $i\ge1$; (d) $\mu_j\cdot\rho_i\le0$ whenever $1\le i<j\le nh/2$.

**(3) Separation from earlier cones.** If $1\le i_1<i_2<\dots<i_m<k\le nh/2$, then $\rho_k$ is not a nonnegative linear combination of $\rho_{i_1},\dots,\rho_{i_m}$.

**(4) Canonical simple systems of the subintervals.** Fix $\sigma\le_Tc$ with $\sigma\ne1$, put $k:=\ell_T(\sigma)=\dim M(\sigma)$ and write $P_\sigma=\{\tau_1<\tau_2<\dots<\tau_t\}$ in the global $\rho$-order. Then:

(i) $P_\sigma$ is the set of positive roots of the reflection subgroup $W_\sigma:=\{w\in W:M(w)\subseteq M(\sigma)\}$, and it contains a **simple system** $\Delta=\{\delta_1,\dots,\delta_k\}$: every root of $P_\sigma$ is a nonnegative linear combination of the $\delta$'s and $\delta_1,\dots,\delta_k$ are linearly independent; moreover $\delta_k=\tau_t$ and, recursively, $\delta_i$ is the last root of $P_\sigma$ lying in $M(\sigma R(\delta_k)R(\delta_{k-1})\cdots R(\delta_{i+1}))$; equivalently $\delta_i$ is the first root of $P_\sigma$ in $M(R(\delta_{i-1})\cdots R(\delta_1)\sigma)$, with the product empty for $i=1$, so in particular $\delta_1=\tau_1$.

(ii) $\varepsilon_i:=R(\delta_1)R(\delta_2)\cdots R(\delta_{i-1})\delta_i$ is a positive root for each $i$, and

$$\sigma=R(\varepsilon_k)R(\varepsilon_{k-1})\cdots R(\varepsilon_1),\qquad \sigma=R(\varepsilon_i)\,R(\delta_1)\cdots R(\delta_{i-1})R(\delta_{i+1})\cdots R(\delta_k).$$

(iii) Let $\mathcal S_\sigma:=\operatorname{cone}\{\delta_1,\dots,\delta_k\}\cap S(M(\sigma))$ be the spherical simplex, where $S(M(\sigma))=\{x\in M(\sigma):B(x,x)=1\}$. Its closed spherical wall opposite $\delta_i$ is
$$\operatorname{cone}\{\delta_j:j\ne i\}\cap S(M(\sigma))=\mathcal S_\sigma\cap \mu(\varepsilon_i)^\perp,$$
and its supporting hyperplane in $M(\sigma)$ is $M(\sigma)\cap\mu(\varepsilon_i)^\perp$.

(iv) If $i<j$ and $\varepsilon_i>\varepsilon_j$ in the global order, then $\varepsilon_i\cdot\varepsilon_j=0$, so $R(\varepsilon_i)$ and $R(\varepsilon_j)$ commute; the reordering $\theta_1<\theta_2<\dots<\theta_k$ of $\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_k$ in the global order satisfies $\sigma=R(\theta_k)R(\theta_{k-1})\cdots R(\theta_1)$ and is lexicographically first among all increasing $k$-tuples $\eta_1<\cdots<\eta_k$ in $P_\sigma$ for which $R(\eta_k)\cdots R(\eta_1)\le_Tc$ and $\ell_T(R(\eta_k)\cdots R(\eta_1))=k$.

(v) If $\ell_T(\sigma)=2$ then $\{\tau_1,\tau_t\}$ is a simple system of $P_\sigma$, the only factorizations of $\sigma$ as a product of two reflections in $W$ are $\sigma=R(\tau_1)R(\tau_t)=R(\tau_2)R(\tau_1)=\dots=R(\tau_t)R(\tau_{t-1})$, and $\tau_1\cdot\tau_t\le0$ while $\tau_i\cdot\tau_{i+1}\ge0$ for $1\le i<t$; dually, for distinct positive roots $\rho_i,\rho_j$ with $R(\rho_i)R(\rho_j)\le_Tc$ one has: if $i<j$ then $\rho_i\cdot\rho_j\le0$, and if $i>j$ then $\rho_i\cdot\rho_j\ge0$.

(vi) Whenever $\tau_{i_1}<\dots<\tau_{i_k}$ is an increasing tuple of roots of $P_\sigma$ for which $R(\tau_{i_k})\cdots R(\tau_{i_1})\le_Tc$ and $\ell_T(R(\tau_{i_k})\cdots R(\tau_{i_1}))=k$, one has $\tau_{i_j}\ge\theta_j$ for every $j=1,\dots,k$.

No Choice is used.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The finite-type irreducible datum with $|S|=n\ge2$ and the bipartite data $\alpha_i,s_i,R_i,\beta_i,\rho_i,\mu_i,a,b,c,h$ of [[def-cg-bipartite-coxeter-element-and-root-recursion]], the enumeration of [[lem-cg-steinberg-bipartite-root-enumeration]], and an element $\sigma\le_Tc$.

[F1] $\Phi_+=\{\rho_1,\dots,\rho_{nh/2}\}$ with the $\rho_i$ pairwise distinct and $\rho_{i+n}=C\rho_i$, $\mu_{i+n}=C\mu_i$ for all $i\ge1$, where $C:=\rho(c)$; $C-\mathrm{id}$ is invertible, $\mu(\rho_i)=\mu_i$, and $M(c)=V$. Hence $\ell_T(c)=n$. [[lem-cg-steinberg-bipartite-root-enumeration]] (1)-(4) [[thm-cg-carter-reflection-length-and-absolute-order]] (1)

[F2] $(C-\mathrm{id})\mu_i=-2\rho_i$ for all $i\ge1$; $\mu_i\cdot\rho_i=1$; $\mu_i\in F(R(\rho_i)c)$ and $F(R(\rho_i)c)$ is one-dimensional; $\mu$ is $C$-equivariant. [[lem-cg-steinberg-bipartite-root-enumeration]] (4) [[def-cg-bipartite-coxeter-element-and-root-recursion]] (4)

[F3] $B$ is positive definite, every root has $B$-norm $1$, reflections act by $r_\alpha(x)=x-2B(x,\alpha)\alpha$, and $B(Cx,Cy)=B(x,y)$. For each positive root $\alpha$ there is a unique reflection $t_\alpha$ with $\rho(t_\alpha)=r_\alpha$; its moved space is $\mathbb R\alpha$. [[def-cg-real-coxeter-form-and-reflection]] (3) [[lem-cg-reflection-representation-descends-and-root-norms]] (2),(3) [[thm-cg-root-inversion-formulas-and-strong-exchange]] (1) [[lem-cg-reflection-form-invariance-and-rank-two-orders]] (2)

[F4] Positive roots have nonnegative simple-root coefficients, and $B(f,\alpha)>0$ for every $f\in\mathcal C^\circ$ and $\alpha\in\Phi_+$. [[thm-cg-root-sign-and-simple-reflection-positivity]] (1),(2)

[F5] Carter's formula gives $\ell_T(w)=\dim M(w)$; $u\le_Tv$ iff $\ell_T(v)=\ell_T(u)+\ell_T(u^{-1}v)$; $u\le_Tv$ implies $M(u)\subseteq M(v)$; if $u,v\le_Tc$, then $u\le_Tv$ iff $M(u)\subseteq M(v)$; and $\ell_T$ is invariant under conjugation and satisfies the triangle inequality. [[thm-cg-carter-reflection-length-and-absolute-order]] (1)-(3)

[F6] On the positive-definite space $(V,B)$, $M(A)=F(A)^\perp$ for orthogonal $A$. For $U\subseteq M(A)$ there is a unique orthogonal restriction $A_U\le_{\mathrm O}A$ with moved space $U$, and every line $L$ is the moved space of the unique orthogonal reflection $\mathrm{id}-2\Pi_L$. For group elements, orthogonal order and reflection order agree by Carter's formula. [[lem-cg-orthogonal-wall-form-and-subspace-restriction]] (1),(3)-(5) [[thm-cg-carter-reflection-length-and-absolute-order]] (1),(2)

[F7] In a reduced reflection factorization $w=t_1\cdots t_m$, the prefix-conjugated root normals form a basis of $M(w)$. [[lem-cg-reflection-factorizations-and-independent-normals]] (3)

[F8] Every nonidentity $w$ has a reduced reflection factorization of length $\dim M(w)$; the factorization lemma also supplies a root normal in $M(w)$ for its induction step. [[lem-cg-reflection-factorizations-and-independent-normals]] (1)-(3)

[F9] The root arrangement $\{H_\alpha:\alpha\in\Phi\}$ is finite. [[def-cg-finite-reflection-arrangement-and-spherical-chambers]]

[F10] For every nonzero point $x\in g\mathcal C_I$, its stabilizer is $gW_Ig^{-1}$; the chambers of a finite standard parabolic reflection group are simplicial, with inward root normals as their simple system. [[thm-cg-finite-chamber-tiling-and-coset-face-identification]] (2)-(3)

[F11] In rank two, the chamber angle is $\pi/m$, the two simple-root rays enclose angle $\pi-\pi/m$, and the positive root rays are the equally spaced dihedral orbit between them; distinct simple roots have nonpositive pairing. [[lem-cg-reflection-form-invariance-and-rank-two-orders]] (3)

[F12] No finite family of proper linear subspaces of a finite-dimensional real vector space covers the whole space. [[lem-finite-dimensional-space-over-an-infinite-field-is-not-a-finite-union-of-proper-subspaces]]

[F13] For a reduced simple-generator expression $w=s_1\cdots s_m$, every prefix-conjugated normal $\rho(s_1\cdots s_{i-1})e_{s_i}$ is a positive root. [[thm-cg-root-inversion-formulas-and-strong-exchange]] (2)



## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 For every $i\ge1$, [F2] gives $(C-\mathrm{id})\mu_i=-2\rho_i$. Since $C$ is an isometry and $B(\rho_i,\rho_i)=1$, $B(\mu_i,\rho_i)=1$ follows by expanding $B(C\mu_i,C\mu_i)=B(\mu_i-2\rho_i,\mu_i-2\rho_i)$. The same identity gives $C\mu_i=\mu_i-2\rho_i$, so $r_{\rho_i}C\mu_i=\mu_i$ and $\mu_i\in F(r_{\rho_i}C)$. For $1\le i\le n$, the reflection word $r_{\rho_i}C$ cancels to $R_1\cdots R_{i-1}R_{i+1}\cdots R_n$, so its reflection length is at most $n-1$; the triangle inequality and $\ell_T(c)=n$ give the lower bound $n-1$. Thus its fixed space is one-dimensional. Since $r_{\rho_{i+n}}C=C(r_{\rho_i}C)C^{-1}$, the same dimension holds for all $i$. The vector $\mu_i$ is nonzero because $\mu_i\cdot\rho_i=1$, and it is the unique vector in that line with this pairing. This proves (1). [F1, F2, F3, F5, algebra]

1.2 For all $i,j\ge1$, $\mu_i\cdot\rho_j=-\mu_{j+n}\cdot\rho_i$. Indeed, $\rho_i=\tfrac12(\mu_i-C\mu_i)$ by [F2], so
$-\mu_{j+n}\cdot\rho_i=-C\mu_j\cdot\tfrac12(\mu_i-C\mu_i)=\tfrac12(\mu_j-C\mu_j)\cdot\mu_i=\rho_j\cdot\mu_i$, using the isometry of $C$ and [F1]-[F2]. This is (2)(a). [F1, F2, algebra]

1.3 Suppose $1\le i\le j\le nh/2$. If $i>n$, then $j>n$ and $C$-equivariance gives $\mu_i\cdot\rho_j=\mu_{i-n}\cdot\rho_{j-n}$. Repeating reduces to a first index at most $n$ while keeping both indices positive and ordered. For $i\le n$, $\mu_i=\beta_i$, and $\beta_i\cdot\rho_j$ is the coefficient of $\alpha_i$ in the positive root $\rho_j$; this is nonnegative by [F4]. Thus (2)(b) holds. [F1, F2, F4, algebra]

1.4 Every reflection lies below $c$. Indeed, $M(c)=V$ by [F1]. For a positive root $\alpha$, the orthogonal reflection with moved line $\mathbb R\alpha$ is uniquely $r_\alpha=\rho(t_\alpha)$ by [F3],[F6]; the Wall restriction for this line is below $C=\rho(c)$ and has moved space $\mathbb R\alpha$. The Wall dimension identity and Carter's formula therefore give $t_\alpha\le_Tc$. [F1, F3, F5, F6]

1.5 We use two elementary consequences of absolute order. First, if $w=r_1\cdots r_m$ is a reduced reflection factorization and $p<q$, then $r_pr_q\le_Tw$: distinct root-reflections have product moved space the two-plane spanned by their distinct normal lines, so their product has reflection length $2$ by Carter's formula. Write $w=xr_pyr_qz$; then $(r_pr_q)^{-1}w=r_qr_pxr_pyr_qz=(r_q(r_pxr_p)y r_q)z$, a product of $m-2$ reflections because conjugation preserves reflections. The triangle inequality forces this complement to have length $m-2$. Second, if $u\le_Tv\le_Tw$, then $v^{-1}w\le_Tu^{-1}w$: write $v=ux$, $w=vy$ with additive reflection lengths; then $u^{-1}w=xy$, while $y^{-1}xy$ has the same length as $x$, so $y\le_Txy$. These facts will be used below. [F3, F5, algebra]

2.1 To prove (2)(c), first reduce $i$ modulo $n$ using $C$-equivariance, which preserves $\mu_{i+t}\cdot\rho_i$, and assume $1\le i\le n$. If $i+t\le n$, then $\mu_{i+t}=\beta_{i+t}$ and $\rho_i=R_1\cdots R_{i-1}\alpha_i$ has no $\alpha_{i+t}$ coefficient, so the pairing is zero. If $i+t>n$, put $j=i+t-n$, so $1\le j<i$. By (2)(a), $\mu_{i+t}\cdot\rho_i=\mu_{j+n}\cdot\rho_i=-\mu_i\cdot\rho_j=-\beta_i\cdot\rho_j=0$, since $\rho_j$ is supported on $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_j$. Now if $1\le i<j\le nh/2$ and $j<i+n$, (2)(d) is (2)(c) with $t=j-i$; if $j\ge i+n$, (2)(a) and (2)(b) give $\mu_j\cdot\rho_i=-\mu_{i+n}\cdot\rho_j\le0$. This proves (2)(c)-(d). [F1, F2, F4, step 1.3, algebra]

2.2 For every $\sigma\le_Tc$, one has $P_\sigma=\Phi_+\cap M(\sigma)$. If $\alpha\in P_\sigma$, then $M(t_\alpha)=\mathbb R\alpha\subseteq M(\sigma)$ by [F5]. Conversely, if $\alpha\in\Phi_+\cap M(\sigma)$, then $t_\alpha\le_Tc$ by step 1.4 and $\sigma\le_Tc$, so moved-space rigidity in [F5] gives $t_\alpha\le_T\sigma$. Also $P_\sigma$ spans $M(\sigma)$: choose a reduced reflection factorization of $\sigma$; its prefix-conjugated normals are a basis of $M(\sigma)$ by [F7], and the reflections with those normals have moved lines in $M(\sigma)$, hence lie below $\sigma$ by the same common-upper-bound argument. Taking the positive sign of each normal puts a spanning set in $P_\sigma$. [F3, F5, F7, F8, step 1.4]

2.3 For any increasing tuple $a_1<\cdots<a_m$ of positive roots, one has $\ell_T(R(a_1)\cdots R(a_m)c)=n-m$ if and only if $\mu(a_i)\cdot a_j=0$ for all $i>j$. If $m=0$, the left side is $\ell_T(c)=n$ by [F1], and the zero-pairing condition is vacuous; hence assume $m\ge1$. Put $r_i=R(a_i)$. The length equality is equivalent to the reverse product $q=r_m\cdots r_1$ being a reduced element below $c$: its complement is $q^{-1}c=r_1\cdots r_m c$. In fact, if $\ell_T(q^{-1}c)=n-m$, then $\ell_T(q)\le m$ and $n=\ell_T(c)\le\ell_T(q)+\ell_T(q^{-1}c)\le n$, so both inequalities are equalities; the converse is the defining absolute-order equality. For the forward implication, the pair-product fact in step 1.5 gives $r_i r_j\le_Tc$ whenever $i>j$. Since $\ell_T(r_i c)=n-1$, this gives $r_j\le_Tr_i c$ from $\ell_T(r_j r_i c)=n-2$; hence $M(r_j)\subseteq M(r_i c)=F(r_i c)^\perp$. The vector $\mu(a_i)$ is a nonzero member of the one-dimensional fixed space $F(r_i c)$, so $\mu(a_i)\cdot a_j=0$. Conversely, suppose all these pairings vanish. The matrix $[\mu(a_i)\cdot a_j]$ is upper triangular with diagonal $1$ by (1), so both the $a_i$ and the $\mu(a_i)$ are linearly independent; in particular $m\le n$. Induct downward on $i$ to prove $q_i:=r_m\cdots r_i\le_Tc$ with length $m-i+1$. The base $q_m=r_m$ is step 1.4. If $q_{i+1}\le_Tc$, then each factor $r_j$ with $j>i$ satisfies $r_j\le_Tq_{i+1}$: writing a reduced factorization $q_{i+1}=x r_j y$, the product $r_jq_{i+1}=(r_jxr_j)y$ uses one fewer reflections, and the triangle inequality makes that expression reduced. Put $v=q_{i+1}^{-1}c$. Complement order reversal from step 1.5 gives $v\le_Tr_jc$, so $\mu(a_j)\in F(r_jc)\subseteq F(v)$ for every $j>i$. These $m-i$ independent vectors form a basis of $F(v)$, whose dimension is $m-i$ by Carter's formula. The assumed zero pairings give $a_i\in F(v)^\perp=M(v)$. The Wall restriction for the line $\mathbb Ra_i$ gives $r_i\le_Tv$; writing $v=r_i z$ then gives $c=q_{i+1}r_i z$, with lengths adding, so $q_i=q_{i+1}r_i\le_Tc$ and $\ell_T(q_i)=m-i+1$. At $i=1$ this proves the claimed equivalence. The same argument applies to every subtuple because the vanishing conditions are inherited. [F1, F2, F3, F5, F6, step 1.1, step 1.4, step 1.5, algebra]

3.1 If $\rho_k=\sum_{r=1}^m a_r\rho_{i_r}$ with $a_r\ge0$ and $i_1<\cdots<i_m<k$, then (2)(d) gives $\mu_k\cdot\rho_{i_r}\le0$ for every $r$, so $1=\mu_k\cdot\rho_k=\sum_r a_r(\mu_k\cdot\rho_{i_r})\le0$, a contradiction. This proves (3). [step 1.1, step 2.1, algebra]

3.2 Put $E=M(\sigma)$ and $F=E^\perp$. Then $W_\sigma=\{w:M(w)\subseteq E\}$ is the pointwise stabilizer of $F$, because $M(w)=F(w)^\perp$. If $F\ne0$, choose $x\in F$ outside every $F\cap H_\alpha$ for which $F\not\subseteq H_\alpha$; this is possible by [F9] and [F12], and $x\ne0$. By [F10], $\operatorname{Stab}_W(x)=gW_Ig^{-1}$ for some $g,I$. Each conjugate simple generator has a root hyperplane containing $x$, hence, by the choice of $x$, containing all of $F$; therefore every generator of this stabilizer fixes $F$ pointwise. Conversely every element fixing $F$ fixes $x$, so $W_\sigma=\operatorname{Stab}_W(x)=gW_Ig^{-1}$. If $F=0$, then $W_\sigma=W$. By step 2.2 the roots of this finite parabolic subgroup are exactly $\pm P_\sigma$, and they span $E$. Choose $f\in\mathcal C^\circ$ and project it orthogonally to $E$; the projection has positive pairing with every root of $P_\sigma$, so it lies in a chamber of the parabolic arrangement. The chamber is simplicial by [F10]; its inward unit root normals form a linearly independent set $\Delta$ of size $k=\dim E$, and every root in $P_\sigma$ is a nonnegative linear combination of these normals. Thus $\Delta$ is a simple system as claimed. [F3, F4, F5, F9, F10, F12, step 2.2]

4.1 In any such positive root subsystem with simple system $\Gamma$, its first root in the global order is a member of $\Gamma$. For if the first root $\eta$ were not simple, write $\eta=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}b_\gamma\gamma$ with $b_\gamma\ge0$; each $\gamma$ is later than $\eta$, so (2)(d) gives $\mu(\gamma)\cdot\eta\le0$. Linearity of $\mu$ and (1) would give $1=\mu(\eta)\cdot\eta=\sum b_\gamma(\mu(\gamma)\cdot\eta)\le0$, impossible. Also the last root of $P_\sigma$ belongs to $\Delta$: otherwise it is a nonnegative combination of the earlier simple roots, contrary to step 3.1. [F1, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.1, algebra]

5.1 We prove the recursive selection and the factorization $\sigma=R(\delta_1)\cdots R(\delta_k)$ by induction on $k$. For $k=1$, $W_\sigma$ has rank one, $P_\sigma$ has its single positive root $\delta_1$, and $\sigma=R(\delta_1)$. For $k>1$, order the simple roots increasingly; step 4.1 makes the last one $\delta_k=\tau_t$. Put $r=R(\delta_k)$. Since $r\le_T\sigma$, write $\sigma=ru$ with $\ell_T(u)=k-1$; then $\sigma':=\sigma r=rur$ has length $k-1$. Write $c=\sigma v$ with $\ell_T(v)=n-k$. The identity $c=\sigma'(rv)$ and the bounds $\ell_T(rv)\le n-k+1$ and $n=\ell_T(c)\le\ell_T(\sigma')+\ell_T(rv)$ force $\ell_T(rv)=n-k+1$, so $\sigma'\le_Tc$. Also $\sigma'$ fixes $F$ pointwise, hence $M(\sigma')\subseteq E$. [F5, step 1.4, step 3.2]

6.1 Let $q$ be the global index with $\delta_k=\rho_q$ and put $\nu=C\mu_q=\mu_{q+n}$. For every $\rho_j\in P_\sigma$ one has $j\le q$ and, by (2)(a) and $C$-equivariance, $\nu\cdot\rho_j=\mu_{q+n}\cdot\rho_j=-\mu_{j+n}\cdot\rho_{q+n}=-\mu_j\cdot\rho_q\le0$ by (2)(b); at $j=q$ this is $-1$. Since $\mu_q\in F(r c)$, conjugating by $C$ gives $\nu\in F(c r)$; and $\ell_T(cr)=n-1$, so $F(cr)=\mathbb R\nu$ and $M(cr)=\nu^\perp$. Moreover $\sigma'\le_Tc r$: its complement is $\sigma'^{-1}cr=rvr$, of length $n-k$, while $\ell_T(cr)=n-1$; the lengths add to $n-1$. Hence $U:=M(\sigma')$ is contained in $E\cap\nu^\perp$, and both have dimension $k-1$ because $\nu\cdot\delta_k=-1$. Thus $U=E\cap\nu^\perp$. The roots $P_{\sigma'}=\Phi_+\cap U$ span $U$ by step 2.2. Each is a nonnegative combination of $\Delta$ and is orthogonal to $\nu$; since $\nu\cdot\delta_k=-1$ and $\nu\cdot\delta_j\le0$ for every $j$, every root in $P_{\sigma'}$ is a combination only of the $\delta_j$ for which $\nu\cdot\delta_j=0$. Those zero-pairing simple roots must span the $(k-1)$-space $U$, so they are exactly $\delta_1,\dots,\delta_{k-1}$. They form a simple system for $P_{\sigma'}$. Induction gives $\sigma'=R(\delta_1)\cdots R(\delta_{k-1})$ and proves the reverse selection at each rank. Therefore $\sigma=R(\delta_1)\cdots R(\delta_k)$. For each $i$, its prefix $p_i:=R(\delta_1)\cdots R(\delta_i)$ and complementary suffix $R(\delta_{i+1})\cdots R(\delta_k)$ multiply to $\sigma$ and have at most $i$ and $k-i$ reflections, so both lengths attain these bounds. Thus $M(p_i)$, contained in $\operatorname{span}(\delta_1,\dots,\delta_i)$, has dimension $i$ and equals that span. Since $\sigma R(\delta_k)\cdots R(\delta_{i+1})=p_i$, the induction shows that $\delta_i$ is the last root of $P_\sigma$ in this subspace. [F1, F2, F4, F5, step 1.3, step 2.2, step 5.1, algebra]

7.1 The other recursive description follows by considering the suffix $q_i:=R(\delta_i)\cdots R(\delta_k)$. The factorization in step 6.1 gives $\ell_T(q_i)=k-i+1$: its prefix and suffix factors together multiply to $\sigma$ of length $k$, and their lengths are bounded by their numbers of reflections, whose sum is $k$. Writing $p_i:=R(\delta_1)\cdots R(\delta_{i-1})$, the same length equality forces $\ell_T(p_i)=i-1$, so $q_i^{-1}\sigma=q_i^{-1}p_iq_i$ has length $i-1$ and $q_i\le_T\sigma$. If $c=\sigma v$ as above, then $q_i^{-1}c=q_i^{-1}(R(\delta_1)\cdots R(\delta_{i-1}))q_i v$ has length at most $(i-1)+(n-k)=n-\ell_T(q_i)$; the triangle inequality forces equality, so $q_i\le_Tc$. Its moved space is $\operatorname{span}(\delta_i,\dots,\delta_k)$, since its factors fix the orthogonal complement of that span and its moved dimension is $k-i+1$. Thus $P_{q_i}=P_\sigma\cap M(q_i)$, and $\{\delta_i,\dots,\delta_k\}$ is a simple system for this subsystem: a root in the tail span has zero coefficients on $\delta_1,\dots,\delta_{i-1}$ in its nonnegative $\Delta$-expansion. By step 4.1 the first root of $P_{q_i}$ is a simple root of this tail system; its first simple root is $\delta_i$. Finally $R(\delta_{i-1})\cdots R(\delta_1)\sigma=q_i$ by cancellation, so $\delta_i$ is the first root of $P_\sigma$ in the moved space in (4)(i), and $\delta_1=\tau_1$. This proves (4)(i). [F5, step 4.1, step 6.1, algebra]

7.2 Put $D_i:=R(\delta_1)\cdots R(\delta_{i-1})$ and $\varepsilon_i:=D_i\delta_i$. The prefix $R(\delta_1)\cdots R(\delta_i)$ has reflection length $i$ by the factorization of $\sigma$ and the triangle inequality, so this simple-generator word in the Coxeter system of $W_\sigma$ is reduced. The root-inversion formula [F13], applied to that parabolic system, gives $\varepsilon_i\in P_\sigma$, hence it is a positive root. Conjugation gives $R(\varepsilon_i)=D_iR(\delta_i)D_i^{-1}$; multiplying these identities and cancelling adjacent inverse prefixes yields $\sigma=R(\varepsilon_k)\cdots R(\varepsilon_1)$. Also $R(\varepsilon_i)D_iR(\delta_{i+1})\cdots R(\delta_k)=D_iR(\delta_i)R(\delta_{i+1})\cdots R(\delta_k)=\sigma$. This proves (4)(ii). [F3, F5, F13, step 6.1, algebra]

7.3 Suppose $\ell_T(\sigma)=2$ and $P_\sigma=\{\tau_1<\cdots<\tau_t\}$. By (4)(i), $\Delta=\{\tau_1,\tau_t\}$ and $\sigma=R(\tau_1)R(\tau_t)$. The rank-two parabolic is dihedral: its $t$ positive root rays are equally spaced in the sector bounded by the simple-root rays, whose angle is $\pi-\pi/t$. The global order on these roots agrees with their angular order: if $r\ge2$ and $\tau_{r+1}$ had smaller angle than $\tau_r$, it would lie in the positive cone on the earlier roots $\tau_1,\tau_r$, contradicting step 3.1; for $r=1$ such a decrease is impossible because $\tau_1$ is a boundary ray. The product of two reflections in this plane is the rotation through twice the difference of their axis angles. Thus the factorizations of the Coxeter rotation $\sigma$ into two reflections are exactly $R(\tau_1)R(\tau_t)=R(\tau_2)R(\tau_1)=\cdots=R(\tau_t)R(\tau_{t-1})$. These are all factorizations in $W$: if $\sigma=R(\alpha)R(\beta)$, then $M(\sigma)\subseteq\operatorname{span}(\alpha,\beta)$, and both spaces have dimension $2$, so $\alpha,\beta\in M(\sigma)$ and their positive representatives lie in $P_\sigma$ by step 2.2. The endpoint angle gives $\tau_1\cdot\tau_t=-\cos(\pi/t)\le0$ and each adjacent angle gives $\tau_i\cdot\tau_{i+1}=\cos(\pi/t)\ge0$. [F3, F5, F11, step 3.1, step 2.2, step 6.1]

8.1 Fix $i$. The second factorization in (4)(ii) writes $\sigma=R(\varepsilon_i)q_i'$ where $q_i'$ is the product of the other $k-1$ simple reflections in $\Delta$. Thus $\ell_T(q_i')=k-1$, $R(\varepsilon_i)\le_T\sigma$, and $q_i'=R(\varepsilon_i)\sigma$. Since $c=\sigma v$ with $\ell_T(v)=n-k$, $R(\varepsilon_i)c=q_i'v$ is reduced, so $q_i'\le_TR(\varepsilon_i)c$. It follows that $\mu(\varepsilon_i)\in F(q_i')$. The moved space $M(q_i')$ is exactly $\operatorname{span}\{\delta_j:j\ne i\}$: it is contained there because those are its reflection normals, and both dimensions are $k-1$. Hence $\mu(\varepsilon_i)\cdot\delta_j=0$ for $j\ne i$. Since $\varepsilon_i-\delta_i$ is a linear combination of $\delta_1,\dots,\delta_{i-1}$, (1) gives $1=\mu(\varepsilon_i)\cdot\varepsilon_i=\mu(\varepsilon_i)\cdot\delta_i$. Thus $\mu(\varepsilon_i)$ is the dual basis to $\Delta$. For $x=\sum_j a_j\delta_j$ with $a_j\ge0$, $\mu(\varepsilon_i)\cdot x=a_i$; intersecting the cone with this zero hyperplane and with the unit sphere proves the wall equality, and the supporting hyperplane in $E$ is $E\cap\mu(\varepsilon_i)^\perp$. This proves (4)(iii). [F2, F3, F5, step 7.2, algebra]

8.2 If distinct positive roots $\rho_i,\rho_j$ satisfy $R(\rho_i)R(\rho_j)\le_Tc$, their product has reflection length $2$ and belongs to the rank-two case of step 7.3. Both roots lie in the corresponding $P_\sigma$ by moved-space rigidity. If $i<j$, the only increasing-order two-factorization in step 7.3 is the endpoint factorization, so $\rho_i\cdot\rho_j\le0$. If $i>j$, the factorization is one of the adjacent descending pairs, so $\rho_i\cdot\rho_j\ge0$. This proves (4)(v). [F3, F5, step 7.3]

9.1 If $i<j$ and $\varepsilon_i>\varepsilon_j$, the factorization $\sigma=R(\varepsilon_k)\cdots R(\varepsilon_1)$ is reduced, and the pair-product fact of step 1.5 gives $R(\varepsilon_j)R(\varepsilon_i)\le_T\sigma\le_Tc$. The rank-two sign result of step 8.2 gives $\varepsilon_i\cdot\varepsilon_j\le0$. On the other hand, since distinct simple roots have nonpositive pairings by [F11], applying $R(\delta_{j-1}),\dots,R(\delta_{i+1})$ successively to $\delta_j$ yields a nonnegative combination of $\delta_{i+1},\dots,\delta_j$, and
$\varepsilon_i\cdot\varepsilon_j=-\delta_i\cdot(R(\delta_{i+1})\cdots R(\delta_{j-1})\delta_j)\ge0$.
Thus the pairing is zero, and the two reflections commute. Sorting the $\varepsilon_i$ into global order uses only swaps of such inverted commuting pairs, so the product is unchanged and $\sigma=R(\theta_k)\cdots R(\theta_1)$. [F3, F11, step 7.2, step 1.5, step 8.2, algebra]

10.1 The tuple $\theta_1<\cdots<\theta_k$ has reverse product $\sigma\le_Tc$ of length $k$, so it is among the tuples in (4)(iv). Let $\eta_1<\cdots<\eta_k$ be any other such tuple. By step 2.3 every prefix $\eta_1<\cdots<\eta_r$ also has reverse product below $c$ of length $r$, so its $r$ roots are linearly independent. For each $s\ge r$, every root $\tau\in P_\sigma$ with $\tau<\theta_r$ has $\mu(\theta_s)\cdot\tau=0$: its pairing is nonnegative because $\tau$ is a nonnegative combination of $\Delta$ and the vectors $\mu(\varepsilon_i)$ are dual to $\Delta$ by step 8.1, while it is nonpositive by (2)(d). The restrictions to $E$ of the $k-r+1$ functionals $\mu(\theta_r),\dots,\mu(\theta_k)$ are independent, again by the dual-basis property. Their common kernel in $E$ therefore has dimension $r-1$. If $\eta_r<\theta_r$, the $r$ independent roots $\eta_1,\dots,\eta_r$ all lie in that kernel, a contradiction. Hence $\eta_r\ge\theta_r$ for every $r$, which proves componentwise domination (4)(vi) and the lexicographic minimality in (4)(iv). No Choice is used.
