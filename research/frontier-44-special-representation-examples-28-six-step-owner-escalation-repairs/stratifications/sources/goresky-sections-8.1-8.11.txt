8.1. The plan. To proceed further with sheaf theory we need to banish Cantor-like phenomena
but still allow singularities. Stratification theory is the solution and the resulting sheaves are called

We say the pair (X, Y ) satisfies condition B if it does so at every point y ∈Y . The decomposition

8.6. Moving the wall. The isotopy lemma is almost always applied following a simple paradigm.
The proof of part (3) Theorem 8.4 provides a simple illustration. The link LY (y, ϵ) is a transversal
intersection of W with (∂Bϵ(y)) and with Ny so it is stratified by the intersections of strata of
these factors. Imagine continuously changing ϵ, y, and Ny, starting at time t = 0, maintaining
transversality of the factors at each instant, to obtain a second model LY (y′, ϵ′) of the link at time
t = 1. This gives a 1-parameter family L of stratified spaces, and a mapping of t : L →R landing
on some interval (a, b) containing [0, 1] ⊂R. By construction, the mapping is a submersion on each
stratum. The isotopy lemma then gives a stratum preserving homeomorphism L ∼= (a, b)×LY (y, ϵ)
and therefore LY (y, ϵ) = t−1(0) ∼= t−1(1) = LY (y′, ϵ′).
In this way the problem presented in Figure 2 can be “resolved”: the space is locally homeo-
morphic, (by a stratum preserving homeomorphism, smooth on each stratum) to the product of
an interval and a spider with 4 legs.
The book [59] contains many such 1-parameter families of “pieces” of W, most of which are
obtained by intersecting W with a 1-parameter family of codimension one subsets, or “walls”

8.9. Whitney himself outlined a procedure for proving that any closed subset W of Euclidean
space defined by analytic equations admits a Whitney stratification. The idea is to start with the
open, nonsingular part W 0 of W as the “top” stratum, and then to look at the set of points in
the singular set Σ = W −W 0 where condition B fails. He proves that this is an analytic subset of
codimension two, whose complement in Σ is therefore the first singular stratum, W 1. Now, carry
both W 0 and W 1 along, looking at the set of points (in what remains) where condition B fails, and
continue in this way inductively. The result is a canonical and minimal Whitney stratification.
Since Whitney’s early work, many advances have been made in the subject (see [143]). Theorem
8.10 below is a partial summary of the work of many people, including [70], [71] [72], [104], [92],
[144], [132], [20], [48], [49], [76], [93].
8.10. Theorem. The following sets admit Whitney stratifications: real and complex alge-
braic varieties, real and complex analytic varieties, semi-algebraic and semi-analytic vari-
eties, subanaltyic sets, and sets with o-minimal structure. Given such an algebraic (resp.
analytic etc.) variety W and a locally finite union Z of algebraic (resp. analytic etc.) sub-
varieties, the stratification of W can be chosen so that Z is a union of strata. Given an
algebraic (resp. proper analytic etc.) mapping f : W →W ′ of algebraic (resp. analytic etc.)
varieties, it is possible to find algebraic (resp. analytic) Whitney stratifications of W, W ′ so
that the mapping f is stratified (see §8.1). Whitney stratified sets can be triangulated by a
triangulation that is smooth on each stratum, such that that the closure of each stratum is a
subcomplex of the triangulation.
A subanalytic set is the image under a projection (for example, a linear projection Rm →Rn) of
an analytic or a semi-analytic set. O-minimal structures allow for certain non-analytic functions
