## Statement

Let $f:K\to L$ be a simplicial map of finite complexes; vertex identifications are allowed. Its topological mapping cylinder
$$M_f=(|K|\times[0,1]\sqcup|L|)/((x,0)\sim f(x))$$
has a finite triangulation of dimension at most $\max(\dim K+1,\dim L)$ and a homeomorphism $H$ to $M_f$ fixing both boundary copies. The triangulated cylinder retraction to $|L|$ is piecewise linear. Before triangulating, its cells and $H$ have the explicit formulas in the proof. For every finite collection of pairs of subcomplexes $(K_j,L_j)$ with $f(K_j)\subseteq L_j$, the corresponding cylinders are subpolyhedra; one simultaneous subdivision makes them subcomplexes. The construction also retains the top subpolyhedron $f^{-1}(|L_j|)$ for each $L_j$. On each cell away from the collapsed end, $H$ is smooth in affine coordinates; no immersion or smooth tangent-star claim is made at a collapsed end. All boundaries may be subdivided: the lemma does not promise an extension of an arbitrarily fixed boundary triangulation without subdivision.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** Finite $K,L$, simplicial $f$, and finitely many subcomplex pairs as above.

[F1] Realizations have barycentric coordinates; for finite complexes their weak and Euclidean topologies coincide and they are compact Hausdorff ([[def-geometric-realization-of-an-abstract-simplicial-complex]], [[lem-finite-simplicial-weak-topology-agrees-with-euclidean-topology]], [[prop-a-finite-simplicial-complex-has-compact-hausdorff-realization]]).

## Proof

1.1 Write $x_v$ for coordinates on $K$ and $z_i=f_i(x)=\sum_{f(v)=i}x_v$ for coordinates on $L$. In every simplex of $K$, cut by the finitely many hyperplanes $f_i=f_j$. Take the resulting closed convex cells and all their faces. Their intersections are faces: cutting each original simplex by the same hyperplane arrangement gives intersections by setting some of its defining affine inequalities to equality, and the original simplex faces already agree. This is a finite polytopal subdivision $\mathcal C$ of $K$. For $C\in\mathcal C$ let $I(C)$ be the vertices $i$ whose $f_i$ is maximal on every point of $C$. It is nonempty; if $C'$ is a face then $I(C)\subseteq I(C')$. It lies in the image of each source simplex containing $C$, so every subset of $I(C)$ is a simplex of $L$. For each nonempty simplex $A$ on vertices in $I(C)$ include the join cell $A*C$ in the Euclidean join of the two disjoint realizations. Include $L$ itself and every face of each such join. The identity $(A*C)\cap(A'*C')=(A\cap A')*(C\cap C')$, with the evident boundary conventions, shows that these cells form a finite polytopal complex $T_f$: intersections are faces of both cells. Its upper boundary is exactly $|K|$ and its lower boundary is $|L|$. A point in its interior has unique join coordinates $(1-t)y+t x$ with $0<t<1$, $y\in|L|$, $x\in|K|$; membership means that the positive-coordinate support of $y$ consists of vertices where $f_i(x)$ attains its maximum. [F1, construct]

2.1 Triangulate any finite convex polytopal complex by the following finite induction on dimension. Choose an interior point in each nonempty cell, having already chosen those of its proper faces, and cone its previously triangulated boundary from that point. A ray from the interior point hits the boundary uniquely, so these cones cover the cell, have disjoint relative interiors, and meet along the common cones over boundary intersections. The boundary constructions agree across cells, hence this gives a finite simplicial complex with the same underlying subset. The same induction on any finite list of included cells respects their unions. Its dimension is at most $\max(\dim K+1,\dim L)$: on a source simplex $\sigma$ with image simplex $\tau$, the affine map onto $\tau$ is surjective. For a cell $A*C$ with $k$ vertices in $A$, the $k-1$ equal-maximum equations are independent on $\tau$, hence on $\sigma$. Thus $\dim C\le\dim\sigma-(k-1)$, and $\dim(A*C)=k+\dim C\le\dim\sigma+1$. Other cuts only reduce dimension, and the separate lower cells have dimension at most $\dim L$. Here $K_j$ and $L_j$ consist of cells and their faces, so the subcylinder cells with $C\subseteq |K_j|$, $A\subseteq|L_j|$, together with $|L_j|$, form a subcomplex. Also $f^{-1}(|L_j|)$ is a union of source faces because $f$ is simplicial, and therefore a union of cells. If one needs these subcomplexes to be full, one more barycentric subdivision does it: its vertices are cells, and a chain all of whose vertices lie in the selected subcomplex is a chain of its cells. [step 1.1, construct]

2.2 For $x\in C$ and $i\in I(C)$ put $P_i(x)_v=x_v/f_i(x)$ if $f(v)=i$, and put it zero otherwise. These coordinates sum to one and lie in the face of the same source simplex mapping to $i$. The denominator is at least $1/N$, where $N$ is the number of vertices of $L$, because it is a maximal coordinate. Define on $A*C$ by $H((1-t)y+t x)=[u,t]$, where $u=t x+(1-t)\sum_{i\in A}y_iP_i(x)$. For $t=0$ this denotes the point $y\in|L|$ of the quotient; for $t=1$ it is $[x,1]$. The vector $u$ stays in the source simplex, and $f(u)=t f(x)+(1-t)y$. Consequently the displayed formulas agree on intersecting cells and fix both boundaries. They are rational smooth formulas with denominators bounded away from zero on each cell before the zero-end quotient. At the zero end continuity in the quotient follows as follows: the quotient map from the compact disjoint union $|K|\times[0,1]\sqcup |L|$ is closed, because its equivalence relation is closed (the only nontrivial identifications are equality of $f(x)$ at height zero). Indeed the saturation of a closed set $E$ is the projection of the compact set consisting of equivalent pairs with first coordinate in $E$, hence is closed; this proves that the quotient map is closed. Two distinct equivalence classes are disjoint compact subsets of the metric disjoint union, so have disjoint open neighborhoods. Within each such neighborhood take the complement of the saturation of its complement; it is an open saturated neighborhood of the class. Their quotient images are disjoint open neighborhoods. Thus $M_f$ is Hausdorff. For a sequence approaching a lower point $y$, any subsequence of $u$ has a convergent subsequence $u'$ by compactness of $K$, and $f(u')=y$ because $f(u)=t f(x)+(1-t)y$; its quotient limit is precisely $y$. This proves continuity at that end and hence of $H$. [F1, step 1.1, algebra, construct]

3.1 To prove bijectivity fix $[u,t]$ with $0<t<1$ and put $z=f(u)$. There is a unique $m>0$ satisfying $\sum_i\min(z_i,m)=t$. Indeed the left side is continuous, zero at zero, strictly increasing until it reaches one at $\max_i z_i$, and $t<1$. Set $v_i=\min(z_i,m)/t$ and $y_i=(z_i-m)_+/(1-t)$. Both are barycentric coordinates, and $z=t v+(1-t)y$. On a source simplex containing $u$, set $x_w=(v_{f(w)}/z_{f(w)})u_w$ when $z_{f(w)}>0$, and zero otherwise. Then $x$ lies in that simplex, $f(x)=v$, and whenever $y_i>0$ one has $v_i=m/t=\max_jv_j$. Hence $(1-t)y+t x\in T_f$, and substitution in step 2.2 yields $u$ in every coordinate. Conversely, for an arbitrary join point giving $[u,t]$, write $a=\max_i f_i(x)$ and $m=t a$. If $y_i=0$ then $z_i=t f_i(x)\le m$; if $y_i>0$ then $f_i(x)=a$ and $z_i=m+(1-t)y_i$. Thus the same threshold equation determines $m$ and recovers $f(x)$ and $y$. Within each vertex group the normalized coordinates of $u$ equal those of $x$, because $u_w=(t+(1-t)y_i/f_i(x))x_w$. The inverse formula therefore recovers $x$ also. This proves uniqueness independently of the cell chosen; boundary points are uniquely their upper or lower coordinates. [step 1.1, step 2.2, algebra, construct]

4.1 The continuous bijection $H$ is a homeomorphism: its source is the finite union of compact polytopes and its target is Hausdorff by step 2.2. The cylinder retraction composed with $H$ is $R((1-t)y+t x)=(1-t)y+t f(x)$, an affine map on each join cell. It is therefore piecewise linear after step 2.1. For a pair $(K_j,L_j)$ the formula preserves its cylinder and the inverse in step 3.1 has source support contained in that of $u$, so the preimage is exactly the subcylinder already specified, including $L_j$. This proves compatibility and all asserted finiteness and smoothness clauses. [F1, step 2.1, step 2.2, step 3.1] ∎

## Source and scope note

The complete eight-page Goresky article was visually read; section 4 was reread from actual page images 197–199. The normalized fibre-coordinate formula is adapted from that proof. The hyperplane-arrangement domain and the threshold inverse above are an expanded local construction, not a citation in place of a proof. This lemma concerns a simplicial map that has already been constructed. It supplies no triangulation or simpliciality theorem for arbitrary algebraic maps.

## Quantitative collar observation

On every positive-height collar $t\ge t_0>0$, the construction supplies the local inverse bound needed for subsequent smooth fitting. The threshold is piecewise linear: on a region where $B=\{i:z_i\ge m\}$, it is $m=(t-\sum_{i\notin B}z_i)/|B|$, with $|B|\ge1$. Its finitely many formulas have bounded derivatives. Also $m\ge t/N$. The inverse source coordinate is $x_w=u_w/t$ on $z_{f(w)}\le m$, and $x_w=m u_w/(t z_{f(w)})$ on $z_{f(w)}\ge m$. Thus the inverse formulas are smooth on their finite closed regions with denominators bounded below by $t_0/N$. The forward formulas have denominator at least $1/N$. Locally the resulting finite piecewise smooth maps and inverse have finite Lipschitz bounds in the affine coordinates. At a point in a smooth stratum, this is a quantitative local inverse contract for the full tangent star of the supplied collar triangulation. A subsequent smooth radial diffeomorphism preserves that contract on compact stratum collars. This observation does not apply at a singular collapsed end.
