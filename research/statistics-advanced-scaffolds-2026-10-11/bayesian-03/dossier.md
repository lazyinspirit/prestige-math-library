## Mathematical claims and proof routes

### IID Monte Carlo and error

For IID real Y_i with mu = E Y_1, the sample mean Ybar_n is unbiased if E|Y_1| is finite. If E Y_1-squared is finite, Var(Ybar_n)=sigma-squared/n. If 0 < sigma-squared < infinity, sqrt(n)(Ybar_n-mu) converges in law to N(0,sigma-squared); if sigma-squared=0, the estimate is constant almost surely and the normalized CLT must not divide by sigma. A plug-in standard error s_n/sqrt(n) is justified by consistency of the sample second moment and Slutsky, with the same finite-second-moment premise.

The local proof route is: law-of-a-random-element and change-of-variables for expectation; existing thm-factorization-of-expectations-for-independent-variables for covariance factorization; existing cor-iid-finite-variance-weak-law for finite-sample variance and consistency; existing thm-lindeberg-levy-iid-central-limit-theorem for the full CLT proof. Variance estimation then applies the IID strong law to Y_i and Y_i-squared and Slutsky. Owen S01 is an independent exposition but does not prove the CLT.

### Control variates

For E h(X)=theta known and fixed beta, the estimator
mu-hat_beta = (1/n) sum_i {f(X_i)-beta(h(X_i)-theta)}
is unbiased for E f(X). Its variance is n^-1 Var(f-beta h). If Var(h)>0, the minimizing scalar is beta_star=Cov(f,h)/Var(h), and the variance is n^-1 Var(f)(1-rho-squared). For vector controls, require a nonsingular covariance matrix after removing redundant predictors. Estimating beta on the same sample generally introduces finite-sample bias; the source's O_p(n^-1) comparison assumes finite fourth moments and fixed dimension. An independent pilot-sample estimate of beta preserves unbiasedness.

Proof route: expand expectation and covariance using linearity; minimize the resulting quadratic. For vector controls solve the normal equations. For a same-sample coefficient, use beta-hat-beta_star=O_p(n^-1/2) and Hbar-theta=O_p(n^-1/2), so their product is O_p(n^-1), not identically zero. Avoid claiming unbiasedness for regression control variates unless the coefficient is independent of the estimation sample.

### Rejection and importance sampling

For normalized densities f,g, a finite envelope f(x)<=c g(x) and a computable ratio are required. Propose Y~g, accept with f(Y)/(c g(Y)), and repeat independently until accepted. The accepted subdensity is g(y)f(y)/(c g(y))=f(y)/c, so conditional on acceptance its density is f. Acceptance probability is 1/c, and proposal count is geometric with mean c. For unnormalized tilde-f, tilde-g, require finite positive integrals and tilde-f<=c tilde-g; the accepted law is proportional to tilde-f, with acceptance probability integral(tilde-f)/(c integral(tilde-g)). If f/g is unbounded, that proposal cannot furnish a finite rejection envelope.

For ordinary importance sampling with target density p, proposal density q, and q>0 wherever f p is nonzero, f(X)p(X)/q(X), X~q, has mean E_p f whenever it is integrable. Its variance exists only if integral((f p)^2/q) is finite, and equals n^-1[integral((f p)^2/q)-mu-squared] for the average. A normal CLT needs this finite second moment. Heavy/light tail mismatch can make variance infinite even if mu exists.

Self-normalized importance sampling is the ratio sum_i f(X_i)w_i / sum_i w_i when normalization constants are unavailable. Require q>0 on the whole target support p>0; this is stronger than ordinary IS's f p-support requirement. Under the strong law and a positive finite denominator mean, SNIS is consistent. It is generally biased for finite n. A CLT and delta-method MCSE require finite second moments of the numerator and denominator inputs (and a nonzero denominator mean); Owen's Theorem 9.2 alone is only an SLLN. For normalized p, the asymptotic variance is \(E_q[w^2(f-\mu)^2]\).

The common importance-weight ESS (sum w_i)^2 / sum(w_i-squared) is a weight-imbalance summary. It can be small when weights collapse, but a large observed ESS does not prove success: the sample may have missed a target region entirely, and a variance estimate computed from the same weights can also be misleading. An IS control variate with known integral h_j=theta_j is unbiased for fixed coefficients only if q>0 wherever either h_j is nonzero or f p is nonzero. Owen's estimated-coefficient asymptotics are cited to another article and must be proved locally if claimed.

### Metropolis-Hastings and Gibbs

For a proper target density tilde-pi (normalization may be unknown) and a proposal density q(y|x), the MH acceptance rule is
alpha(x,y)=min(1, tilde-pi(y)q(x|y)/(tilde-pi(x)q(y|x)))
when the denominator is positive; zero-support cases require an explicit convention consistent with the transition measure. The transition includes the rejected self-loop, and rejected values stay in ergodic averages. The accepted off-diagonal flow is tilde-pi(x)q(y|x)alpha(x,y), equal to the reverse flow, so detailed balance implies invariance. This proof is density-based; a general-state formulation should instead use a common dominating measure/Radon-Nikodym derivative or prove the symmetric measure identity directly. A citation to detailed balance does not prove irreducibility, aperiodicity, uniqueness, mixing, or a CLT.

Gibbs updates one block by an exact draw from its proper full conditional, leaving other coordinates fixed. Each block update preserves the joint target; random scan is a mixture of invariant reversible kernels. A systematic full sweep preserves the target as a composition, but typically is not reversible and its one-coordinate process is time-inhomogeneous. Support geometry can make single-site Gibbs reducible. A correct stationary marginal is not a convergence theorem.

Proof route: define the general probability kernel and invariant law using existing def-measure-kernel-and-probability-kernel and def-invariant-and-stationary-distribution-for-a-markov-kernel; prove general reversibility implies invariance by Tonelli/Fubini. For MH, verify the detailed-balance identity including the diagonal rejection mass. For Gibbs, integrate the conditional update against the marginal of the retained coordinates. State properness and support hypotheses before applying either construction. Existing local detailed balance items are countable-state only.

### Ergodicity, mixing, and Monte Carlo error for a Markov chain

The general-state convergence route in S06 assumes a countably generated state sigma-algebra, phi-irreducibility, aperiodicity, and an invariant law for total-variation convergence from pi-almost every starting point; Harris recurrence strengthens this to all starting points. Reducibility and periodicity are distinct failures. A small-set minorization P^n0(x,.)>=epsilon nu(.) on C, combined with geometric drift PV<=lambda V+b 1_C, yields geometric ergodicity under the source's irreducibility and aperiodicity conditions. Uniform ergodicity needs whole-space minorization. Quantitative mixing rates and finite-time useful convergence are stronger questions than asymptotic ergodicity.

For stationary h(X_n), the CLT variance is the long-run variance sigma_f-squared = Cov(f(X_0),f(X_0))+2 sum_{k>=1}Cov(f(X_0),f(X_k)), when the series and CLT conditions hold. S06/S07 give alternatives: uniform ergodicity with pi(h-squared)<infinity; geometric ergodicity with pi(|h|^(2+delta))<infinity; geometric ergodicity plus reversibility with only pi(h-squared)<infinity; or a Poisson equation h-pi(h)=g-Pg with g in L2(pi), reducing the sum to a martingale array plus a negligible boundary. Jones also gives geometric-ergodicity/log-moment and polynomial-ergodicity/moment alternatives. Finite variance alone is insufficient: a sticky chain can fail a root-n CLT.

Local proof route: prove a Markov ergodic theorem for the precise class used. One route is a regeneration/coupling proof under the small-set condition, then apply the existing IID strong law to excursion rewards. For a CLT, either prove the Poisson equation solution in L2(pi), use thm-martingale-central-limit-theorem, and bound the endpoint terms; or prove a mixing-rate bound and state a matching classical alpha-mixing CLT. A general-state path-shift/Birkhoff route additionally needs a proof that the stationary Markov shift is ergodic. Existing Birkhoff suppliers alone do not provide that Markov-specific step.

Batch means can estimate long-run variance under a Markov-chain CLT and a suitable batch-size regime, but the pedagogical batching paragraph in S05 is heuristic. If a batch-means consistency theorem is included, state and prove its exact assumptions; do not turn the heuristic “about 20 batches” into a theorem.

### HMC, MALA, and unadjusted Langevin bias

For a proper differentiable target on R^d with density proportional to exp(-U(q)), choose a symmetric positive-definite mass matrix M, kinetic energy K(p)=p^T M^-1 p/2, and refreshed momentum p~N(0,M). Hamilton's equations are q-dot=M^-1 p and p-dot=-gradient U(q). Where the Hamiltonian vector field has a global differentiable flow, that flow is energy-preserving, volume-preserving, and reversible after momentum negation; an exact-flow proposal accepts with probability one. Leapfrog uses half momentum/full position/half momentum steps. Each component is a shear with determinant one; the full map is volume-preserving and reversible after momentum negation. Under the required smoothness, its global trajectory error is second order in the step size, and it does not exactly conserve H at a nonzero step size.

With L leapfrog steps of size epsilon, propose the endpoint with momentum negated and apply alpha=min(1,exp(-H(q*,p*)+H(q,p))). The volume-preserving reversible map makes this the usual MH ratio for the joint canonical density. This correction preserves the target exactly despite integration error; omitting it leaves a discretization error and generally targets a different law. This is the key distinction between corrected HMC/MALA and unadjusted methods.

For ULA with potential U, X_(k+1)=X_k-gamma gradient U(X_k)+sqrt(2 gamma)Z_(k+1). For U(x)=x-squared/2, the stationary recursion is X_(k+1)=(1-gamma)X_k+sqrt(2 gamma)Z_(k+1), so for 0<gamma<2 the invariant variance is 2/(2-gamma), not the target variance 1. This exact Gaussian calculation proves nonzero constant-step bias without an external numerical theorem. In d dimensions with U(x)=||x||-squared/2, the same discrepancy holds coordinatewise. MALA uses this Euler Gaussian as an asymmetric MH proposal, with the reverse/forward proposal-density ratio restoring exact target invariance.

S09 supplies stronger quantitative results, but only with assumptions: Theorem 10 assumes gradient-Lipschitz L1 and superlinear radial drift H1, with a step-size bound, and establishes an invariant pi_gamma plus a V^(1/2)-TV bias O(sqrt(gamma)); Theorem 21 treats a gradient-Lipschitz potential strongly convex outside a ball and gives finite-time bounds. The ULA paper's Theorem 10 proof uses Girsanov/KL/Pinsker and diffusion contraction; it does not give a short elementary derivation. Do not claim the quantitative rate for arbitrary smooth targets.

Ergodicity still requires separate support and mixing conditions. Neal's one-dimensional Gaussian example shows exact/near periodicity when trajectory length resonates with the dynamics; randomizing trajectory length or step size may mitigate this but is a tuning strategy, not a general proof. Step sizes above stability limits cause exploding energy and near-zero acceptance, and preliminary tuning in one region can fail elsewhere. The product-target high-dimensional scaling discussion is not a universal rate theorem.

### Adaptive MCMC and diagnostic limits

Assume each fixed kernel P_gamma is pi-ergodic and preserves the same target pi. Diminishing adaptation requires D_n=sup_x ||P_(Gamma_(n+1))(x,.)-P_(Gamma_n)(x,.)||_TV -> 0 in probability; it does not require Gamma_n to converge or sum D_n to be finite. For a family that is simultaneously uniformly ergodic, this plus diminishing adaptation gives convergence in distribution (S12 Theorem 5). Without uniformity, S12 Theorem 13 requires convergence-time random variables M_epsilon(X_n,Gamma_n) to be bounded in probability uniformly over n; this is the containment condition. A common sufficient condition is simultaneous geometric drift and minorization for all P_gamma, plus diminishing adaptation and an initial V-moment (S12 Theorem 18).

Under Theorem 5's stronger uniform conditions, the same source proves a weak law for bounded functions. It also gives a counterexample where a strong law fails despite diminishing adaptation and uniform ergodicity. It does not give a general adaptive CLT. A naturally tuned adaptive chain without diminishing adaptation may fail even to converge; independent or finite adaptation have separate, narrower preservation results and can still lose irreducibility in systematic scans.

Diagnostics are not proofs. Traditional split-Rhat can be near 1 when chains differ in scale or have infinite variance with a location shift. Rank normalization handles heavy tails/location; folding also checks scale/tail behavior; local/tail ESS and quantile MCSE are needed for interval endpoints. Their thresholds are empirical recommendations. A small Rhat does not imply a large enough ESS, and ESS is functional-specific. The source's centered eight-schools example shows all chains can miss the same funnel region and appear to mix; thinning removes sticking information and made diagnostics look good despite a biased posterior mean. Diverse overdispersed starts, trace/rank plots, divergences (for HMC), bulk/tail ESS, and problem-specific MCSE are useful checks, but no finite diagnostic certifies convergence.

## Minimal missing suppliers and open proof obligations

The following are the smallest missing interfaces needed to keep the proposed proofs local and Category 3 self-contained:

1. General-state reversibility. A definition of reversible probability kernel via symmetry of pi(dx)P(x,dy), followed by a locally proved detailed-balance-implies-invariance theorem. The active local version is countable-only.
2. General-state ergodicity. A carefully scoped phi-irreducible/aperiodic/Harris theorem for total-variation convergence, with separate almost-everywhere and all-starting-point claims. If geometric rates are included, give the small-set minorization and drift hypotheses and prove the conversion. S06's proof of Theorem 4 relies on Jain-Jameson; its proof of Theorem 9 relies on a cited petite-to-small theorem. Add those lemmas or use an alternative self-contained route.
3. Markov-chain LLN/CLT layer. Existing countable-state ergodic and IID CLTs are insufficient for continuous MCMC. Choose either a general Harris LLN plus excursion-reward proof, or a stationary-shift ergodicity proof before invoking Birkhoff. For the CLT, prove the Poisson-equation/martingale decomposition or include the exact mixing-CLT hypotheses and proof. S06's Theorem 25 imports an operator-norm result; S07 imports classical alpha-mixing CLTs. A full library proof needs to expose these dependencies.
4. HMC leapfrog. The HMC pair can own this directly: show each half-momentum/full-position map is an invertible shear with determinant one, compose them, and show momentum negation makes the map reversible. Then prove MH invariance from the change-of-variables formula. If exact Hamiltonian-flow claims are made, state the ODE regularity/global-existence assumptions and keep the numerical map separate.
5. ULA bias. The standard-Gaussian example has an elementary local proof and should establish that a fixed Euler step is generally biased. General quantitative bounds need explicit smoothness/tail/curvature assumptions; S09 Theorem 10 is not universal and its proof uses external diffusion-contractivity results.
6. Adaptive CLT. No general adaptive CLT should be promised from S12. If a later result is desired, add a separate source and local theorem with precise stochastic-approximation/drift/moment conditions; this is outside the minimum for the assigned adaptive-ergodicity scope.
7. Estimated control coefficients and SNIS CLTs. Owen gives either an external citation (estimated CV coefficient) or consistency only (SNIS). Use a local regression/delta-method proof under stated finite-moment and nondegeneracy assumptions, or omit those asymptotic refinements.
8. Diagnostics. There is no theorem that a finite trace, Rhat, ESS, or any diagnostic certifies stationarity. Keep numerical thresholds explicitly heuristic and distinguish a theorem about asymptotic variance from its finite-chain estimate.

The ULA and HMC content can use existing calculus, Gaussian, Brownian, and matrix-factorization suppliers listed above; no generic Category 4 optimization, backpropagation, architecture, or learned-gradient theorem is required. Any assumptions about a computable gradient are part of the algorithm input contract, not a Category 4 result.
