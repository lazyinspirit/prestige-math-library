## Source-backed claims and exact limits

### Differentiation and training algorithms

Automatic differentiation evaluates local derivative rules along the executed finite computation trace. Forward mode propagates tangent vectors and computes Jv products in one pass; reverse mode starts from output cotangents, accumulates contributions at shared nodes, and computes J-transpose-v products. For a scalar objective of many parameters, reverse mode computes the full gradient with cost proportional to the primal trace up to a constant factor, at the cost of retaining or recomputing intermediates. This is the mathematical basis of backpropagation.

The correct local theorem contract is a finite directed acyclic execution graph whose primitive operations are differentiable at the evaluated values and whose registered local rules equal their derivatives. A reverse-topological induction and the chain rule then show that each accumulated adjoint equals the derivative of the graph output with respect to that node; fan-out contributions must be summed. A dynamic branch differentiates the path actually executed and is locally valid only where the path is locally fixed. At a ReLU kink or another nondifferentiability, the selected software convention is not automatically a classical derivative. AD avoids finite-difference truncation error, but it does not remove floating-point roundoff or ill-conditioning.

For a nonconvex L-smooth objective bounded below along the iterates, deterministic GD with a sufficiently small fixed step yields a finite sum of squared gradient norms and hence gradients tending to zero. This is a stationarity result, not convergence to a global minimum. Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal give a wider stochastic-direction theorem: F is continuously differentiable with L-Lipschitz gradient; along the iterates F is bounded below; the expected direction has a positive alignment with ∇F and a comparable norm; and its conditional variance is at most M+M_V||∇F||². With α≤μ/(L M_G), their fixed-step result bounds the expected average squared gradient by a noise floor plus O(1/(Tα)). For unbiased SGD, μ=μ_G=1. With positive steps satisfying sum α_t=∞ and sum α_t²<∞, their weighted expected average squared gradient tends to zero. Ghadimi–Lan independently prove a random-iterate stationarity result for L-smooth F, an unbiased stochastic first-order oracle with bounded variance, and their specified random output distribution; their constant-step choice gives the familiar O(1/T + 1/sqrt(T)) scale. None of these is a global-minimum theorem.

Heavy-ball momentum is the update w_(t+1)=w_t−α∇F(w_t)+β(w_t−w_(t−1)). On a strictly convex quadratic with eigenvalues in [c,L], optimally selected parameters give the heavy-ball contraction (sqrt(κ)−1)/(sqrt(κ)+1), κ=L/c, versus (κ−1)/(κ+1) for fixed-step steepest descent. This depends on the quadratic spectrum and parameter choice. Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal explicitly describe stochastic-momentum guarantees for general learning objectives as elusive; momentum intuition and successful experiments are not a general convergence theorem.

Adaptive rules need separate algorithm and guarantee items. AdaGrad accumulates coordinatewise squared gradients, RMSProp uses a moving average, and Adam combines an exponential first-moment average with an exponential second-moment average. Reddi–Kale–Kumar give an explicit one-dimensional online convex sequence on [-1,1] for which Adam has nonzero average regret, and extend the failure to fixed β1<sqrt(β2); adding fixed ε does not remove the issue. Their AMSGrad update replaces the denominator estimate by its coordinatewise historical maximum. Its regret theorem assumes bounded feasible diameter, bounded coordinate gradients, α_t=α/sqrt(t), and β1/sqrt(β2)<1. A sublinear O(sqrt(T)) conclusion additionally follows for schedules such as β1t=β1 λ^(t−1) (or β1/t); a constant β1 used in practice is not the schedule required by their stated regret corollary. These results are online convex-optimization results, not convergence guarantees for nonconvex neural-network loss.

### Initialization, interpolation and implicit bias

He–Zhang–Ren–Sun derive the ReLU initialization recurrence under layerwise independent, identically distributed zero-mean weights, independent equal-distribution inputs, symmetric preactivations and zero biases. For a ReLU layer with fan-in n_l, Var(y_l)=0.5 n_l Var(w_l) Var(y_(l−1)); choosing Var(w_l)=2/n_l prevents this simplified forward second moment from multiplying by a factor different from one at each layer. The backward recurrence instead uses fan-out; the paper says either choice prevents exponential scaling in its model, not that both constraints can always be met for arbitrary architectures. For PReLU slope a the corresponding factor is 2/((1+a²)n_l). This is a variance derivation under independence assumptions, not a theorem that a network trains or generalizes. ImageNet depth and accuracy comparisons in the paper are experiments.

Two implicit-bias regimes are distinct. (i) For linear least squares, GD initialized at zero and with 0<η≤1/λ_max(XᵀX) converges to the minimum-Euclidean-norm least-squares solution; this is a direct row-space argument and holds even when the solution is nonunique. (ii) For separable linear classification with logistic or exponential loss, Soudry et al. prove that full-batch GD with a sufficiently small fixed step has loss tending to zero, ||w_t||→∞, and w_t/||w_t|| tending to the L2 hard-margin SVM direction. Their all-dataset theorem assumes a smooth decreasing loss with the stated tight exponential tail; exponential loss needs the local-smoothness step-size caveat, while logistic loss is globally smooth. The sharper bounded-residual expansion holds for almost every dataset; degenerate data can require iterated-log corrections. This is an optimizer- and loss-specific statement, not a generic implicit norm penalty and not a test-risk result.

For L-homogeneous classifiers with exponential-type loss, Lyu–Li show a smoothed normalized margin increases after training loss has fallen below the stated threshold; every limit point of the parameter direction is along a KKT point of min 0.5||θ||² subject to all training margins ≥1. KKT is necessary, not sufficient, for global maximum margin when the network constraints are nonconvex. Their full-batch GD theorem requires a smooth parameterization away from zero and a sufficiently small step; the gradient-flow theorem permits locally Lipschitz, chain-rule models such as ReLU under its assumptions. If the data are not separable or the loss does not reach that threshold, the result does not apply.

Du et al. prove a finite-width training-to-interpolation result for particular randomly initialized networks and squared empirical loss. For fully connected networks, inputs satisfy ||x_i||=1, labels are bounded, the activation is Lipschitz, smooth and analytic non-polynomial, the initialization is Gaussian, and the recursively defined training Gram matrix has λ_min>0; their width and step-size requirements are exponential in depth. Their every-layer residual architecture improves this to polynomial width/depth conditions, with η=O(λ_min H²/n²), and loss contracts as (1−ηλ_min/2)^k with probability 1−δ over initialization. Their residual block has a skip at each layer and explicit c_res/(H sqrt(m)) scaling; the paper says it is not the standard every-two-layer ResNet block. It proves zero training loss, not good test performance. ReLU is outside the smooth-activation theorem.

The rigorous double-descent example is ridgeless minimum-norm least squares, not arbitrary neural training. Under iid training pairs with y=xᵀβ+ε, independent centered noise of variance σ², iid isotropic feature entries with mean zero, unit variance and finite 4+η moment, ||β||²=r², and p/n→γ, the almost-sure design-conditional risk is σ²γ/(1−γ) for γ<1 and r²(1−1/γ)+σ²/(γ−1) for γ>1. It diverges at γ=1, then can descend on the overparameterized side as variance decreases; its limit as γ→∞ is the null risk r². The risk is not monotone decreasing immediately for all SNR, and in the well-specified isotropic case its global minimum remains underparameterized. Anisotropy, misspecification, latent directions and ridge regularization change the curve.

Belkin–Hsu–Ma–Mandal document double descent empirically for random Fourier features, two-layer networks, trees and ensembles. Their suggested small-norm mechanism is not a universal theorem for neural networks. Their rigorous noiseless approximation theorem is narrower: uniform random points on a compact cube, target in the Gaussian RKHS, and any interpolating h in that same RKHS; the sup error is bounded by a fill-distance rate times ||h*||+||h||. It does not establish noisy-data deep-network generalization.

### NTK and mean-field/feature learning

Jacot–Gabriel–Hongler define the NTK as the sum over parameters of parameter-Jacobian outer products. For fully connected networks with the paper’s 1/sqrt(width) normalization, iid Gaussian initialization and sequential hidden-width limits, the initialization NTK converges in probability to a deterministic recursive kernel. Under a Lipschitz C² activation with bounded second derivative and a training direction whose integrated function-space norm stays bounded on each fixed time interval, the NTK stays uniformly close to that limit on that interval, and function evolution becomes kernel gradient flow. On a finite dataset, positive definiteness of the Gram matrix gives convergence for convex costs; for least squares the dynamics are a linear kernel ODE with modewise factors exp(−t λ_i). The paper proves positivity for non-polynomial Lipschitz activations on the unit sphere at depth at least two. This infinite-width, finite-time result is the lazy/kernel regime; it is not a finite-width theorem or a claim that trained finite networks never learn features.

Chizat–Bach instead treat an overparameterized one-hidden-layer model as a measure over particles. Under their Hilbert-space, smoothness, semiconvexity and growth assumptions, empirical particle gradient flows converge in the many-particle limit to a unique Wasserstein gradient flow. Their global-optimum results add convex loss, positive homogeneity, a Sard-type regularity assumption and a separating support initialization; they also assume convergence of the flow (or of a specified projected measure). This allows nontrivial particle-position/feature evolution in the mean-field limit. It is continuous-time and qualitative, has no finite-particle rate, and is not a global-convergence guarantee for arbitrary finite-width SGD. For ReLU, the classical parameterization is not differentiable enough for their gradient-flow result; their application uses a signed-square reparameterization.

### Generalization statements and caveats

Bartlett–Foster–Telgarsky prove a simultaneous multiclass margin bound for iid examples, fixed Lipschitz nonlinearities with σ_i(0)=0, and networks with spectral complexity

RA = (product_i ρ_i||A_i||_2) (sum_i (||A_iᵀ−M_iᵀ||_(2,1) / ||A_i||_2)^(2/3))^(3/2).

Their error upper bound is empirical margin loss plus Õ(||X||_2 RA log(W)/(γ n) + sqrt(log(1/δ)/n)), where W is maximum input/hidden/output width and X contains the sample inputs. It is a norm/margin-dependent uniform bound over the stated network class; it does not imply good margins or small norm after a particular optimizer.

Arora–Ge–Neyshabur–Zhang prove a general compression bound: if a classifier is margin-preservingly compressible on the sample to q discrete parameters, each with r possible values, then the compressed classifier has population zero-one error at most the original empirical margin error plus O(sqrt(q log(r)/m)) with high probability. The theorem bounds the compressed classifier. It bounds the original trained classifier only if an additional all-domain loss-preservation argument is supplied. Their fully connected/convolutional compression constructions require layer stability/cushion assumptions and are separate from this general theorem.

Hardt–Recht–Singer prove uniform stability implies an expected generalization gap at most ε. For convex, β-smooth, L-Lipschitz per-example losses and SGM steps α_t≤2/β, ε≤(2L²/n)sum_t α_t. For nonconvex training, their bound assumes each loss lies in [0,1], is L-Lipschitz and β-smooth, and uses nonincreasing α_t≤c/t; then ε is at most

(1+1/(βc))/(n−1) · (2cL²)^(1/(βc+1)) · T^(βc/(βc+1)).

This is an expected generalization bound for the randomized sample-selection update; it depends on the number of steps and loss regularity and does not say all SGD training generalizes.
