## Example

The polynomial $x^4+2x^2-8$ has roots $\pm\sqrt2$ and $\pm2i$, so its splitting field over $\mathbb Q$ is $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i)$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The polynomial $x^4+2x^2-8\in\mathbb Q[x]$.

[F1] The real number $2$ has a square root $\sqrt2$ with square $2$ ([[thm-of-square-roots]]).

[F2] The complex numbers form a field containing $i$ with $i^2=-1$ ([[thm-complex-numbers-form-a-field]]).

[F3] A splitting field is the field generated over the base by all roots of the polynomial ([[def-polynomials-that-split-and-splitting-fields]]).

## Verification

**Proof technique:** direct factorisation.

1.1 Over $\mathbb Q$, direct multiplication gives $x^4+2x^2-8=(x^2-2)(x^2+4)$. By [F1] and [F2], the first factor has roots $\pm\sqrt2$ and the second has roots $\pm2i$. [F1, F2, algebra]

2.1 All four roots lie in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i)$. Conversely, the field generated by them contains $\sqrt2$ and $i=(2i)/2$, so it is exactly $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i)$. The claim follows from [F3]. [F3, step 1.1] ∎
