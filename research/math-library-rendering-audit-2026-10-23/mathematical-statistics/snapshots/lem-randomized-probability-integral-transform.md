## Statement

Let $Y$ be a real random variable with cumulative distribution function $F(y)=P(Y\le y)$, and let $U$ be independent of $Y$ with the uniform Borel probability law on $[0,1]$. Write $F(y-):=P(Y<y)$ and $\Delta F(y):=F(y)-F(y-)=P(Y=y)$. Then
$$V:=F(Y-)+U\Delta F(Y)$$
has the uniform distribution on $[0,1]$, for arbitrary continuous, discrete, mixed, or singular laws of $Y$.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A real random variable $Y$ with law $\mu$, its distribution function $F$, and an independent $U$ whose law is uniform on $[0,1]$.

[F1] The distribution function is $F(x)=\mu((-∞,x])$. [[def-cumulative-distribution-function-of-a-random-variable]]

[F2] Inclusion of measurable sets implies monotonicity of their measures. [[prop-measure-monotonicity]]

[F3] Measures are continuous along increasing sequences of measurable sets. [[thm-continuity-from-below-for-measures]]

[F4] A measure finite on the first set of a decreasing sequence is continuous along that sequence. [[thm-continuity-from-above-for-measures]]

[F5] For nested measurable sets with finite smaller-set measure, the measure of their difference is the difference of their measures. [[prop-measure-of-a-set-difference]]

[F6] Every nonempty subset of $\mathbb R$ that is bounded above has a supremum. [[def-complete-ordered-field]]

[F7] Product sigma-algebras are generated by measurable rectangles. [[def-product-sigma-algebra-and-finite-product-sigma-algebras]]

[F8] Continuous maps have Borel preimages, and measurable maps compose. [[thm-continuous-preimages-of-borel-sets-are-borel]], [[thm-composition-with-borel-functions-preserves-measurability]]

[F9] Sums and scalar multiples of measurable real functions are measurable, as are pointwise increasing limits. [[prop-closure-properties-of-measurable-functions-used-by-the-integral]]

[F10] A real or extended-real function is measurable if all of its strict superlevel sets are measurable. [[def-extended-real-valued-measurable-function]]

[F11] The Borel sigma-algebra is generated by open sets. [[def-borel-sigma-algebra]]

[F12] The rationals are countable and dense in $\mathbb R$. [[thm-rationals-countable]], [[lem-q-and-irrationals-dense-r]]

[A1] $U$ is independent of $Y$ and has $P(U\le u)=u$ for $0\le u\le1$; this is the uniform randomization stipulated in the Statement. [[def-independent-random-elements]]

## Proof

All sequences below are indexed by integers $n\ge1$.

1.1 By [F1] and [F2], $F$ is nondecreasing. For each real $c$, $\{x:F(x)>c\}$ is an upper set, hence is empty, all of $\mathbb R$, or a ray with or without its finite endpoint; each such ray is Borel by [F11]. Thus every strict superlevel set is Borel, and [F10] makes $F$ Borel-measurable. For each $x$, the sets $(-\infty,x+1/n]$ decrease to $(-\infty,x]$ and have finite measure, so [F4] gives right continuity. The sets $(-\infty,-n]$ decrease to $\varnothing$ and $(-\infty,n]$ increase to $\mathbb R$, so [F3] and [F4] give the limits $0$ and $1$ at the two infinities. Also $(-\infty,x-1/n]$ increases to $(-\infty,x)$, so [F3] gives $F(x-)=\mu((-∞,x))$; [F5] then gives $F(x)-F(x-)=\mu(\{x\})$. [F1, F2, F3, F4, F5, F6, F10, F11]

2.1 For every $x$, step 1.1 gives $F(x-)=\lim_nF(x-1/n)$. Each translate $x\mapsto F(x-1/n)$ is measurable because translation is continuous and [F8] preserves measurability under composition; [F9] makes their increasing limit $F(x-)$ measurable. Thus $\Delta F=F-F(\cdot-)$ is measurable by [F9]. Since $Y$ is measurable, $F(Y-)$ and $\Delta F(Y)$ are measurable. Fix a bijection $e:\mathbb Q\to\mathbb N$ supplied by [F12]. Each rational open rectangle is encoded by four natural numbers, and iterating the injection $(a,b)\mapsto 2^a(2b+1)$ shows there are only countably many such rectangles. Density of $\mathbb Q$ makes them a base of $\mathbb R^2$. Every Euclidean open set is therefore a countable union of rectangles in $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\otimes\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$. Conversely the coordinate projections are continuous, and [F8] makes the inverse image of each Borel set under either projection Borel; finite intersections then show every Borel rectangle is Euclidean Borel. Thus the product Borel sigma-algebra equals $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^2)$. The pair $(U,\Delta F(Y))$ is measurable into the product sigma-algebra because preimages of generating rectangles are intersections of coordinate preimages, hence it is measurable into $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^2)$. The map $(u,d)\mapsto ud$ is continuous, as $|ud-u_0d_0|\le |u|\cdot|d-d_0|+|d_0|\cdot|u-u_0|$ and $|u|$ is bounded near $u_0$; hence [F8] makes it Borel-measurable. Composing and then summing with $F(Y-)$ proves $V$ is measurable. [A1, F7, F8, F9, F12, step 1.1]

2.2 Fix $0<u<1$ and set $A_u:=\{x:F(x)\ge u\}$. The upper-tail limit in step 1.1 makes $A_u$ nonempty, and the lower-tail limit makes it bounded below. Applying [F6] to $-A_u$ gives $q:=-\sup(-A_u)=\inf A_u$. For every $n$, the infimum property gives $F(q+1/n)\ge u$, so right continuity from step 1.1 yields $F(q)\ge u$. Since $q-1/n<q$ and $q$ is a lower bound for $A_u$, $F(q-1/n)<u$; the sets $(-\infty,q-1/n]$ increase to $(-\infty,q)$, so [F3] and step 1.1 give $F(q-)=\lim_nF(q-1/n)\le u$. Put $a:=F(q-)$ and $b:=F(q)$. If $Y<q$, then $F(Y)\le a\le u$, so $V\le u$; this event has probability $a$. If $b>a$, then $P(Y=q)=b-a$ and, conditional on $Y=q$, $V=a+U(b-a)$ is uniform on $[a,b]$, contributing $(u-a)$ to $P(V\le u)$. If $b=a$, the inequalities force $a=b=u$ and $P(Y=q)=0$, so this contribution is zero. [A1, F1, F3, F4, F6, step 1.1, given, algebra]

3.1 For $Y>q$, monotonicity gives $F(Y-)\ge F(q)=b\ge u$. If $F(Y-)>u$, then $V>u$. If $F(Y-)=u$ and $\Delta F(Y)>0$, the event $V\le u$ requires $U=0$, which has probability zero by [A1]. In the remaining case $F(Y-)=F(Y)=u$, put $L:=\{y>q:F(y-)=F(y)=u\}$. By steps 1.1 and 2.1, both $F$ and $F(\cdot-)$ are Borel-measurable, so $L=\{y>q\}\cap\{F(y-)=u\}\cap\{F(y)=u\}$ is Borel and therefore $\mu$-measurable. If $L$ is empty it contributes no mass. Otherwise let $r=\sup L$, allowing $r=+\infty$ when $L$ is unbounded. For every $z$ with $q<z<r$, some $y\in L$ has $z<y$, so monotonicity gives $u=F(q)\le F(z)\le F(y-)=u$ and hence $F(z)=u$. If $r<\infty$, define $c_n=q+(r-q)n/(n+1)$; if $r=+\infty$, define $c_n=q+n$. In either case $c_n\uparrow r$ (with the limit interpreted as $+\infty$ in the second case), and [F5] gives $\mu((q,c_n])=F(c_n)-F(q)=0$. By [F3], $\mu((q,r))=0$. If finite $r$ belongs to $L$, its mass is $\Delta F(r)=0$, so $\mu(L)=0$. Thus $Y>q$ contributes no probability, and step 2.2 yields $P(V\le u)=a+(u-a)=u$. [A1, F2, F3, F5, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 2.2, given, construct]

4.1 For $u_n=1/n$ with $n\ge2$, the events $\{V\le u_n\}$ decrease to $\{V\le0\}$; [F4] and the formula from step 3.1 give $P(V\le0)=0$. For $v_n=1-1/n$ with $n\ge2$, the events $\{V\le v_n\}$ increase to $\{V<1\}$; [F3] and step 3.1 give $P(V<1)=1$. Finally $0\le V\le1$ by its definition, so $P(V\le1)=1$. Thus $P(V\le u)=u$ for all $u\in[0,1]$, which is the uniform law. [F3, F4, step 3.1] ∎
