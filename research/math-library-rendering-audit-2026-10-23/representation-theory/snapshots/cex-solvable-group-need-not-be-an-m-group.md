## Statement refuted

"Every finite solvable group is an $M$-group
([[def-monomial-representation-and-m-group]])."

The **binary tetrahedral group**
$$ T:=\langle Q_8\cup\{u\}\rangle=\langle Q_8,u\rangle\le\mathbb H^\times, \qquad u:=\tfrac12(-1+i+j+k), $$
generated inside the nonzero quaternions by the quaternion group
$Q_8$ ([[def-quaternion-group-of-order-eight]]) and the element $u$
([[def-quaternions]]), is of order $24$ and is the internal semidirect product
$T=Q_8\rtimes C_3$ ([[def-internal-semidirect-product]]). The group $T$ is
solvable, and left multiplication on $\mathbb H$, for the complex structure of
step 1.3 below, is a faithful irreducible $2$-dimensional complex
representation $V$ of $T$. Its character $\chi_V$ is an irreducible complex
character, and $\chi_V$ is **not** monomial: $T$ has no subgroup of index two,
whereas a monomial character of degree two is induced from a linear character
of a subgroup of index two. Hence $T$ is a finite solvable group that is not an
$M$-group.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The quaternions $\mathbb H=\mathbb R^4$ with basis $1,i,j,k$ and conjugate $x\mapsto\bar x$ and norm $N(x)$ ([[def-quaternions]]), the quaternion group $Q_8=\{\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}\subseteq\mathbb H^\times$ ([[def-quaternion-group-of-order-eight]]), the element $u=\tfrac12(-1+i+j+k)\in\mathbb H$, and the subgroup $T=\langle Q_8\cup\{u\}\rangle\le\mathbb H^\times$ generated by $Q_8$ and $u$ ([[def-generated-subgroup]]).

[F1] In $\mathbb H$ one has $i^2=j^2=k^2=-1$, $ij=k$, $jk=i$, $ki=j$, $ji=-k$, $kj=-i$, $ik=-j$, the real multiples of $1$ are central, and $\mathbb H$ is a ring in which $x\bar x=\bar x x=N(x)$; if $x\ne0$ then $N(x)>0$ and $x^{-1}=N(x)^{-1}\bar x$, so $\mathbb H\setminus\{0\}$ is a group. ([[def-quaternions]], [[thm-quaternions-form-a-division-ring]]).

[F2] $Q_8$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb H^\times$ with $|Q_8|=8$ whose elements are exactly $\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k$, each written as a quadruple with coordinates in $\{0,1,-1\}$; $-1$ is its only element of order $2$ and each of $\pm i,\pm j,\pm k$ has order $4$. ([[def-quaternion-group-of-order-eight]], [[prop-the-quaternion-group-has-order-eight-and-a-unique-element-of-order-two]]).

[F3] If $N\trianglelefteq G$, $G=NH$ and $N\cap H=\{1\}$ for subgroups $N,H\le G$, then $G$ is the internal semidirect product of $N$ by $H$. ([[def-internal-semidirect-product]], [[def-normal-subgroup]], [[def-subgroup]]).

[F4] A nonempty subset $H\subseteq G$ of a group is a subgroup exactly when $gh^{-1}\in H$ for all $g,h\in H$; and $\langle S\rangle$ is the smallest subgroup of $G$ containing $S$, so $\langle S\rangle\subseteq H$ for every subgroup $H$ with $S\subseteq H$. ([[lem-subgroup-criterion]], [[def-generated-subgroup]]).

[F5] For $g,h\in G$ the commutator is $[g,h]=ghg^{-1}h^{-1}$ and $[G,G]$ is the subgroup generated by all commutators. ([[def-commutator-and-commutator-subgroup]]).

[F6] If $N\trianglelefteq G$ then $G/N$ is abelian if and only if $[G,G]\subseteq N$; and $G'=[G,G]$ is characteristic and normal, $G/G'$ is abelian, and every homomorphism $f:G\to A$ into an abelian group satisfies $f=\bar f\circ q$ for a unique homomorphism $\bar f:G/G'\to A$, where $q:G\to G/G'$ is the quotient map. ([[thm-quotient-abelian-iff-contains-commutator-subgroup]], [[thm-derived-subgroup-is-characteristic-and-abelianization-is-universal]]).

[F7] $G^{(0)}=G$ and $G^{(r+1)}=[G^{(r)},G^{(r)}]$, and $G$ is solvable when $G^{(n)}=1$ for some $n$. ([[def-derived-series-solvable-group-and-derived-length]]).

[F8] If $H\le G$ has index $2$ then $H\trianglelefteq G$; a group of order $p^2$ for a prime $p$ is abelian; and for finite $G$ and $H\le G$ one has $|G|=[G:H]\,|H|$. ([[thm-index-two-subgroup-is-normal]], [[cor-groups-of-order-p-squared-are-abelian]], [[thm-lagrange]]).

[F9] A complex representation of a group $G$ is a group homomorphism $G\to\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbb C}(V)$ on a finite-dimensional $\mathbb C$-vector space $V$; its character is $g\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(\rho(g))$, its degree is $\dim_{\mathbb C}V=\chi(1)$, and a nonzero representation is irreducible exactly when $0$ and $V$ are its only invariant subspaces. ([[def-finite-dimensional-representation-of-a-group-over-a-field]], [[def-character-of-a-complex-representation]], [[def-subrepresentation-and-irreducible-representation]]).

[F10] A character $\chi$ of $G$ is monomial if $\chi=\operatorname{Ind}_H^G\lambda$ for some $H\le G$ and linear character $\lambda$; $G$ is an $M$-group if every irreducible complex character of $G$ is monomial; a nonzero representation is monomial exactly when its character is; the character of an irreducible representation is irreducible; and $\dim_k\operatorname{Ind}_H^GW=[G:H]\dim_kW$. ([[def-monomial-representation-and-m-group]], [[def-irreducible-complex-character]], [[def-induced-r-linear-g-module-by-h-covariant-functions]], [[cor-dimension-of-an-induced-finite-dimensional-representation]]).

[A1] If $a,b\in\mathbb H$ have scalar parts $s_1,s_2$ and imaginary parts $v_1,v_2$ in the basis $i,j,k$, then $ab$ has scalar part $s_1s_2-v_1\cdot v_2$ and imaginary part $s_1v_2+s_2v_1+v_1\times v_2$, by the product formula of [[def-quaternions]].

## Counterexample

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 The element $u$ satisfies $u^2=\bar u$ and $u^3=1$. Indeed $u=\tfrac12(-1+(i+j+k))$ with $(i+j+k)^2=i^2+j^2+k^2+(ij+ji)+(ik+ki)+(jk+kj)=-3$ by [F1], so $u^2=\tfrac14\bigl(1-2(i+j+k)+(i+j+k)^2\bigr)=\tfrac12(-1-i-j-k)=\bar u$; by [A1] the product $u\bar u$ has scalar part $\tfrac14-\tfrac14\bigl((1)(-1)+(1)(-1)+(1)(-1)\bigr)=\tfrac14+\tfrac34=1$ and imaginary part $\tfrac14(-1,-1,-1)-\tfrac14(1,1,1)+0=0$, the cross product vanishing because the imaginary parts of $u$ and $\bar u$ are antiparallel, so $N(u)=u\bar u=1$ and $u^{-1}=\bar u=u^2$ by [F1], whence $u^3=uu^2=1$. Moreover $u\notin Q_8$ and $u^2\notin Q_8$: the coordinates of $u$ and of $u^2$ are half-integers, whereas all coordinates of elements of $Q_8$ lie in $\{0,1,-1\}$ by [F2]. Hence $1,u,u^2$ are three distinct elements with $u\cdot u^2=u^3=1$ and $u^a u^b=u^{a+b}$ (exponent reduced modulo $3$), so $\langle u\rangle=\{1,u,u^2\}$ is a subgroup of order $3$ and $Q_8\cap\langle u\rangle=\{1\}$. [F1, F2, A1, construct]

1.2 The derived subgroup of $Q_8$ is $Q_8'=\{\pm1\}$. First $-1\in Q_8'$, since $[i,j]=iji^{-1}j^{-1}=(ij)(ji)^{-1}=k\cdot(-k)^{-1}=k\cdot k=k^2=-1$ by [F1] and [F5]. Second, $Q_8/\{\pm1\}$ has order $8/2=4$ by [F8] and is therefore abelian by [F8], so [F6] gives $Q_8'\subseteq\{\pm1\}$. [F1, F5, F6, F8, algebra]

1.3 The subring $\mathbb C':=\mathbb R+\mathbb Ri$ of $\mathbb H$ is a field isomorphic to $\mathbb C$ via $a+bi\mapsto a+bi$, and one obtains a complex structure on $\mathbb H$ by $a\cdot x:=xa$ for $a\in\mathbb C'$ and $x\in\mathbb H$. This is a $\mathbb C'$-vector space structure: $a\cdot(x+y)=(x+y)a=xa+ya=a\cdot x+a\cdot y$, $(a+a')\cdot x=x(a+a')=xa+xa'$, $(aa')\cdot x=x(aa')=(xa)a'=a\cdot(a'\cdot x)$ and $1\cdot x=x$, all by associativity and distributivity in the ring $\mathbb H$ of [F1]. The elements $1,j$ form a $\mathbb C'$-basis: for $a=a_0+a_1i$ and $b=b_0+b_1i$ one computes $a\cdot1+b\cdot j=a+jb$ with coordinates $(a_0,a_1,b_0,-b_1)$ by [F1], which vanishes only for $a=b=0$; and every $x\in\mathbb H$ with coordinates $(x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3)$ is $x=(x_0+x_1i)\cdot1+(x_2-x_3i)\cdot j$, since $(x_2-x_3i)\cdot j=j(x_2-x_3i)=x_2j-x_3ji=x_2j+x_3k$. Hence $\mathbb H$ with this structure is a $2$-dimensional complex vector space. [F1, construct]

2.1 Conjugation by $u$ permutes the generators of $Q_8$: one computes $ui=\tfrac12(-1+j-i-k)$ and $u^{-1}=\bar u=\tfrac12(-1-i-j-k)$, and expanding the four products $(-1+j-i-k)(-1)$, $(-1+j-i-k)(-i)$, $(-1+j-i-k)(-j)$, $(-1+j-i-k)(-k)$ with the multiplication table of [F1] gives $(-1+j-i-k)(-1-i-j-k)=4k$, so $uiu^{-1}=\tfrac14\cdot 4k=k$; the same expansion with $uj=\tfrac12(-1-i-j+k)$ and $uk=\tfrac12(-1+i-j-k)$ gives $uju^{-1}=i$ and $uku^{-1}=j$. Since $-1$ is central by [F1] and every element of $Q_8$ is $\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k$ by [F2], conjugation by $u$ maps the set $Q_8$ bijectively onto itself; the same holds for $u^2=u^{-1}$, whose conjugation is the inverse permutation. [F1, F2, step 1.1, algebra]

2.2 For $q\in\mathbb H^\times$ the left multiplication $L_q(x):=qx$ is $\mathbb C'$-linear, because $L_q(a\cdot x)=q(xa)=(qx)a=a\cdot L_q(x)$ by associativity [F1]; moreover $L_1=\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb H}$ and $L_{qq'}=L_q\circ L_{q'}$, so $L:\mathbb H^\times\to\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbb C'}(\mathbb H)$ is a group homomorphism. Restricting $L$ to the subgroup $T$ gives a $2$-dimensional complex representation $V:=\mathbb H$ of $T$ in the sense of [F9], since $T$ is a group and the restriction of a homomorphism is a homomorphism. [F1, F4, F9, step 1.3, construct]

2.3 In the $\mathbb C'$-basis $(1,j)$ the matrices are $L_i=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix}$ and $L_j=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$: by step 1.3, $L_i(1)=i=i\cdot1$ and $L_i(j)=ij=k=(-i)\cdot j$ because $(-i)\cdot j=j(-i)=-ji=k$ by [F1], while $L_j(1)=j=1\cdot j$ and $L_j(j)=j^2=-1=(-1)\cdot1$. [F1, step 1.3, algebra]

3.1 $Q_8$ is normal in $T$. Let $N=\{g\in\mathbb H^\times:gQ_8g^{-1}=Q_8\}$; conjugation by any $g$ is a bijection of $\mathbb H^\times$, so $g\in N$ if and only if $g^{-1}\in N$, and if $g,h\in N$ then $(gh)Q_8(gh)^{-1}=g(hQ_8h^{-1})g^{-1}=gQ_8g^{-1}=Q_8$, so $gh\in N$. Thus $N$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb H^\times$ by [F4], and it contains $Q_8$ (conjugation by an element of the subgroup $Q_8$ preserves $Q_8$) and $u,u^2$ by step 2.1. As $T=\langle Q_8\cup\{u\}\rangle$ is the smallest subgroup containing $Q_8\cup\{u\}$ by [F4], one has $T\subseteq N$, that is $Q_8\trianglelefteq T$. [F3, F4, step 2.1, construct]

3.2 The representation $V$ is faithful: if $L_q=\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb H}$ for some $q\in T\subseteq\mathbb H^\times$, then $q=q\cdot1=L_q(1)=1$. [step 2.2, algebra]

3.3 The $T$-module $V$ is irreducible. Let $W\subseteq V$ be a $T$-invariant $\mathbb C'$-subspace with $W\ne0$. If $W\ne V$ then $\dim_{\mathbb C'}W=1$ by step 1.3, so $W=\mathbb C'w$ is a line, and $W$ is invariant under $L_i=\operatorname{diag}(i,-i)$ of step 2.3, hence equals one of the eigenspaces of the two distinct eigenvalues $i\ne-i$, namely $\mathbb C'\cdot1$ or $\mathbb C'\cdot j$. But $W$ is also invariant under $L_j$, whereas $L_j(1)=j\notin\mathbb C'\cdot1$ and $L_j(j)=-1\notin\mathbb C'\cdot j$, since $\mathbb C'\cdot1\cap\mathbb C'\cdot j=\{0\}$ and $1,j\ne0$ by steps 1.3 and 2.3. This contradiction shows $W=V$, so $V$ has no nonzero proper $T$-invariant subspace and is irreducible by [F9]. [F9, step 1.3, step 2.3, algebra]

4.1 Every element of $T$ has the form $qu^m$ with $q\in Q_8$ and $m\in\{0,1,2\}$. For $S=\{qu^m:q\in Q_8,\ m\in\{0,1,2\}\}$ one has $(qu^m)(q'u^{m'})=q\,(u^mq'u^{-m})\,u^{m+m'}$ with $u^mq'u^{-m}\in Q_8$ by step 3.1 (for $m=0,1,2$, using $u^2=u^{-1}$) and $u^{m+m'}=u^{m''}$ for the residue $m''$ of $m+m'$ modulo $3$ by step 1.1, so the product lies in $S$; and $(qu^m)^{-1}=u^{-m}q^{-1}=(u^{-m}q^{-1}u^{m})u^{-m}\in S$. Hence $S$ is a subgroup by [F4], and it contains $Q_8$ and $u$, so $T\subseteq S$ and therefore $T=S$. The expression is unique: $qu^m=q'u^{m'}$ gives $q^{-1}q'=u^{m-m'}\in Q_8\cap\langle u\rangle=\{1\}$ by step 1.1, hence $q=q'$ and $m=m'$ because $1,u,u^2$ are distinct. Consequently $|T|=|Q_8|\cdot|\langle u\rangle|=8\cdot3=24$, the group $T$ is the internal semidirect product of $Q_8$ by $\langle u\rangle\cong C_3$ in the sense of [F3], and $u^m\mapsto u^mQ_8$ is an isomorphism $\langle u\rangle\to T/Q_8$, so $T/Q_8\cong C_3$ is cyclic of order $3$. [F3, F4, step 1.1, step 3.1, algebra]

5.1 The derived subgroup of $T$ is contained in $Q_8$: by step 4.1 the quotient $T/Q_8$ is isomorphic to the abelian group $C_3$, so [F6] gives $[T,T]\subseteq Q_8$. [F6, step 4.1]

6.1 Conversely $Q_8\subseteq T'=[T,T]$. By [F5] and step 2.1, $[u,i]=uiu^{-1}i^{-1}=k(-i)=-j$, $[u,j]=uju^{-1}j^{-1}=i(-j)=-k$ and $[u,k]=uku^{-1}k^{-1}=j(-k)=-i$ all lie in $T'$, where $i^{-1}=-i$, $j^{-1}=-j$ and $k^{-1}=-k$ by [F2]; as $T'$ is a subgroup containing $i=-(-i)$, $j=-(-j)$, $k=-(-k)$ and $-1=i\cdot i$, it contains all eight elements of $Q_8$. Together with step 5.1 this gives $T'=Q_8$. [F4, F5, F2, step 2.1, step 5.1, algebra]

7.1 The derived series of $T$ terminates: by steps 6.1 and 1.2, $T''=[T',T']=[Q_8,Q_8]=Q_8'=\{\pm1\}$, and $T'''=[\{\pm1\},\{\pm1\}]=1$ because $\{\pm1\}$ is abelian (its commutators are trivial by [F5]). Hence $T^{(3)}=1$ and $T$ is solvable by [F7]. [F5, F7, step 6.1, step 1.2]

7.2 The group $T$ has no subgroup of index $2$. Suppose $H\le T$ satisfies $[T:H]=2$. Then $H\trianglelefteq T$ and $T/H$ has order $2$ by [F8], so the quotient map $\pi:T\to T/H$ is a homomorphism onto an abelian group; by [F6] it factors as $\pi=\psi\circ q$ with $q:T\to T/T'$ the quotient map and $\psi:T/T'\to T/H$. By steps 6.1 and 4.1, $T/T'=T/Q_8\cong C_3$; writing $\bar x$ for a generator of $T/T'$ one has $\psi(\bar x)^3=\psi(\bar x^3)=1$ and $\psi(\bar x)^2=1$ because $T/H$ has order $2$, so the order of $\psi(\bar x)$ divides both $3$ and $2$ by [F8] and is therefore $1$; hence $\psi$ is trivial and so is $\pi$, contradicting that $\pi$ is surjective onto a group of order $2$. [F6, F8, step 4.1, step 6.1, algebra]

8.1 The character $\chi_V$ of the irreducible $2$-dimensional $T$-module $V$ is an irreducible complex character of $T$ by [F9] and [F10], and it is not monomial. Suppose $\chi_V=\operatorname{Ind}_H^T\lambda$ for some $H\le T$ and linear character $\lambda$; by [F10] the underlying representation is then $V\cong\operatorname{Ind}_H^TL$ for the one-dimensional $H$-module $L$ affording $\lambda$, so $\dim_{\mathbb C'}V=[T:H]\dim_{\mathbb C'}L=[T:H]$ by [F10], that is $[T:H]=2$, contradicting step 7.2. Hence $T$ is a solvable group, by step 7.1, with an irreducible complex character that is not monomial, so $T$ is not an $M$-group by [F10]; the statement that every finite solvable group is an $M$-group is therefore false. [F9, F10, step 7.1, step 3.3, step 7.2] ∎
