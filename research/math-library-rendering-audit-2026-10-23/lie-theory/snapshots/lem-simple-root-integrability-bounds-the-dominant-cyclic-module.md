## Statement

Assume the Axiom of Choice. Let $\mathfrak g$ be a finite-dimensional complex
semisimple Lie algebra with Cartan subalgebra $\mathfrak h$ and a chosen
positive system, and let $\lambda$ be dominant integral. Then the cyclic module
$M_{\mathrm{int}}(\lambda)$ of
[[def-dominant-integrable-highest-weight-cyclic-module]] is finite-dimensional.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** The Axiom of Choice, such $\mathfrak g,\mathfrak h$, a chosen positive system with simple roots $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_r$, a dominant integral $\lambda$ with $m_i=\langle\lambda,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle$, the module $M=M_{\mathrm{int}}(\lambda)$ and its generator $v=v_\lambda$.

[A1] The Axiom of Choice is assumed; it enters through the root-space theory supplying [L3] and through the Weyl-group suppliers [L6]–[L8] ([[def-axiom-of-choice]]).

[L1] $v\ne0$ has weight $\lambda$, $\mathfrak n^+\cdot v=0$, and $f_i^{\,m_i+1}\cdot v=0$ for chosen lowering vectors $f_i\in\mathfrak g_{-\alpha_i}$; $M=U(\mathfrak g)v$ is generated by $v$ ([[lem-pbw-shows-the-dominant-cyclic-highest-weight-generator-survives]]).

[L2] $M=U(\mathfrak n^-)\mathbb Cv$, every weight of $M$ satisfies $\mu\le\lambda$ in the root order, and $M_\lambda=\mathbb Cv$ ([[lem-highest-weight-modules-have-weights-below-the-top-weight]], [[def-partial-order-on-weights]]).

[L3] For each $i$ there is an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-triple $(e_i,f_i,h_{\alpha_i})$ with $[e_i,f_i]=h_{\alpha_i}$; a vector $x\in\mathfrak g_\alpha$ maps $M_\mu$ into $M_{\mu+\alpha}$ ([[thm-root-sl-two-triple]], [[prop-root-vectors-shift-weight-spaces]]).

[L4] A finite-dimensional $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module is a direct sum of irreducible submodules; an irreducible submodule has a top weight $m\ge0$ and $h$-eigenvalues $m,m-2,\dots,-m$, each on a one-dimensional subspace ([[thm-finite-dimensional-representations-of-sl-two]]).

[L5] The PBW monomials in an ordered basis of $\mathfrak g$ adapted to $\mathfrak g=\mathfrak n^-\oplus\mathfrak h\oplus\mathfrak n^+$ form a basis of $U(\mathfrak g)$ ([[thm-triangular-decomposition-of-a-complex-semisimple-lie-algebra]], [[thm-poincare-birkhoff-witt]]).

[L6] Every element of the Weyl group $W$ is a product of simple reflections ([[prop-weyl-length-equals-positive-root-inversion-number]]), and $W$ is finite ([[prop-the-weyl-group-is-finite-and-acts-faithfully-on-the-root-system]]).

[L7] The open Weyl chambers are the connected components of the complement of the finitely many root hyperplanes, and $W$ acts simply transitively on them; the fundamental chamber is $C=\{(x,\alpha_i)>0\}$ with closure $\overline C=\{(x,\alpha_i)\ge0\}$ ([[def-open-and-closed-weyl-chambers]], [[thm-the-weyl-group-acts-simply-transitively-on-weyl-chambers]]).

[L8] $\lambda$ is an integral element of $E$ with $\lambda=\sum_im_i\omega_i$, $m_i\ge0$, and $(\lambda,\alpha_i)=m_i(\alpha_i,\alpha_i)/2$; the form on $E$ is a positive definite inner product ([[prop-dominant-integral-weights-are-nonnegative-combinations-of-fundamental-weights]], [[def-fundamental-weights]], [[prop-the-roots-form-a-reduced-crystallographic-euclidean-root-system]]).

[L9] Every positive root is a nonzero nonnegative integral combination of the simple roots, and the simple roots form a basis of $E$ ([[thm-simple-roots-form-a-basis-and-every-root-has-one-sign-of-integral-coordinates]]).

## Proof

**Proof technique:** direct.

1.1 Fix $i$ and consider the vectors $f_i^kv$, $k\ge0$; the $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-commutation identity $[e_i,f_i^k]=kf_i^{k-1}(h_{\alpha_i}-(k-1))$, proved by induction from $[e_i,f_i]=h_{\alpha_i}$ and $[h_{\alpha_i},f_i]=-2f_i$, gives $e_i\cdot(f_i^kv)=k(m_i-k+1)f_i^{k-1}v$ and $h_{\alpha_i}\cdot(f_i^kv)=(m_i-2k)f_i^kv$ by [L1] and [L3], so the span of $\{f_i^kv:k\ge0\}$ is an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-submodule of $M$. [A1, L1, L3]

2.1 By [L1] $f_i^{m_i+1}v=0$, so the submodule of step 1.1 is spanned by $v,f_iv,\dots,f_i^{m_i}v$ and is finite dimensional; hence $v$ generates a finite-dimensional $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module for every $i$. [L1, step 1.1]

3.1 Let $M^{\mathrm{int}}:=\{w\in M:\text{for every }j\text{ the }\mathfrak{sl}_2\text{-module generated by }w\text{ is finite dimensional}\}$. It is a linear subspace, because the module generated by $w+w'$ is contained in the sum of the modules generated by $w$ and by $w'$. Let $w\in M^{\mathrm{int}}$, $x\in\mathfrak g$, and fix $j$. Put $S:=U(\mathfrak{sl}_2^{(j)})w$, which is finite dimensional by the definition of $M^{\mathrm{int}}$, and let $X:=U(\mathfrak{sl}_2^{(j)})x$ for the adjoint action; this is finite dimensional because $X\subseteq\mathfrak g$. The representation identity $y\cdot(x'\cdot s)=[y,x']\cdot s+x'\cdot(y\cdot s)$ for $y\in\mathfrak{sl}_2^{(j)}$, $x'\in X$, and $s\in S$ shows that the finite-dimensional space $\operatorname{span}(X\cdot S)$ is an $\mathfrak{sl}_2^{(j)}$-submodule containing $xw$. Hence $U(\mathfrak{sl}_2^{(j)})(xw)\subseteq\operatorname{span}(X\cdot S)$ is finite dimensional. Therefore $xw\in M^{\mathrm{int}}$ for all $x\in\mathfrak g$, so $M^{\mathrm{int}}$ is a subrepresentation. [L3, step 2.1]

4.1 Since $v\in M^{\mathrm{int}}$ by step 2.1 and $M=U(\mathfrak g)v$ by [L1], the subrepresentation $M^{\mathrm{int}}$ is all of $M$; in particular every vector of $M$ generates a finite-dimensional $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module for every $i$. [L1, step 2.1, step 3.1]

5.1 Let $\mu$ be a weight of $M$ and $0\ne t\in M_\mu$; fix $i$, put $q=\mu(h_{\alpha_i})$, and let $T=U(\mathfrak{sl}_2^{(i)})t$ be the finite-dimensional module generated by $t$, which by [L4] is a direct sum of irreducible summands $T=\bigoplus_rT_r$; the $q$-eigenspace of $h_{\alpha_i}$ on $T$ contains $t$ and is the sum of its intersections with the $T_r$, so some summand $T_0$ has a nonzero $q$-eigenspace; choose $0\ne w\in T_0$ with $h_{\alpha_i}\cdot w=qw$. [L4, step 4.1]

6.1 The summand $T_0$ is irreducible with top weight $m\ge0$ and eigenvalues $m,m-2,\dots,-m$, so $q=m-2k$ for some $k\in\{0,\dots,m\}$; if $q>0$ and $f_i^{\,q}w=0$, take the least $j$ with $0\le j<q$ and $f_i^{\,j+1}w=0$: then $0\ne f_i^{\,j}w$ lies in the kernel of $f_i$ in the eigenspace of eigenvalue $q-2j$, and the submodule of the irreducible module $T_0$ that it generates is spanned by its $e_i$-orbit, of dimension $\frac{m-(q-2j)}{2}+1<m+1$ because $2j\le2q-2<m+q$, so it is a nonzero proper submodule of $T_0$, contradicting irreducibility; hence $f_i^{\,q}w\ne0$. Applying the same argument inside each irreducible summand $T_r$ with $t_r\ne0$, whose top weight $m_r\ge0$ satisfies $q=m_r-2k_r$ for some $k_r\in\{0,\dots,m_r\}$, gives $f_i^{\,q}t_r\ne0$ for every $r$ with $t_r\ne0$; since the summands are independent, this gives $f_i^{\,q}t=\sum_rf_i^{\,q}t_r\ne0$, and $f_i^{\,q}t\in M_{\mu-q\alpha_i}$ is a nonzero vector by [L3]; if $q<0$ the same argument with $e_i$ gives $0\ne e_i^{-q}t\in M_{\mu-q\alpha_i}$; if $q=0$ then $\mu-q\alpha_i=\mu$ is already a weight, so in every case $s_i(\mu)=\mu-q\alpha_i$ is a weight of $M$. [L3, L4, step 5.1]

7.1 By step 6.1 the weight set of $M$ is invariant under every simple reflection; since $W$ is generated by the simple reflections by [L6], the weight set of $M$ is $W$-invariant. [L6, step 6.1]

8.1 Every element of $E$ lies in the closure of some open chamber: the union of the finitely many root hyperplanes is closed with empty interior, so any point is a limit of points outside it, each of which lies in a chamber, and since there are finitely many chambers some chamber contains a sequence converging to the point; by the simple transitivity of [L7] some $w\in W$ carries that chamber to the fundamental chamber, so $w(\mu)\in\overline C$, that is, $(w\mu,\alpha_i)\ge0$ for all $i$. [L7, step 7.1]

9.1 The weight $w(\mu)$ of step 8.1 satisfies $w(\mu)\le\lambda$ by [L2], and only finitely many dominant weights are below $\lambda$: let $\nu$ be dominant with $\nu\le\lambda$ and write $n:=\lambda-\nu=\sum_in_i\alpha_i$ with $n_i\ge0$. Both $\lambda$ and $\nu$ are dominant, so $(\lambda,\alpha_i)=m_i(\alpha_i,\alpha_i)/2\ge0$ and $(\nu,\alpha_i)\ge0$ for every $i$ by [L8]; hence $(\lambda,n)=\sum_in_i(\lambda,\alpha_i)\ge0$ and $(\nu,n)=\sum_in_i(\nu,\alpha_i)\ge0$, and therefore $(n,n)=(\lambda,n)-(\nu,n)\le(\lambda,n)\le|\lambda|\,|n|$ for the positive definite inner product of [L8]. Thus $|n|\le|\lambda|$, and since the simple roots form a basis of $E$ while all norms on $E$ are equivalent, the nonnegative integers $n_i$ are bounded by a constant depending only on the chosen simple roots times $|\lambda|$; as $n_i$ is determined by $\nu$, only finitely many dominant weights $\nu$ are below $\lambda$. [L2, L8, step 8.1]

10.1 Each weight of $M$ is $W$-conjugate to one of the finitely many dominant weights below $\lambda$ by steps 7.1, 8.1 and 9.1, and $W$ is finite by [L6]; hence $M$ has only finitely many weights. [L6, step 7.1, step 8.1, step 9.1]

11.1 Each weight space $M_\mu$ is finite dimensional: by [L2] and [L5] the space $M$ is spanned by the vectors $u\cdot v$ with $u$ a monomial in a basis $f_1,\dots,f_N$ of $\mathfrak n^-$ consisting of root vectors $f_j\in\mathfrak g_{-\beta_j}$, and $u\cdot v\in M_\mu$ forces $\lambda-\mu=\sum_ja_j\beta_j$ with $a_j$ the exponents of $u$; writing $\lambda-\mu=\sum_in_i\alpha_i$ and $\beta_j=\sum_ic_i^{(j)}\alpha_i$ with $c_i^{(j)}\ge0$ and using uniqueness of the simple-root coefficients in [L9] gives $n_i=\sum_ja_jc_i^{(j)}\ge a_jc_i^{(j)}$, and since each positive root has some $c_i^{(j)}\ge1$ we get $a_j\le\max_in_i$; thus only finitely many monomials contribute and $M_\mu$ is spanned by finitely many vectors. [L2, L5, L9, step 10.1]

12.1 Steps 10.1 and 11.1 show that $M$ is a direct sum of finitely many finite-dimensional weight spaces, hence finite dimensional, as asserted. [step 10.1, step 11.1] ∎
