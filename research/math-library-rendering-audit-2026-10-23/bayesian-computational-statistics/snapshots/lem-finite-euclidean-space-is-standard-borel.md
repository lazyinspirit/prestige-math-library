## Statement

For every natural $n\ge1$, let $\mathbb R^n$ be the set of functions
$x:n\to\mathbb R$ with its finite-product topology and Borel sigma-algebra
([[def-product-topology]], [[def-borel-sigma-algebra]]). Then
$(\mathbb R^n,\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n))$ is standard Borel.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** A natural $n\ge1$; $\mathbb R^n$ with its finite-product topology;
the Euclidean metric $d_2$ and maximum metric $d_\infty$ from
[[lem-metrics-on-rn]].

[F1] The functions $x:n\to\mathbb R$ form $\mathbb R^n$; $d_\infty$ is a
metric, and its balls are defined by the metric topology
([[lem-metrics-on-rn]], [[def-metric-topology]]).

[F2] Every nonempty finite set of real numbers has a maximum and minimum, each
belonging to the set and bounding it on the corresponding side
([[lem-finite-set-has-max]], [[def-max-min]]).

[F3] For $u\in\mathbb R$, $u^2=|u|^2$; finite sums of nonnegative terms are
nonnegative, each term is at most the sum, sums are monotone, and
$\sum_{i<n}c=nc$ ([[lem-of-abs-value]], [[lem-finite-sum-laws]]).

[F4] Nonnegative numbers have unique nonnegative square roots; square-root
order is monotone, and the real image of a natural $n\ge1$ is positive
([[thm-of-square-roots]], [[lem-of-square-monotone]],
[[lem-of-naturals-positive]]).

[F5] The Euclidean metric $d_2$ on $\mathbb R^n$ is complete for $n\ge1$
([[thm-euclidean-space-complete]]).

[F6] $\mathbb Q^n$ is at most countable and dense, and rational open boxes form
a basis for the finite-product topology on $\mathbb R^n$
([[thm-rational-points-and-boxes-in-rn]]).

[F7] Polish means separable and admitting a compatible complete metric;
separable means having an at most countable dense subset
([[def-polish-space]], [[def-separable-space]]).

[F8] Standard Borel means measurably isomorphic to the Borel space of a Polish
topology; a Borel sigma-algebra is generated by the open sets
([[def-standard-borel-space]], [[def-borel-sigma-algebra]]).

[F9] A metric Cauchy sequence has tails whose pairwise distances are below
each positive rational tolerance, and metric convergence means distance to
the limit tends to zero ([[def-cauchy-in-metric]], [[def-metric-convergence]]).

[F10] Every nonempty real interval contains a rational number, so each positive
real tolerance has a smaller positive rational tolerance
([[lem-rat-embeds-dense]]).

## Proof

**Proof technique:** compare the maximum metric topology with the product
topology, then use completeness and rational density.

1.1 Every $d_\infty$-ball about $x$ of radius $r>0$ is the product box $\prod_{i<n}(x_i-r,x_i+r)$, so it is open in the product topology. Conversely, let $U$ be product-open and $x\in U$. By [F6], choose a rational box $Q=\prod_{i<n}(a_i,b_i)$ with $x\in Q\subseteq U$. The finite set $S=\{x_i-a_i,b_i-x_i:i<n\}$ is nonempty because $n\ge1$, and every member is positive. By [F2], $r=\min S$ exists and is positive. If $d_\infty(x,y)<r$, then $|x_i-y_i|\le d_\infty(x,y)<r\le x_i-a_i$ and $|x_i-y_i|<r\le b_i-x_i$ for every $i<n$, hence $y\in Q\subseteq U$. Thus each product-open set is $d_\infty$-open, and the topologies agree. [F1, F2, F6]

1.2 Fix $x,y\in\mathbb R^n$, put $u_i=x_i-y_i$ and $M=d_\infty(x,y)$. By [F1] and [F2], $M=|u_j|$ for some $j<n$ and $0\le|u_i|\le M$ for every $i<n$. Hence [F3] gives $M^2=u_j^2\le\sum_{i<n}u_i^2=d_2(x,y)^2$, so [F4] yields $M\le d_2(x,y)$. Also $u_i^2=|u_i|^2\le M^2$ for each $i$, whence $d_2(x,y)^2=\sum_{i<n}u_i^2\le\sum_{i<n}M^2=nM^2$ by [F3]. Since $n>0$, [F4] makes $\sqrt n\,M$ nonnegative and $(\sqrt n\,M)^2=nM^2$; square-root monotonicity now gives $d_2(x,y)\le\sqrt n\,M$. Thus $d_\infty\le d_2\le\sqrt n\,d_\infty$ for all pairs. [F1, F2, F3, F4, algebra]

2.1 Let $(x^{(m)})$ be $d_\infty$-Cauchy and fix a positive rational $\varepsilon$. By [F10], choose a positive rational $\rho<\varepsilon/(1+\sqrt n)$. The Cauchy condition [F9] gives a tail on which $d_\infty(x^{(m)},x^{(l)})<\rho$. Step 1.2 then gives $d_2(x^{(m)},x^{(l)})\le\sqrt n\,d_\infty(x^{(m)},x^{(l)})<\sqrt n\rho<\varepsilon$, so the sequence is $d_2$-Cauchy. [F9, F10, step 1.2, algebra]

3.1 By [F5], this sequence has a $d_2$-limit $x\in\mathbb R^n$. Step 1.2 gives $d_\infty(x^{(m)},x)\le d_2(x^{(m)},x)\to0$; by [F9] this is convergence in $d_\infty$. Therefore $d_\infty$ is complete. [F5, F9, step 1.2, step 2.1]

4.1 By [F6], $\mathbb Q^n$ is at most countable and dense in the product topology. Step 1.1 identifies that topology with the $d_\infty$ topology, so the same subset is dense for $d_\infty$. Thus the product topology is separable by [F7], and step 3.1 supplies its compatible complete metric. Consequently $\mathbb R^n$ with the product topology is Polish. [F6, F7, step 1.1, step 3.1]

5.1 The identity from $(\mathbb R^n,\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n))$ to the Polish space of step 4.1 is a bijection. The topologies are identical by step 1.1, so they have the same open sets and therefore, by [F8], the same Borel sigma-algebra. The identity is thus a measurable isomorphism as required by the definition of standard Borel. [F8, step 1.1, step 4.1] ∎
