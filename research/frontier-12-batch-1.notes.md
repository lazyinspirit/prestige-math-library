## Proposed page summaries

### `the-field-of-fractions-and-localisation`

Localization turns selected elements of a commutative ring into units by forming fractions and imposing the equality relation forced by possible zero divisors. This page constructs that relation, proves that arithmetic is well defined, identifies the kernel of the canonical map, and proves the universal property. Specializing to the nonzero elements of an integral domain constructs its field of fractions, embeds the domain, and gives the expected universal property and uniqueness. The named consequence $F(t)=\operatorname{Frac}(F[t])$ for a field $F$, especially $\mathbb R(t)=\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb R[t])$, is kept on the main page so later results may cite it.

The page then develops principal and iterated localization, local rings, localization at a prime ideal, the unit test, ideal correspondence, and compatibility with quotient rings. These results identify the local ring $R_{\mathfrak p}$ and its residue field without importing module theory. The companion page computes familiar localizations and records the essential boundary failures: inverting zero collapses the ring, nonzero elements need not be multiplicative outside a domain, zero-divisor denominators can destroy injectivity, and a total quotient ring need not be a field.

### `splitting-fields`

A polynomial splits when it factors completely into linear terms, and its splitting field is the field generated by all its roots. This page proves existence by Kronecker’s root-adjunction step and strong induction, extends the construction to finite families, and identifies products with composites inside a common extension. It then proves that coefficient-field isomorphisms transport roots, extend across simple root adjunctions, and ultimately extend to splitting fields, yielding uniqueness up to an isomorphism over the base field.

The construction also yields an explicit spanning family of root monomials bounded by $n!$ for a degree-$n$ polynomial, stated without presupposing the later theory of vector-space dimension and the tower law. The page treats $x^n-a$, explains why endomorphisms permute roots, defines normal algebraic extensions by minimal-polynomial splitting, proves that algebraic splitting extensions are normal, and proves that finitely generated normal extensions are splitting fields. The companion page supplies quadratic, cubic, repeated-root, and finite-family computations together with counterexamples to confusing a root field with a splitting field or uniqueness up to isomorphism with uniqueness of the isomorphism.

## Convention decisions and disagreements

- Rings and ring homomorphisms are unital, matching the published ring definitions.
- A multiplicative subset contains $1$ and is closed under multiplication. It is allowed to contain $0$, following Stacks and the broad CRing construction; the resulting localization is the zero ring. Sources that build `0 not in S` into the definition are handled as a convention difference, not as a contradiction.
- A local ring is taken to be a **nonzero** commutative ring with a unique maximal ideal. Some sources allow the zero ring to count as local under alternate ideal conventions; excluding it matches the library’s proper maximal-ideal convention and keeps the unit characterizations literal.
- `Frac(D)` is reserved for an integral domain. For a general commutative ring, inverting all non-zero-divisors produces the total quotient ring, which need not be a field.
- `F(t)` is reserved for `Frac(F[t])` with $F$ a field; the scaffold does not introduce potentially ambiguous `D(t)` notation for a general domain. It writes ordinary numerals and never applies a canonical embedding symbol to a natural number.
- A splitting field is defined for a nonzero polynomial or a family of nonzero polynomials. Nonzero constants split vacuously, and the empty family has base field $F$ as its splitting field. The zero polynomial is excluded because its degree and generated-root minimality are unsuitable for the induction.
- Root multiplicities are retained when writing a complete linear factorization; the finite root set used by endomorphisms is the set of distinct roots.
- Splitting fields are unique only up to existence of an $F$-isomorphism. The isomorphism itself is generally nonunique.
- The source degree bound is translated into an explicit spanning family of at most $n!$ root monomials. The scaffold does not write `[E:F]` or invoke the tower law, because general vector-space dimension is not in the prerequisite closure.
- Normality uses the standard algebraic-extension/minimal-polynomial splitting criterion. Both directions supported by current prerequisites are included: an algebraic splitting extension is normal, and a finitely generated normal extension is a splitting field. The degree page can remove the first result’s explicit algebraicity assumption after establishing transitivity of algebraicity for finite towers.
- Judson’s root-field counterexample uses $x^3-3$; the B page uses the equally standard $x^3-2$ so it can reuse its preceding fully worked splitting-field example. This is an intentional change of witness, not a change of theorem.

No source disagreement changes a theorem’s truth value.
