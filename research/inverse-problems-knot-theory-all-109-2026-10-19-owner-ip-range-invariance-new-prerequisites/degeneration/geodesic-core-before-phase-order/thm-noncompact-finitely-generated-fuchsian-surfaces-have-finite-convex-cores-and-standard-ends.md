## Statement

Assume AC, hence Countable Choice. Let $H\le\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb R)$ be discrete torsion-free nonelementary and finitely generated, and suppose $Y=H^2/H$ is noncompact. Let $h$ be its free rank supplied by [[lem-finitely-generated-noncompact-fuchsian-surfaces-have-compact-incompressible-cores]]. Then $h\ge2$. Its convex core $C(\Lambda_H)/H$ is a finite-area surface with finitely many closed geodesic boundary circles and finitely many standard cusp ends, with a compact truncation $N$ and
$$\operatorname{area}(C(\Lambda_H)/H)=2\pi(h-1).$$
Here $C(\Lambda_H)$ is the hyperbolic convex hull in $H^2$. The complement of the core is exactly one standard funnel for each geodesic boundary circle:
$$\{r>0,\ t\in\mathbb R/L\mathbb Z\},\qquad ds^2=dr^2+\cosh^2r\,dt^2.$$
Each cusp is exactly $\{y>T\}/\langle z\mapsto z+\ell\rangle$, with its actual primitive peripheral generator, and the cusp tails may be chosen disjoint. The total number of geodesic rims and cusps is at most $h+1$.

More precisely, any compact essential surface core supplied by the preceding lemma can have its boundary curves replaced, through finitely many ambient isotopies in $Y$, by the simple geodesic in each loxodromic class and a deep standard horocycle in each parabolic class. All rim labels and their homotopy classes are retained. If this cover is mapped to a different ambient surface, distinct rims MAY project to the same geodesic; this theorem asserts embedded boundaries in $Y$ itself and does not exclude those paired projected rims.

If $H$ is an infinite-index finitely generated subgroup of a cocompact torsion-free Fuchsian group, there are no parabolics and the core itself is compact, with area at most $2\pi(R-1)$ when $H$ is generated by at most $R$ elements. This is the closed-base operation needed before a divergence-field expansion argument; no quasiconvexity, Scott core theorem or finitely-generated-implies-geometrically-finite theorem is invoked by name.

## Proof

1.1 We first record the elementary annular facts used in the straightening, with their justification. For a nontrivial cyclic real group $A$, the quotient $H^2/A$ is an open cylinder, both in the parabolic model $z\mapsto z+\ell$ and in the loxodromic model $\log z=u+iv$, $u\bmod L$, $0<v<\pi$. Reparametrizing the open transverse interval identifies it topologically with $\mathbb C^*$. The fundamental group is $\mathbb Z$, computed by lifting the angular coordinate to $\mathbb R$; a loop's lifted endpoint differs from its start by its integer winding. Any essential embedded circle in this cylinder represents a generator or its inverse. Indeed its Jordan disc in the plane contains zero, since otherwise that disc would contract it in $\mathbb C^*$. By [F3] the punctured Jordan disc is homeomorphic to a punctured round disc, so its fundamental group is $\mathbb Z$ generated by its boundary. Its inclusion into $\mathbb C^*$ is onto on fundamental groups because it contains a small round circle around zero. A surjection $\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z$ has multiplier $\pm1$, proving the assertion. Two disjoint essential circles in the cylinder bound a compact annulus: their Jordan discs both contain zero and hence are nested; cut the region between them along one simple joining arc to obtain a Jordan disc, use [F3] to identify it with a rectangle with the two arc copies as opposite sides, and reglue those sides. A joining arc exists by polygonal connectivity after small boundary collars; removal of an inner disc preserves connectivity by replacing excursions through it by boundary arcs. These constructions prove the annulus facts rather than assume a classification of noncompact surfaces. [A1, F2, F3, construct, algebra]

2.1 Every essential embedded circle in $Y$ is primitive in the full stabilizer of its ideal fixed set. Lift it to the annular cover for that full stabilizer from [F2]. Its lift is an embedded essential circle, and if its element is the $m$th power of the stabilizer generator, its class in that annulus is $m$. Step 1.1 gives $m=\pm1$. For a loxodromic circle, a lifted period arc is compact and all its translates lie at bounded distance from the axis; thus the full proper lifted line has the loxodromic ideal endpoints. Disjoint such lifted lines have nonalternating endpoint pairs by Jordan separation: a proper arc together with a complementary ideal-circle arc separates the closed disc. Their geodesic axes consequently do not cross. Primitivity and the full stabilizer classification show that the axis projects to a SIMPLE closed geodesic in $Y$: distinct translates have disjoint axes unless they have the same endpoints, in which case the translating element belongs to its primitive cyclic stabilizer. This also proves disjointness of geodesic representatives for disjoint nonparallel essential circles. [F1, F2, F3, step 1.1, construct, algebra]

3.1 We need the parallel-circle annulus in $Y$, not merely in a cyclic cover. If two disjoint essential circles are freely homotopic up to reversal, take their lifts in the cyclic cover for their common primitive class. By step 1.1 they bound a compact annulus there. Its lift to $H^2$ is a strip between two disjoint proper periodic lines. Its ideal boundary consists only of the two fixed points in the loxodromic case, or the one parabolic point in the parabolic case: each period arc is compact, so the strip has bounded normal width about the axis, or bounded positive heights in parabolic coordinates. The strip projects injectively into $Y$. To see this, all deck translates of its boundary lines are disjoint, since they lift the two embedded circles. If another translated strip overlaps it, either one of its boundary lines enters the first strip, or one entire strip is contained in the other. A proper periodic line contained in that strip must have its ideal fixed set in the indicated one- or two-point ideal boundary. In the loxodromic case an elementary line with both endpoints there has the same pair; a parabolic fixing just one endpoint cannot occur together with that loxodromic, since conjugating its translation by loxodromic powers would violate discreteness. In the parabolic case the same argument excludes a loxodromic sharing its parabolic point. Thus the translating element preserves the same fixed set and belongs to the full primitive cyclic stabilizer [F2]. It preserves the original strip. This also rules out strict containment, by applying the argument to a boundary line of the smaller strip. Hence distinct translates have disjoint strip interiors, and the annulus embeds. This proves that disjoint parallel essential circles in $Y$ bound an actual compact annulus. [F1, F2, F3, step 1.1, step 2.1, construct, algebra]

4.1 No two boundary circles of the nonelementary core $N_0$ can be parallel in $Y$. If some are, take adjacent circles in their finite parallel family so that the intervening annulus from step 3.1 contains no other core boundary. Its interior either belongs entirely to $N_0$ or lies entirely outside it. In the former case connectedness and the two boundary sides make $N_0$ that annulus, contrary to its noncyclic fundamental group. In the latter case define a continuous map $Y\to S^1$ by $\exp(2\pi it)$ on the annulus with transverse coordinate $t\in[0,1]$, and by $1$ outside it. The definitions agree on both boundary circles. A loop crossing the annulus once and returning through connected $N_0$ has image of winding one, while every loop in $N_0$ maps constantly to $1$. This contradicts the surjectivity $\pi_1(N_0)\to\pi_1(Y)$ in [F1]. Thus all core boundary classes are distinct, even though their projections to a DIFFERENT ambient surface need not be distinct. In particular the loxodromic geodesics from step 2.1 are pairwise disjoint and distinct. [F1, step 1.1, step 3.1, construct, algebra]

5.1 For each parabolic boundary class choose a deep standard precisely invariant horoball from [F2]. There are finitely many such classes, distinct by step 4.1; choose their tails disjoint and deep enough to avoid the compact $N_0$ and the finitely many closed target geodesics from step 2.1. Proper embedding of the closed horocylinder quotients [F2] ensures that increasing the height eventually avoids any specified compact set. Each boundary circle and its target horocycle are disjoint and parallel, so step 3.1 supplies an embedded annulus between them. Its interior contains no other essential core boundary: such a boundary would have to lie entirely in the annulus, hence be parallel to its core by step 1.1, contrary to step 4.1. The same reasoning excludes the already chosen disjoint nonparallel target curves. A product parametrization of this annulus, with small collars on its two sides, moves the original circle to the horocycle by an ambient isotopy: choose an increasing homeomorphism of the transverse interval fixed at its endpoints and carrying the first level to the second, and interpolate monotonically to identity. It preserves orientation and is the identity outside that collar. The finitely many parabolic rims can therefore be placed on their actual standard horocycles without changing the other rim classes or the core's inclusion isomorphism. [A1, F1, F2, step 1.1, step 3.1, step 4.1, construct, algebra]

6.1 We supply the geodesic-circle isotopy as well, rather than cite it. Let $C$ be an essential simple loxodromic rim and $A$ its simple geodesic. Approximate $C$ within its finite smooth collar by a sufficiently fine simple geodesic polygon, using the collar to identify this approximation with a small isotopy; the positional and tangent errors tend to zero on compact regular arcs, so the polygon is a normal collar graph and is embedded. Choose its finitely many vertices slightly generically so its segments do not coincide with any segment of $A$ and avoid tangencies and vertex intersections. Properness gives finitely many relevant lifted segment intersections, so these are finitely many forbidden choices. The resulting $C$ meets $A$ transversely in finitely many points. If they meet, in their common primitive annular cover a lifted $C$ returns to the axis on one side, because its finite crossing pattern repeats periodically. A return arc and the intervening axis segment bound a Jordan bigon. The full locally finite arrangement of lifts of $C$ and $A$ cuts this compact disc by finitely many arcs. A $C$-arc can enter it only through the $A$ side, and an $A$-arc only through the $C$ side, since lifts of the same embedded circle are disjoint. Choosing an innermost returning arc repeatedly therefore gives a bigon whose interior contains no such arc. This is a complementary face of the full arrangement. Its interior has disjoint translates. Its closed disc also projects injectively: any identification on one boundary side must come from the common primitive cyclic stabilizer; two periodic return arcs in the same half-plane cannot have alternating axis endpoints, so the bounding axis interval has length less than one primitive period. Equality would identify consecutive crossings, impossible because the transverse crossing pattern of a periodic curve changes sides an even positive number of times per period. Interior side points are not intersections, so no identification between the two different sides is possible. Thus this is an actual embedded bigon in $Y$. [A1, F1, F2, F3, step 1.1, step 2.1, construct, algebra]

7.1 An isotopy across that empty bigon removes its two intersections and creates none. In its disc parametrization take a narrow collar of the two sides and slide the $C$ arc across the disc to a nearby arc parallel to the $A$ side; a monotone rectangle-coordinate interpolation gives a boundary-compatible ambient isotopy supported in a slightly enlarged disc. All other $C$ arcs miss the empty bigon and that collar. Repeating decreases the finite intersection count until $C$ and $A$ are disjoint, or already agree. When disjoint, step 3.1's compact annulus and the product-coordinate move of step 5.1 carry $C$ exactly to $A$. The process can fix all previously positioned target rims: such a rim is essential and disjoint from both $C$ and $A$, so it cannot cross a bigon's boundary or be contained in its disc. Nor can a nonparallel essential target lie inside the final annulus. Consequently the isotopies avoid those fixed curves. Induction over the finitely many loxodromic boundaries gives a compact surface $N$ homeomorphic to $N_0$, with the same labeled homotopy classes and inclusion isomorphism, whose boundaries are exactly the target geodesics and standard peripheral horocycles. Its final boundary is smooth even if intermediate polygonal isotopies were only piecewise smooth. [F1, F2, F3, step 3.1, step 4.1, step 5.1, step 6.1, construct, algebra]

8.1 The chosen cusp tails lie on the OUTSIDE side of $N$. Otherwise connectedness would put $N$ inside the cyclic horocylinder, making its entire fundamental-group image cyclic, contrary to nonelementarity and [F1]. Add these closed tails to $N$ and call the resulting closed region $N_\infty\subset Y$. Its only boundaries are the finitely many geodesic rims. The added cylinders retract to their boundary circles, so inclusion $N\to N_\infty$ induces an isomorphism on fundamental groups; in particular its image in $Y$ is all of $H$. Its full lift $D\subset H^2$ is connected, by the elementary lifted-path argument in [F1], and every boundary component of $D$ is a complete geodesic. The family is locally finite by properness and compact periods. The region is precisely the intersection of the closed inner half-planes of these boundary lines. Indeed it lies on the inner side of each by connectedness. If a point in all these inner half-planes were outside $D$, the geodesic segment from an interior point of $D$ to it would have a first exit across a boundary line; a segment with both endpoints on the inner side of a geodesic cannot exit across it. This contradiction proves equality and therefore convexity. If there are no boundary lines, $D$ is the whole plane. [F1, F2, step 5.1, step 7.1, construct, algebra]

9.1 The outward open half-planes of these boundary geodesics are pairwise disjoint and exhaust $H^2\setminus D$. To see disjointness, two disjoint boundary lines with overlapping outward half-planes would have one line strictly on the outward side of the other; that line then cannot actually be a boundary of a region lying on the latter's inner side. Exhaustion follows by the first-exit segment argument of step 8.1: after a segment crosses a geodesic outward it cannot cross that same line back, so the endpoint belongs to the outward half-plane. Deck transformations permute the outward half-planes, and their stabilizers are the full primitive cyclic stabilizers of their boundary lines, by step 2.1. Thus every such half-plane quotient is embedded, and in Fermi coordinates is exactly $$r>0,\quad t\bmod L,\quad ds^2=dr^2+\cosh^2r\,dt^2.$$ The metric follows directly from the hyperboloid parametrization around an axis. Its boundary is the corresponding geodesic rim. There is one orbit for each of finitely many geodesic boundary circles of $N$. This proves the full complement/funnel assertion, without an unproved exhaustion theorem for annular ends. [F2, step 2.1, step 8.1, construct, algebra]

10.1 We verify that the convex region just obtained is the ACTUAL convex hull of the limit set. First deepen each assigned cusp, if necessary, so its lifted horoball lies in $C=C(\Lambda_H)$. This follows explicitly, not from a convex-core convention: normalize its primitive translation to $z\mapsto z+\ell$ and choose any finite $a\in\Lambda_H$ besides infinity. Then $a+k\ell\in\Lambda_H$ for every integer $k$. The ideal triangles with vertices $\infty,a+k\ell,a+(k+1)\ell$ lie in $C$ and contain every point of height greater than $\ell/2$ between their bounding vertical lines. Their union therefore contains the full horodisc $y>\ell/2$. Shrinking the cusp tails changes $N$ only by compact collars and does not change $N_\infty$ or its lift $D$. Thus the cusp-tail part of $D$ lies in $C$. [F2, step 8.1, construct, algebra]

11.1 The ideal boundary of $D$ is exactly $\Lambda_H$. Orbit points based at any interior point of $D$ stay in $D$, so every orbit limit belongs to its ideal boundary. Conversely each point of the full lifted compact truncation $N$ lies at uniformly bounded distance from an $H$-orbit: take finitely many compact covering-chart lifts of compact $N$, whose translates cover its full inverse image. Thus any ideal limit of that part is an orbit limit. The remaining cusp-tail part is contained in $C$ by step 10.1. The ideal boundary of $C$ is contained in $\Lambda_H$: any point outside this closed limit set has a small ideal interval disjoint from it, and the opposite closed hyperbolic half-plane contains $C$ while excluding an ideal neighborhood of that point. Hence all ideal limits of $D$ lie in $\Lambda_H$. Since $D$ is closed convex, this already gives $C\subseteq D$. For the reverse, take any complementary ideal interval $I$ of $\Lambda_H$ and its facing open half-plane $P_I$. No boundary line of $D$ enters $P_I$, since both endpoints of each such line are loxodromic fixed points in $\Lambda_H$ and a crossing would require an endpoint in $I$. Connected $P_I$, missing the boundary of $D$, therefore either is outside $D$ or lies wholly inside it. The latter would put $I$ in the ideal boundary of $D$, impossible. Thus $D$ avoids every $P_I$. The complement of their union is exactly the planar convex hull, since it is the intersection of the ideal supporting half-planes. Therefore $D\subseteq C$ and $D=C$. This identifies the geometric core rather than merely constructing some finite-topology convex region. [F2, step 8.1, step 9.1, step 10.1, construct, algebra]

12.1 The core area and end count are explicit. The compact truncation $N$ is homeomorphic to $N_0$, so $\chi(N)=1-h$. Every geodesic rim has geodesic curvature zero. A cusp truncation $y\le T$ has signed boundary geodesic curvature $-1$, length $\ell/T$, and its removed tail has area $\ell/T$, by the upper-half-plane metric. Gauss–Bonnet [F4] gives $$-\operatorname{area}(N)-\sum_{\rm cusps}\ell/T=2\pi(1-h).$$ Adding the tail areas and using step 11.1 proves $\operatorname{area}(C/H)=2\pi(h-1)$. It is positive, so $h\ge2$, also clear from nonelementarity. Capping the $b$ boundary circles of $N$ gives a closed orientable surface of some genus $g_N\ge0$, and $1-h=2-2g_N-b$, as established in the compact-core supplier. Hence $b=h+1-2g_N\le h+1$. These $b$ circles correspond exactly to cusps and funnels. No additional unlisted end remains by step 9.1. [F1, F2, F4, step 7.1, step 9.1, step 11.1, construct, algebra]

13.1 Finally suppose $H$ is an infinite-index subgroup of a cocompact torsion-free Fuchsian group $\Gamma$. The base surface has positive minimum injectivity radius: that radius is positive continuous on the compact quotient by [F2]. A parabolic in $\Gamma$ would have displacements tending to zero along a vertical ray, contradicting that minimum; thus neither $\Gamma$ nor $H$ has parabolics. Its covering $Y$ is noncompact, for a compact covering of a compact base has finite fibers: a fiber is closed and discrete, so compactness makes it finite. Consequently the preceding conclusions apply with no cusp tails, and $C/H=N$ is compact with geodesic boundary. If $H$ has at most $R$ generators, [F1] gives $h\le R$, so its area is at most $2\pi(R-1)$. Trivial and cyclic subgroups are deliberately outside the nonelementary hypothesis and have the explicit plane and loxodromic cylinder models of [F2]; they can be treated separately in an expansion estimate. This proves all stated claims. [F1, F2, step 9.1, step 11.1, step 12.1, given, algebra] ∎
