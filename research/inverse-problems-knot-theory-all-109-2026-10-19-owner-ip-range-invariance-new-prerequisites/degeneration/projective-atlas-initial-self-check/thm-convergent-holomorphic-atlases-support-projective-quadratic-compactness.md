## Statement

Assume AC. Let $X$ be one connected second-countable Riemann surface with a distinguished point, and let $X_n$ be connected second-countable Riemann surfaces. Supply the following convergent holomorphic atlas data. There is a countable coordinate cover $\phi_a:\Delta_a\to X$ by plane discs, and for all sufficiently large $n$ holomorphic embeddings $\phi_{a,n}:\Delta_a\to X_n$. On each relatively compact subset of an overlap, the corresponding transition $\phi_{b,n}^{-1}\phi_{a,n}$ is defined for large $n$ and converges locally uniformly to $\phi_b^{-1}\phi_a$. Require asymptotic separation as well: for every finite family of coordinate closed subdiscs with pairwise disjoint images in $X$, the corresponding images in $X_n$ are pairwise disjoint for all sufficiently large $n$. The distinguished point is represented using one fixed chart and its corresponding points in $X_n$. These are supplied analytic convergence data; compactness or existence of such atlases is not asserted here.

Fix an integer $d\ge1$. If meromorphic maps $f_n:X_n\to\widehat{\mathbb C}$ are either constant or have at most $d$ preimages of each value counted with local multiplicity, a subsequence converges chordally locally uniformly in these charts away from a finite set $E\subset X$, $|E|\le3d$, to a meromorphic map $f:X\to\widehat{\mathbb C}$ or a constant map, including infinity. A nonconstant limit has the same valence bound. If no $f_n$ is identically zero or infinity, there are constants $c_n\in\mathbb C^*$ and a subsequence for which the limit of $c_nf_n$ is neither identically zero nor identically infinity. If every $f_n$ is nonconstant, there are affine postcompositions $a_nf_n+b_n$, $a_n\ne0$, with a nonconstant meromorphic limit. All limits in these assertions are on this one supplied connected pointed component; no simultaneous nonzero normalization on several disconnected geometric limits is claimed.

Now suppose $X_n=\overline X_n\setminus P_n$, where $\overline X_n$ is compact of a fixed genus $g$ and $|P_n|=N$. Let $q_n\ne0$ be holomorphic quadratic differentials on $X_n$ extending meromorphically with at most simple poles at $P_n$. Then there are $\lambda_n\in\mathbb C^*$ and a subsequence with
$$\phi_{a,n}^*(\lambda_nq_n)\longrightarrow\phi_a^*q$$
locally uniformly on every $\Delta_a$, where $q$ is a holomorphic quadratic differential on $X$ that is not identically zero. Thus the entire analytic projective-quadratic limit step follows from the supplied atlas, without a reference cyclic Poincare series or a Fenchel–Nielsen pairing theorem. If $X$ itself has a supplied finite-type compactification, the limit has finite-order meromorphic poles at its punctures; their total order is at most $12g+N+4$. The statement is vacuous for a topological type admitting no such nonzero $q_n$; genus zero and zero-dimensional quadratic spaces are not silently excluded.

This theorem does not prove compactness of the underlying pointed surfaces or covers, their limiting finite-type topology, metric control of thick/thin regions, or faithful transport of quadratic mass. These remain distinct hypotheses or subsequent obligations in a full geometric degeneration alternative.

## Facts & Assumptions

**Given:** AC, exactly the connected pointed atlas, overlap and asymptotic-separation data above; bounded-valence functions for the function conclusions; compact fixed-genus punctured surfaces and the stated nonzero quadratics only for the projective conclusion.

[A1] Full AC is [[def-axiom-of-choice]]; it implies [[def-countable-choice]] by restriction and supplies all subsequence and finite-point label choices below.

[F1] Holomorphic families omitting $0$ and $1$ are chordally normal, with meromorphic or infinite constant limits. [[thm-montel-caratheodory-theorem]], [[def-chordal-local-uniform-convergence-and-meromorphic-normality]]

[F2] A bounded holomorphic function on a punctured disc extends holomorphically. Strict boundary approximation preserves holomorphic zero counts by Rouche. A holomorphic function continuous on a closed disc is bounded there by its boundary maximum; Cauchy formulas give limits and all derivative limits from uniform boundary convergence. [[thm-removable-singularity-characterizations]], [[thm-rouche-theorem]], [[thm-boundary-maximum-modulus-principle]], [[thm-cauchy-integral-formula-higher-derivatives]]

[F3] Nonconstant holomorphic functions are open maps. [[thm-open-mapping-theorem-holomorphic-functions]]

[F4] The compact Riemann–Roch formula is $\ell(D)-\ell(K-D)=\deg D+1-g$, with $\ell(0)=1$, $\ell(K)=g$ and a nonzero meromorphic differential available; a principal divisor has degree zero. [[thm-riemann-roch-compact-riemann-surfaces]], [[def-divisor-principal-and-canonical-divisor-riemann-surface]]

[F5] A nonconstant meromorphic function on a compact connected Riemann surface is a proper holomorphic map to the sphere, whose degree is its total pole order and bounds every fiber with multiplicity. [[thm-proper-holomorphic-map-riemann-surfaces-has-degree]], [[def-divisor-principal-and-canonical-divisor-riemann-surface]]

## Proof

1.1 We first make finite moving-point extraction precise in the supplied atlas. Consider at most $L$ points in each $X_n$, allowing missing labels. Choose arbitrary labels $1,\ldots,L$ and pass to subsequences successively for their trajectories. A trajectory either visits the images of some fixed compact coordinate subdisc infinitely often, when compactness gives a subsequence converging in its coordinate to a point of $X$, or visits each such compact image only finitely often, when a diagonal choice over their countable exhaustion makes it escape every compact coordinate set. These alternatives also cover missing labels. A point trajectory cannot have two distinct limits in $X$: choose disjoint closed coordinate discs about the two alleged limits; asymptotic separation forbids the same point from lying in both images eventually. Overlap transition convergence identifies two coordinate limits at the same point. After at most $L$ subsequence choices every label therefore has one limit point or escapes. Denote their finite limit set by $E$. For any compact coordinate set disjoint from $E$, all moving points avoid its image for large $n$. Otherwise a fixed label occurs there infinitely often, and a coordinate subsequence gives a limit in that compact set, contradicting either its chosen different limit or its escape. [A1, given, construct, algebra]

2.1 If infinitely many $f_n$ are constant, choose that subsequence and a sphere-convergent subsequence of their values; it gives the stated constant limit. Otherwise discard finitely many constants. Apply step 1.1 to all preimages of $0,1,\infty$, at most $3d$ points; ignoring multiplicities only decreases their number. On every coordinate subdisc compactly contained in $X\setminus E$, the maps are eventually holomorphic and omit $0,1$, because they avoid all three exceptional values there. By [F1] and a diagonal subsequence on a countable cover, their expressions converge chordally locally uniformly to meromorphic functions or infinite constants. The limits agree under the limiting transition maps. Indeed the original expressions agree under the varying transitions, which converge on compact overlaps by hypothesis; local uniform convergence to a continuous sphere-valued map allows its composition with those varying arguments to pass to the limit. Thus they glue on $X\setminus E$. Removing finitely many points from a connected surface leaves it connected: join two points by a path and reroute that path through small punctured coordinate discs about the finitely many removed points. The glued limit is consequently meromorphic everywhere on $X\setminus E$, or is one constant including infinity. If an infinite-constant chart met a nonconstant meromorphic chart, the latter would equal infinity on an open set and hence be identically infinity by its local power-series uniqueness; connectedness rules out a mixture. [A1, F1, step 1.1, given, construct, algebra]

3.1 A nonconstant limit on $X\setminus E$ has at most $d$ preimages of any value there, counted with multiplicity. Otherwise choose finitely many disjoint small coordinate discs about preimages whose multiplicities sum to more than $d$. Make a fixed target Mobius change taking that value to zero and leaving the limit finite on all these closed discs. Chordal convergence there is Euclidean uniform, since the limit stays a positive distance from infinity. For late $n$ the varying functions also have no pole on those closed discs. Rouche [F2] gives at least the same number of zeros in the corresponding discs of $X_n$, which are disjoint by asymptotic separation. That contradicts their global valence bound. This argument also covers the target value infinity by the initial target change. [F2, step 2.1, given, algebra]

3.2 Such a limit extends meromorphically across each $e\in E$, with no essential singularity hidden. It has at most $d$ poles on $X\setminus E$ by step 3.1, so shrink a punctured coordinate disc about $e$ to exclude all of them; its coefficient is holomorphic there. If it did not extend meromorphically, its image on every smaller punctured disc would be dense in the finite plane. For if it avoided a disc about $a$, the bounded function $1/(f-a)$ would extend by [F2]; a nonzero value at the center would give a finite extension of $f$, and a finite-order zero would give a pole. The extension cannot vanish identically since its values off the center are nonzero. This proves the density assertion directly, without Picard. Density and openness [F3] now contradict bounded valence: choose a small source disc away from the center with nonempty open image; successively choose $d$ more disjoint source discs nearer the center whose images meet the preceding common open image. At each step openness gives a nonempty open intersection, and density in arbitrarily small punctured discs allows the next choice. A value in the final common image has at least $d+1$ preimages, contrary to step 3.1. Thus all the singularities extend meromorphically. Constant limits extend as constants. The extended nonconstant map still has valence at most $d$: for values outside the finite set $f(E)$ the preceding bound applies; a fiber of an exceptional value, with its local multiplicities, is approximated by fibers of nearby values avoiding $f(E)$ using Rouche on disjoint coordinate circles. The same bound follows for it. [F2, F3, step 3.1, algebra]

4.1 Suppose now no $f_n$ is identically zero or infinity. If infinitely many are constants, normalize them to one and the claim follows. Otherwise first extract the trajectories of their zeros and poles, at most $2d$, by step 1.1. Choose $x\in X$ outside that finite limit set and one compact coordinate disc about $x$ avoiding it. Eventually $f_n$ is holomorphic and has no zeros on the corresponding whole closed disc. Write $x_n$ for its corresponding coordinate point, and set $c_n=1/f_n(x_n)\in\mathbb C^*$. Apply steps 2.1 and 3.2 to $g_n=c_nf_n$, obtaining a meromorphic or constant limit off a finite exceptional set. That limit is not identically zero: choose a small coordinate circle around $x$ in the zero/pole-free disc and avoiding the finite exceptional set. If the limit were zero, $g_n$ would tend uniformly to zero on this circle. The boundary maximum principle [F2], since all $g_n$ are holomorphic inside, would give $g_n(x_n)\to0$, contradicting $g_n(x_n)=1$. If the limit were infinity, apply exactly the same argument to $1/g_n$, holomorphic on that disc since it is zero-free. Hence its meromorphic limit is neither identically zero nor identically infinity, as asserted. This supplies the normalization proof even when its normalized point is itself an exceptional preimage of one. [A1, F2, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 3.2, given, construct, algebra]

5.1 If all $f_n$ are nonconstant, choose $x_0$ outside the limit set of their at most $d$ poles, extracted by step 1.1. A fixed closed coordinate disc about $x_0$ is pole-free for large $n$. Put $h_n=f_n-f_n(x_{0,n})$. These functions are nonzero, have the same poles as $f_n$, have valence at most $d$, and vanish at $x_{0,n}$. Apply step 4.1 to normalize $a_nh_n$ to a limit $h$ neither identically zero nor infinity. The limit is holomorphic across a neighborhood of $x_0$ and has value zero there. Here is the full extension justification. Choose a coordinate circle around $x_0$ avoiding its finite exceptional set and any poles of $h$. Uniform convergence on that circle is Euclidean, and the functions $a_nh_n$ have no poles inside it. Cauchy's formula therefore gives uniform convergence on each smaller disc to a holomorphic function with value zero at $x_0$; it agrees with $h$ on the complement of the exceptional points and extends it. Thus $h(x_0)=0$. It cannot be a nonzero constant, and its identically zero or infinite possibilities were already excluded. Therefore it is nonconstant. Writing $b_n=-a_nf_n(x_{0,n})$ gives the required affine postcompositions. This argument uses an actual persistent pole-free neighborhood, not evaluation convergence at an exceptional point assumed without proof. [A1, F2, step 1.1, step 4.1, given, construct, algebra]

6.1 We next produce bounded-degree functions for the quadratic application from the actual compact Riemann–Roch formula. On any compact genus-$g$ surface choose one point $p$. With $D=(g+1)[p]$, [F4] gives $\ell(D)\ge\deg D+1-g=2$. Constants form only a one-dimensional subspace, so choose a nonconstant meromorphic $f\in L(D)$. It has only a pole at $p$, of order at most $g+1$, and [F5] gives degree at most $d=g+1$. Choose such functions on all $\overline X_n$ using [A1] and restrict to $X_n$. They remain nonconstant with that global valence bound. Step 5.1 provides affine postcompositions, still denoted $f_n$, converging away from a finite exceptional set to a nonconstant meromorphic $f$ on the one connected supplied component $X$. On compact coordinate subsets avoiding that exceptional set and the at most $d$ poles of $f$, convergence is Euclidean and [F2] gives derivative convergence. Hence $$\theta_n=(df_n)^2\longrightarrow\theta=(df)^2$$ there as meromorphic quadratic coefficients. The limit is not identically zero: a holomorphic function with derivative zero is locally constant by its power series, and connectedness would make the nonconstant meromorphic $f$ constant. This supplies a nonzero meromorphic reference limit without cyclic Poincare series or Wolpert independence. [A1, F2, F4, F5, step 3.2, step 5.1, given, construct, algebra]

6.2 We prove the requisite uniform ratio-degree bound. Formula [F4] with $D=0$ gives $\ell(K)=g$, and with $D=K$ gives $g-1=\deg K+1-g$, hence $\deg K=2g-2$. The divisor of a nonzero meromorphic differential has this degree, since all canonical divisors differ by principal divisors of degree zero. Therefore $\deg(\theta_n)=4g-4$. At a pole of $f_n$ of order $k$, $df_n$ has pole order exactly $k+1$, by differentiating its first nonzero Laurent term. Its square has pole order $2(k+1)$. Since the total pole order of $f_n$ is at most $d$ and the number of its poles is at most $d$, the total pole order of $\theta_n$ is at most $4d$. Its total zero order is consequently at most $4g-4+4d\le8g$. The nonzero function $r_n=q_n/\theta_n$ is meromorphic on $\overline X_n$. Its poles can occur only at zeros of $\theta_n$ or the at most $N$ simple marked poles of $q_n$, and their total order is at most $8g+N$. By [F5], $r_n$ is constant or has valence at most $D=\max(1,8g+N)$. The affine scalings and translations used for $f_n$ change no divisor bound. These statements include genus zero; if the assumptions require a nonzero quadratic in a zero-dimensional space there simply is no such sequence. [F4, F5, step 6.1, given, algebra]

7.1 Apply step 4.1 to $r_n$, choosing $\lambda_n\in\mathbb C^*$ and a subsequence with $\lambda_nr_n\to r$, where $r$ is neither identically zero nor infinity. Retain the already chosen subsequence for $f_n$. On the complement of finitely many points—the two exceptional sets and poles of $f$ and $r$—we have locally Euclidean uniform convergence of the functions and of $df_n$, by step 6.1. Thus $$\lambda_nq_n=(\lambda_nr_n)(df_n)^2\longrightarrow r(df)^2$$ locally uniformly there as quadratic coefficients. This limit is nonzero and meromorphic. It actually extends holomorphically across every omitted point and convergence holds on its full neighborhood. Choose a coordinate circle enclosing just that omitted point and avoiding the other finite exceptional points. On the circle the quadratic coefficients converge uniformly and are finite. Each coefficient $\phi_{a,n}^*(\lambda_nq_n)$ is holomorphic on the whole enclosed disc, since the chart maps into $X_n$, not its removed punctures. Cauchy's formula [F2] therefore gives uniform convergence on every smaller disc to a holomorphic coefficient, agreeing with $r(df)^2$ away from the center. Repeating at all omitted points gives a holomorphic quadratic $q$ on all of $X$, with full local uniform convergence in every chart. Coordinate compatibility follows either from the already global meromorphic expression $r(df)^2$, or by passing the varying transition identities to the limit; transition derivatives converge by Cauchy. Nonzeroness persists because the meromorphic factors $r$ and $df$ were not identically zero. This proves the claimed projective quadratic compactness on the supplied connected atlas. [F2, step 3.2, step 4.1, step 6.1, step 6.2, construct, algebra]

8.1 If $X$ has a supplied finite-type compactification, bounded-valence meromorphic $f$ and $r$ extend meromorphically across each puncture by exactly the punctured-disc argument of step 3.2. Their valence bounds remain $d$ and $D$ by its nearby-fiber argument. As maps of the compactification, [F5] then gives total pole orders at most $d$ and $D$; a nonzero constant $r$ has no poles. The square $(df)^2$ has total pole order at most $4d$, by the computation in step 6.2. Consequently $q=r(df)^2$ has total pole order at most $D+4d$. When a nonzero $q_n$ exists, $8g+N\ge1$ except for compact genus zero, where it cannot exist. Hence $D+4d\le12g+N+4$. The apparent poles at points of $X$ have already been removed in step 7.1, so any remaining poles are at the supplied punctures, of finite order as claimed. No assertion that a geometric limit necessarily has this compactification, or that one scaling is nonzero on other disconnected limiting components, has been used. [F2, F5, step 3.2, step 6.1, step 6.2, step 7.1, given, algebra] ∎
