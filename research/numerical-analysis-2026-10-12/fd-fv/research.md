# Finite differences and finite volumes for PDEs

This dossier covers the linear finite-difference error theory for elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic problems, and the nonlinear finite-volume theory for scalar conservation laws. It separates the linear Lax equivalence theorem from nonlinear entropy convergence, and distinguishes a CFL condition from a complete stability or convergence proof. It is source research for a later scaffold; it does not author production library items.

## Sources and what they establish

The main proof-bearing finite-difference source is Endre Süli's complete Oxford note, *B6.1 Numerical Solution of Partial Differential Equations* (2025), all 110 PDF pages read. Its sections 1–2 (PDF pp. 1–12) set out continuous, (L^p), Sobolev and weak elliptic spaces. Section 3 (pp. 13–53) develops the finite-difference framework and elliptic boundary-value examples. Sections 4–5 (pp. 54–75) cover heat schemes; Section 6 (pp. 76–110) covers waves, linear first-order hyperbolic equations, their boundaries, and a brief nonlinear pointwise estimate. Its §3 Theorem 6 is an **elliptic** boundary-scheme consistency-plus-stability error result, not the Lax–Richtmyer IVP equivalence theorem. It does not supply nonlinear finite-volume entropy convergence.

For the exact Lax–Richtmyer statement and standard hypotheses, I read all of Lloyd Trefethen's author-hosted Chapter 4, *Accuracy, Stability, and Convergence* (40 PDF pages; printed pp. 147–185). It gives the abstract Banach-space setup, same-norm stability/consistency/convergence definitions, and Theorem 4.1, and sharply warns that the theorem is linear. It also explicitly labels the proof “Not yet written” and says the section omits essential rigor around applying the differential operator on a dense subspace and relating continuous to discrete norms. Accordingly, the theorem contract below is recorded, but the source is not misrepresented as giving a complete proof. The proof route can be filled in under a common-Banach-space formulation using the local published uniform boundedness principle for the converse and a Duhamel error recursion for stability-to-convergence; a concrete mesh implementation still needs its embedding and norm-comparison interface. ## Linear finite-difference setting and exact concepts

For a mesh parameter (h\downarrow0), a discrete state is a grid function on a stated grid (G_h), or a vector in a finite-dimensional space (X_h). A weighted grid norm must be specified; on a uniform one-dimensional grid a standard choice is
\[
\|V\|_{p,h}=\left(h\sum_j|V_j|^p\right)^{1/p},\quad 1\le p<\infty,
\qquad \|V\|_{\infty,h}=\max_j|V_j|.
\]
The approximation map (I_h) (point sampling in a smooth setting, or cell averaging when an integral formulation is used) and the reconstruction used to compare with the PDE solution must be named. Boundary nodes, ghost cells, periodic identification, and inflow data are part of the scheme, not afterthoughts.

For a differential operator (L) and its mesh operator (L_h), the local residual on a smooth exact solution is (\tau_h=L_h I_hu-I_hLu), with sign depending on convention. A scheme is consistent of spatial order (p) when this residual is (O(h^p)) in the stated norm for the required smoothness class. Fully discrete schemes also have temporal residuals; for a smooth solution a typical order statement is (O(h^p+\Delta t^q)). Boundary residuals must be stated separately or included in a boundary norm. A Taylor expansion proves such an order only if the needed derivatives exist and the grid/boundary closure has the asserted order. Pointwise smooth consistency says nothing by itself about convergence for rough or discontinuous solutions.

For a linear initial-boundary-value scheme, stability is a bound on its discrete solution operator, uniform in refinement on each fixed time interval and in the same norm in which consistency is measured. It must control initial perturbations and forcing or boundary perturbations as they enter the scheme. For a two-level scheme written (P_hU^{n+1}=Q_hU^n+\Delta t\,g_h^n), it includes the mesh-uniform bounds for the propagators built from (P_h^{-1}Q_h), together with the appropriate forcing map. A spectral-radius statement at one mesh or one time step is not this uniform stability estimate.

The reusable linear error argument is direct. Subtract the exact sampled solution from the numerical update to obtain an error recursion with a forcing term equal to the truncation residual. Apply the uniform stability estimate to the initial error and the accumulated residuals. Thus consistency plus stability yields convergence and, when the two have orders, an error estimate. For a stationary elliptic problem, the analogous residual equation is (A_he_h=-\tau_h); a uniform bound on (A_h^{-1}) in the comparison norm gives the error estimate once the boundary residual is included. This elliptic argument is a direct residual estimate; it should not be called the full Lax–Richtmyer equivalence theorem.

The precise abstract Lax–Richtmyer contract reported by Trefethen is this. Let (B) be a Banach space with norm \(\|\cdot\|\). The well-posed linear IVP is (u_t=Au) on (0\le t\le T), with (u(0)=u_0\in B), a unique solution, and continuous dependence on every initial datum. Each time step (k\downarrow0) fixes a spatial mesh (h=h(k)) and a bounded linear one-step operator (S_k:B\to B), with (v^{n+1}=S_kv^n). Order (p>0) consistency means
\[
\|u(t+k)-S_ku(t)\|=O(k^{p+1})
\]
for sufficiently smooth exact solutions. Convergence means (S_k^nu_0\to u(t)) for every (u_0\in B) at fixed finite times as (k\to0) and (nk\to t). Stability means (\|S_k^n\|\le C_T) uniformly for (0\le nk\le T), with (C_T) independent of (k,n). The same norm is used in all three definitions. The theorem states convergence iff stability for a consistent approximation to that well-posed linear IVP. Its standard formulation allows multiple dimensions, implicit formulas, boundary conditions and variable coefficients; multistep/vector methods are handled by one-step vector augmentation. It does **not** cover nonlinear schemes.

The standard proof route has two halves. For stability to convergence, on a smooth dense class write
\[
e_n=S_k^nu_0-u(t_n)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}S_k^{n-1-j}\bigl(S_ku(t_j)-u(t_{j+1})\bigr).
\]
Uniform stability bounds the propagators and consistency makes each residual (o(k)) (or (O(k^{p+1}))); summing (O(1/k)) terms gives an error tending to zero (or (O(k^p))). Approximate general initial data by the smooth dense class and use stability plus continuum continuous dependence. For convergence to stability, convergence for every (u_0\in B) makes the family \(\{S_k^n:0\le nk\le T\}\) pointwise bounded, because each continuum orbit is bounded on the compact interval. The published Banach-space uniform boundedness principle (`items/thm-uniform-boundedness-principle.md`) then gives a uniform operator-norm bound. This proof needs all mesh operators to act on one common Banach space; if the implementation uses varying (X_h), define projections/reconstructions or a common embedding and prove the required norm comparison before applying the theorem. This fills a transparent proof route, not the gaps in Trefethen's chapter. Do not remove well-posedness, density/consistency, compatibility, norm matching or uniform-in-(h) finite-time stability.

### Elliptic schemes

For a Dirichlet elliptic problem, a standard five-point grid Laplacian illustrates the method. Discrete summation by parts identifies the grid inner product of (-\Delta_hV) with a sum of squared nearest-neighbour differences when the boundary closure is compatible. A discrete Poincare inequality then yields coercivity, uniqueness and an inverse bound in the discrete energy norm. A discrete maximum principle can instead give max-norm stability when the stencil has the relevant sign pattern/diagonal dominance and boundary closure. Variable-coefficient diffusion should be discretized in flux/divergence form when preserving symmetry and coercivity matters; expanding coefficients and applying centered differences independently can lose that structure.

Boundary consistency is a separate proof obligation. A formally second-order interior stencil may give lower global order if a Neumann or Dirichlet closure is only first order or if corner/boundary regularity limits the continuous solution. For weak solutions with limited regularity, pointwise Taylor residuals may be unavailable; use an integral/weak formulation or a separately justified approximation argument. The Oxford note treats smooth classical solutions and a particular rough-data route, but cites Bramble–Hilbert rather than proving it.

### Parabolic and hyperbolic stability

For constant-coefficient problems on periodic or infinite uniform grids, von Neumann analysis substitutes a Fourier mode (e^{ij\theta}) and bounds the amplification symbol. It is a modewise stability test in that setting. For periodic normal amplification operators it can yield the corresponding (\ell^2) estimate. It does not by itself prove stability for finite boundaries, variable coefficients, or non-normal amplification matrices; an energy estimate, matrix-power bound, or boundary stability analysis is then needed.

The heat-equation forward-time centered-space scheme has (\mu=\kappa\Delta t/h^2\le1/2) in one space dimension for the standard Fourier/max-principle stability condition. In (d) dimensions with spacings (h_i), the standard explicit condition is (\Delta t\sum_i\kappa_i/h_i^2\le1/2). Backward Euler is unconditionally (L^2)-stable. For the theta method, (\theta\ge1/2) gives unconditional (L^2) stability under the note's setup; Crank–Nicolson is second order in time but does not damp the highest modes as strongly as backward Euler. An (L^2) stability claim does not imply a discrete maximum principle: the latter can require the stricter restriction ((1-\theta)\mu\le1/2).

For the centered explicit wave scheme, the one-dimensional bound is (c\Delta t/h\le1); a discrete energy mimics the continuous energy after summation by parts, and the CFL bound enters the positivity of that discrete energy. Multidimensional bounds depend on the stencil and mesh. Hyperbolic CFL conditions compare the numerical domain of dependence to the PDE characteristic speeds. For local explicit schemes this is a necessary domain-of-dependence constraint, but a CFL inequality alone is not sufficient stability for every discretization or boundary closure. The actual sufficient condition must be derived for the scheme and geometry.

For first-order advection, boundary data enter only at inflow boundaries (where characteristics enter the domain). Prescribing data at outflow without a separate well-posed formulation can overdetermine the PDE. The discrete energy/flux balance must account for boundary terms. Upwind methods are one-sided in the direction of transport; centered methods can be unstable for pure advection despite a consistent Taylor expansion.

## Scalar conservation laws and finite volumes

Fix (u_t+f(u)_x=0). A finite-volume cell state (U_i^n) approximates the average of (u) on a cell. Integrating the conservation law over the space-time cell gives the conservative update
\[
U_i^{n+1}=U_i^n-\lambda\bigl(F(U_i^n,U_{i+1}^n)-F(U_{i-1}^n,U_i^n)\bigr),
\qquad \lambda=\Delta t/\Delta x,
\]
where (F(a,b)) is a numerical flux satisfying consistency (F(u,u)=f(u)). Shared interface fluxes cancel when summed over cells, giving exact local conservation. This discrete conservation law is the mechanism that can retain the correct Rankine–Hugoniot shock speed; conservation alone does not choose the entropy solution.

A scalar numerical flux is monotone when (F) is nondecreasing in its first argument and nonincreasing in its second. Under the explicit CFL condition that makes each update map nondecreasing (for differentiable fluxes, (\lambda(\partial_1F(a,b)-\partial_2F(c,a))\le1) for all states; a global Lipschitz sufficient bound is (\lambda(L_1+L_2)\le1)), the update preserves order and invariant intervals and satisfies an (L^\infty) bound. Standard examples are Godunov, Lax–Friedrichs/Rusanov and Engquist–Osher fluxes. The exact CFL bound is flux- and scheme-dependent.

For the Kruzhkov entropy (\eta_k(u)=|u-k|), the physical entropy flux is (q_k(u)=\operatorname{sgn}(u-k)(f(u)-f(k))). A monotone consistent numerical flux gives a discrete entropy flux, for example
\[
Q_k(a,b)=F(a\vee k,b\vee k)-F(a\wedge k,b\wedge k),
\]
which is consistent with (q_k). The fully discrete entropy inequality is proved from monotonicity of the update; it is not a consequence of linear von Neumann stability. A first-order monotone method is generally diffusive. Godunov's barrier says a **linear** monotonicity-preserving scheme cannot have order above one. High-order practice therefore uses nonlinear limiters/reconstruction such as TVD/ENO/WENO together with time integrators preserving a convex stability property (SSP), and must prove its own invariant/entropy bounds.

The standard BV convergence route is:

1. Consistency plus monotonicity and a CFL condition yield a uniform range bound. For (u_0\in BV), monotonicity/translation invariance yields total-variation control (TVD) and a temporal-translation estimate for the reconstructed functions.
2. Helly/BV compactness, or an equivalent translation-compactness theorem with tail control, gives a strongly (L^1_{\mathrm{loc}})-convergent subsequence. On (\mathbb R), global (L^1) convergence also needs uniform tail control; boundedness alone does not supply it.
3. The conservative update and consistent flux pass to the weak conservation law in the limit by summing over cells and testing. Monotonicity supplies a discrete entropy inequality; its limit is the Kruzhkov entropy inequality.
4. Entropy uniqueness identifies every subsequential limit with the same continuum solution, so the whole family converges strongly (locally (L^1); globally (L^1) when the global integrability/tail assumptions apply).

The ETH theorem's exact setup includes (f\in C^1), (u_0\in BV(\mathbb R)), a consistent conservative monotone two-point flux that is locally Lipschitz, the stated CFL condition, and (\Delta t/\Delta x\ge c>0) along the family. Its proof constructs a piecewise-linear-in-time interpolant. The time-step lower bound prevents the chosen temporal interpolation/translation estimates from degenerating; it is not a universal condition for every FV convergence theorem. Droniou's full BV route is a second treatment. Droniou's no-BV section is a route sketch only; it invokes entropy-process/Young-measure machinery and is not a self-contained proof.

The **Lax–Wendroff limit theorem** must not be conflated with Lax–Richtmyer equivalence. The former says, under a conservative and consistent scheme plus a suitable boundedness/compactness or strong-convergence hypothesis, a convergent approximation has a weak solution as its limit. It does not select an entropy solution. The Waterloo chapter states a loose version without the compactness assumptions or proof, so the precise theorem and proof are a source gap for this dossier: a later author should either give a complete theorem with explicit hypotheses from a full source or prove the needed special case directly. To get entropy convergence, separately establish a discrete entropy inequality and invoke entropy uniqueness. Conservation plus linear stability cannot fill these steps.

For nonlinear systems the scalar monotonicity argument generally does not transfer: systems do not have a scalar order structure with the same contraction theory. ETH treats entropy pairs and local Riemann solvers under strict hyperbolicity and the condition that every field is genuinely nonlinear or linearly degenerate, for sufficiently small Riemann data. Those are local system results. No global convergence theorem for arbitrary nonlinear systems is claimed here.

## Splitting, discrete energy, and boundaries

For (u_t=(A+B)u), Lie splitting applies the (A)-flow and then the (B)-flow in each step. For sufficiently regular finite-dimensional or bounded operators, the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff expansion has leading local error (\tfrac12\Delta t^2[A,B]), giving first-order global accuracy on a fixed interval. Strang splitting (e^{\Delta tA/2}e^{\Delta tB}e^{\Delta tA/2}) cancels the second-order term; its leading local error uses nested commutators such as ([A,[A,B]]) and ([B,[B,A]]), giving second-order global accuracy with stronger regularity. If (A) and (B) commute, the flows split exactly. For unbounded PDE operators, the commutators may not be defined on all states; domain invariance, solution regularity, and stability are additional proof obligations. MIT lecture 20 supplies the introductory expansion, not a blanket unbounded-operator theorem.

Discrete energy proofs mimic integration by parts. One states the grid inner product, proves the summation-by-parts identity including boundary terms, identifies the discrete energy, then proves positivity/coercivity and tracks the time increment. Boundary stencils and data determine whether the boundary term is dissipative, zero, or uncontrolled. Continuous heat/wave energy theorems in the local PDE library are useful model suppliers, but do not establish any discrete identity.

Boundary conditions belong in the definition of consistency and stability. Elliptic schemes require a closure for each boundary condition and a compatible discrete uniqueness/stability argument. Parabolic schemes must account for initial-boundary compatibility when high-order regularity is used. Hyperbolic problems prescribe data only for incoming characteristic fields. Finite-volume boundaries also require numerical boundary fluxes whose consistency, entropy behavior and stability are checked; ghost-cell recipes alone do not prove the corresponding bound.

## Research boundaries to preserve in the scaffold

- Keep the linear Lax–Richtmyer equivalence theorem separate from the nonlinear Lax–Wendroff weak-limit theorem.
- State exact mesh, norm, boundary closure, consistency, stability, regularity and initial-data assumptions before claiming an error bound.
- A CFL inequality is not a universal sufficiency theorem; show the scheme-specific monotonicity, energy or Fourier estimate.
- Pointwise smooth-solution truncation error does not imply convergence through a shock.
- Linear stability of a nonlinear flux discretization does not imply entropy convergence.
- Conservative consistency gives the weak equation only after compactness/limit passage; entropy admissibility requires a discrete entropy argument and continuum uniqueness.
- A scalar monotone-flux theorem does not license a theorem for systems.
- For splitting, formal commutator order and a rigorous PDE order estimate are distinct claims.
- Include boundary rows/fluxes in stability and consistency rather than treating only the interior stencil.
- Generic Navier–Stokes formulation is established material; the precise unresolved 3D regularity question is a separate claim and not a logical dependency.
