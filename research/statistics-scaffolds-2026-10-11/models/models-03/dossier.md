# Models-03 source dossier: causal inference, missing data, and measurement error

**Status:** research-complete for the focused units listed below; scaffold proposals only. This dossier does not author library pages or certify any future gates.

## Scope and mathematical stance

The proposed models-03 material defines scientific targets before methods. A causal estimand is a functional of a specified full-data causal model; identification asks whether the observed-data law plus explicit assumptions determine that functional. Identification is a logical property of a model class, not empirical plausibility. In particular, conditional exchangeability, many DAG restrictions, instrumental exclusion, missing-at-random, and measurement-error laws are usually substantive assumptions that the observed distribution cannot verify. Positivity, some implications of a proposed IV model, and some other restrictions can have observable implications, but passing those checks does not establish the full causal model.

Use a target population, treatment versions, time order, outcome, contrast, and interference scope in every statement. State consistency and any no-interference restriction separately. For an intervention $a$, $Y(a)$ denotes the outcome under the specified treatment intervention. Consistency says $Y=Y(A)$ for the received treatment; it does not itself say treatment assignment is unconfounded. The ATE is $E\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}$, when that target is appropriate. Generally the joint distribution of an individual's $Y(1),Y(0)$, and hence their individual effect, is not identified by observing one treatment per unit.

An observed-data functional $\psi(P_{\mathrm{obs}})$ identifies a target $\Psi(P_{\mathrm{full}})$ relative to assumptions $\mathcal A$ if every full-data law compatible with $P_{\mathrm{obs}}$ and $\mathcal A$ gives the same value of $\Psi$. If compatible laws give different target values, it is not point identified; partial identification reports the set or bounds of compatible values. A formula is not an identification theorem until its assumptions, target population, support, and causal model are specified.

### Pair 1 — potential outcomes, estimands, and identification

- **A:** `def-potential-outcome-model-and-causal-estimand`; `def-causal-identification-and-partial-identification`; `thm-identification-by-consistency-exchangeability-and-positivity`.
- **B:** `ex-potential-outcomes-treatment-versions-and-confounding`.
- **Purpose and definitions:** Define potential outcomes $Y(a)$, observed outcome $Y$, target-population ATE/ATT/CATE, consistency, conditional exchangeability, positivity, and point versus partial identification. Make treatment-version and target-population restrictions explicit. In the single-treatment setup, do not imply that $Y(1)-Y(0)$ is individually observed.
- **Result and hypotheses:** For discrete $A$, if consistency holds, $Y(a)\perp A\mid L$, and $P(A=a\mid L)>0$ almost surely on the target population's covariate support, then
  \[
  E\{Y(a)\}=\sum_l E(Y\mid A=a,L=l)P(L=l),
  \]
  with the corresponding integral for general $L$. For the ATE subtract the two identified means; conditional versions require the matching conditional support condition. If any assumption fails, this formula is not established by this theorem.
- **Proof route:** Consistency identifies the conditional law of $Y(a)$ among units with $A=a,L=l$. Conditional exchangeability transports that law to the target $L=l$ stratum. Positivity makes the observed conditional law available on the target support. Average over the target covariate distribution. For nonidentification, exhibit two compatible full-data laws with the same observed law but different target values.
- **B examples/counterexamples:** Randomized assignment; confounding despite consistency; treatment versions that invalidate a single $a$; a binary-outcome example where the observed law leaves the individual effect and, without exchangeability, the ATE undetermined.
- **Source anchors:** Hernán–Robins (H&R), ch. 1 §§1.1–1.5 and ch. 3 §§3.1–3.6, printed pp. 3–12, 27–45, especially the identifiability definition and Technical Points 3.1–3.2; Kang, “Identification Under Unmeasured Confounding,” opening notation and strong-ignorability review, extracted lines 36–119.
- **Limits:** Exchangeability is not diagnosed by balance on measured covariates; no observed-data test rules out unmeasured confounding. Positivity must be stated almost surely relative to the target support, not as an informal universal claim at impossible covariate values.

### Pair 2 — structural causal models, DAGs, and interventions

- **A:** `def-structural-causal-model-and-causal-dag`; `def-do-intervention-on-an-scm`; `thm-backdoor-adjustment`; `thm-frontdoor-identification`.
- **B:** `ex-confounding-collider-frontdoor-and-mutilated-dag`.
- **Purpose and definitions:** A structural causal model consists of assignments $V_j=f_j(\operatorname{pa}_j,U_j)$ and a graph of direct parent relations. A $do(A=a)$ intervention replaces the structural assignment for $A$, rather than conditioning on observed $A=a$. Define directed paths, backdoor paths, d-separation, and the Markov property. Distinguish an assumed missing edge from observed conditional independence; faithfulness is an additional restriction used when learning graph structure from independences, not a consequence of the DAG Markov property.
- **Results and hypotheses:** Under the causal Markov factorization and a valid backdoor set $L$ (no descendants of $A$, and blocking every path from $A$ to $Y$ that starts with an arrow into $A$), the adjustment formula identifies $P(Y\mid do(A=a))=\sum_l P(Y\mid A=a,L=l)P(L=l)$ (with an integral for continuous $L$). Under the frontdoor conditions—all directed $A\to Y$ paths pass through $M$, no open backdoor path from $A$ to $M$, and every backdoor path from $M$ to $Y$ is blocked by $A$—the frontdoor functional $P(Y\mid do(A=a))=\sum_m P(m\mid A=a)\sum_{a'}P(Y\mid m,a')P(a')$ identifies the effect (with analogous integrals for continuous variables). State the Markovian independent-error model used for the simple truncated factorization; do not apply it to latent-confounded graphs without an appropriate identification argument.
- **Proof route:** In the intervened SCM remove the equation for $A$, derive the truncated factorization for the remaining variables under independent exogenous errors, and sum over the adjustment set. For frontdoor, apply the two justified adjustments in sequence: identify the mediator distribution under intervention and the mediator-to-outcome causal response after conditioning on treatment, then integrate. For d-separation implications use the graph factorization; do not invoke faithfulness unless reversing an independence implication.
- **B examples/counterexamples:** A fork (confounding), collider conditioning (opened path), mediator adjustment (changes the total-effect target), a valid backdoor set, and a frontdoor graph with latent $A,Y$ confounding.
- **Source anchors:** Pearl, §§3.1–3.3, PDF pp. 24–36 / journal pp. 119–131, extracted lines 1244–1829; H&R ch. 6 §§6.1–6.6 and ch. 7 §§7.1–7.6, printed pp. 75–107.
- **Limits:** A DAG records causal assumptions, not merely correlations. The Markov property says graph separation implies independence; faithfulness is a separate converse-type condition. Adjustment criteria are sufficient for their displayed functionals and do not guarantee the graph itself is correct.

### Pair 3 — standardization and inverse-probability identification

- **A:** `alg-standardization-for-a-causal-mean`; `alg-inverse-probability-weighting-for-a-causal-mean`; `thm-standardization-identifies-a-causal-mean`; `thm-inverse-probability-weighting-identifies-a-causal-mean`.
- **B:** `ex-standardization-and-weights-with-overlap-failure`.
- **Purpose and definitions:** Define the standardization functional and treatment weight $I(A=a)/P(A=a\mid L)$ for a discrete treatment. Keep the formula/algorithm definition separate from the theorem proving identification. For continuous treatment, require a density-based formulation and its support conditions; do not silently reuse discrete probabilities.
- **Results and hypotheses:** Under Pair 1 assumptions, standardization equals $E\{Y(a)\}$, and
  \[
  E\!\left[\frac{I(A=a)Y}{P(A=a\mid L)}\right]=E\{Y(a)\}.
  \]
  These are population identities, not claims about finite-sample stability or estimator asymptotics. An estimated propensity score is a separate estimation question.
- **Proof route:** Standardization follows from the iterated conditional-expectation argument for Pair 1. For the weighted identity, condition on $L$ and use $E[I(A=a)Y\mid L]=P(A=a\mid L)E[Y\mid A=a,L]$. Cancel the propensity on its positive support, then average over $L$. The Radon–Nikodym change-of-measure view is an optional measure-theoretic formulation, not a new assumption.
- **B examples/counterexamples:** A finite-stratum calculation showing equality of the two functionals; weights diverging as overlap approaches zero; structural nonpositivity where a target stratum has no treated observations.
- **Source anchors:** H&R ch. 2, printed pp. 13–25, especially Technical Points 2.1–2.3 and their standardization/IPW derivations; ch. 3, printed pp. 27–45.
- **Limits:** Positivity violations are not repaired by algebra. Near-positivity can make weighting unstable even when strict positivity holds. No semiparametric, doubly robust, or double/debiased-machine-learning extension belongs in this category-2 pair.

### Pair 4 — instrumental variables and noncompliance

- **A:** `def-instrumental-variable-potential-outcomes`; `alg-wald-ratio`; `thm-wald-identifies-a-homogeneous-effect`; `thm-wald-identifies-the-complier-average-effect`.
- **B:** `ex-iv-assumption-audit-and-late-versus-ate`.
- **Purpose and definitions:** For binary assignment/instrument $Z$, treatment receipt $A$, and outcome $Y$, define $A(z)$, $Y(a,z)$, exclusion $Y(a,z)=Y(a)$, instrument independence/exogeneity, relevance, and monotonicity $A(1)\ge A(0)$. The Wald ratio is the reduced-form mean contrast in $Y$ divided by the first-stage mean contrast in $A$, when the denominator is nonzero.
- **Results and hypotheses:** With consistency, exclusion, $Z\perp\{Y(0),Y(1),A(0),A(1)\}$, and relevance, a constant additive treatment effect identifies that common effect by the Wald ratio. With binary $Z,A$, the same core assumptions and monotonicity but heterogeneous effects identify the LATE among latent compliers, not generally the ATE. Without extra restrictions an IV does not point-identify the population ATE; bounds and testable implications are model-specific and do not prove that IV assumptions hold.
- **Proof route:** Decompose the first-stage contrast by principal stratum. Independence and exclusion cancel always-takers and never-takers from the reduced form; under monotonicity there are no defiers, leaving the complier treatment effect times the complier proportion. The first-stage contrast equals that proportion, so their ratio is the complier mean effect. Under effect homogeneity the same decomposition reduces to the common ATE. Show the distinction with two full-data potential-outcome laws that agree on the instrument-observed distribution but yield different ATEs when homogeneity/monotonicity is absent.
- **B examples/counterexamples:** Randomized encouragement; defiers breaking the LATE derivation; exclusion violation from a direct effect of encouragement; a LATE that differs from ATE because effects vary by compliance type.
- **Source anchors:** H&R ch. 16 §§16.1–16.6, printed pp. 209–225, especially Technical Points 16.1–16.7; Kang, randomized encouragement and ITT, extracted lines 120–225, and IV conditions/compliance types/LATE proof, lines 275–371; Pearl §3.5, PDF pp. 37–41 / journal pp. 132–136, extracted lines 1831–2169.
- **Limits:** Relevance is observable; exclusion and independence are largely substantive and not established by a strong first stage. Monotonicity is also substantive. State whether the estimand is an ATE, a homogeneous effect, or a LATE before interpreting the ratio.

### Pair 5 — mediation and cross-world assumptions

- **A:** `def-controlled-and-natural-mediation-effects`; `thm-natural-effect-identification-under-cross-world-independence`; `def-interventionist-mediation-effect`.
- **B:** `ex-natural-effect-cross-world-nonidentification`.
- **Purpose and definitions:** Let $M(a)$ be the mediator under treatment $a$, and $Y(a,m)$ the outcome under joint interventions on treatment and mediator. Define controlled direct effects $E[Y(a,m)-Y(a',m)]$, and natural direct/indirect effects with nested counterfactuals such as $Y(a,M(a'))$. Distinguish these from effects of a feasible intervention on a mediator or on separable treatment components.
- **Results and hypotheses:** Controlled effects have an intervention-based definition but require the corresponding treatment/mediator identification assumptions. The usual natural-effect mediation formula needs consistency, positivity, no unmeasured exposure-outcome and mediator-outcome confounding (including no exposure-induced mediator-outcome confounder for the standard formula), and a cross-world independence restriction. H&R's FFRCISTG setup does not imply this cross-world restriction; NPSEM-IE supplies one route. Do not state natural effects as identified from ordinary conditional independence alone. Interventionist/separable effects require meaningful separable treatment components and dismissible-component restrictions; they define a different target.
- **Proof route:** Express the nested counterfactual mean using the cross-world independence assumption to factor the mediator distribution under one treatment from the outcome response under the other; use consistency and observed-data exchangeability/positivity to replace each factor by an observed conditional distribution. The limitation is established by showing that ordinary single-world observed laws do not determine the cross-world joint distribution. For separable effects, derive the estimand only after writing the component interventions and component-specific restrictions.
- **B examples/counterexamples:** A randomized treatment with an unmeasured mediator-outcome common cause; an exposure-induced mediator-outcome confounder; a component-treatment intervention that changes the estimand.
- **Source anchors:** H&R ch. 23 §§23.1–23.4 and Technical Points 23.1–23.3, printed pp. 323–331; Pearl §5.1, PDF pp. 39–41 / journal pp. 134–136, extracted lines 2008–2169.
- **Limits:** No cross-world assumption is empirically testable from one-world observed data. Natural direct and indirect effects are not automatic decompositions of an identified total effect.

### Pair 6 — interference, exposure mappings, and strategy-specific effects

- **A:** `def-potential-outcomes-under-interference`; `def-partial-interference-and-treatment-strategy`; `def-exposure-mapping-under-interference`; `alg-ht-exposure-specific-mean`; `thm-unbiasedness-of-ht-exposure-specific-mean`; `thm-two-stage-randomization-unbiasedness-under-partial-interference`.
- **B:** `ex-direct-indirect-and-overall-effects-under-spillover`.
- **Purpose and definitions:** Replace $Y_i(a_i)$ with $Y_i(\mathbf a)$ when another unit's assignment can affect unit $i$. Partial interference assumes units belong to groups and there is no interference across groups; it still allows arbitrary within-group spillovers. In the general exposure-mapping framework, define $D_i=f(Z,\theta_i)$ and require that the mapping fully captures which assignments can change $Y_i$. Define direct, indirect, total, and overall effects relative to an explicit group coverage/allocation strategy or an exposure contrast.
- **Results and hypotheses:** For a fixed finite population, known randomized design, correct exposure mapping, consistency, and positive individual exposure probabilities $\pi_i(d)=P(D_i=d)>0$, the exposure-specific mean $\mu(d)=N^{-1}\sum_iY_i(d)$ is design-identified by the Horvitz–Thompson functional $N^{-1}\sum_i I(D_i=d)Y_i/\pi_i(d)$ and its expectation equals $\mu(d)$. Differences of such means identify exposure contrasts. Under the two-stage group/within-group design in Hudgens–Halloran, their estimators of direct, indirect, total, and overall effects are design-unbiased under partial interference and positive design probabilities. Their variance estimators require an additional stratified-interference condition; this condition is not required for point-effect unbiasedness and may fail under nonadditive spillover effects.
- **Proof route:** For the general exposure result, condition on the fixed potential-outcome schedule: $E[I(D_i=d)Y_i/\pi_i(d)]=Y_i(d)$ by exposure consistency and the known design probability; sum over units and divide by $N$. For Hudgens–Halloran, average the assignment indicators under the known two-stage design probabilities to obtain each strategy-specific target. For variance, expand design covariance terms and state the extra stratified-interference assumption explicitly.
- **B examples/counterexamples:** Vaccine coverage with within-village spillovers; contamination across nominal groups violating partial interference; different direct effects at two coverage levels; nonadditive responses that invalidate the variance condition.
- **Source anchors:** Hudgens–Halloran, §§2–4 and Theorems 1–3, PMC full text lines 56–340 and Appendix A.1–A.9 proofs, lines 341–452; Aronow–Samii, §§3–4 and Lemma 4.1 / Proposition 4.2, PDF pp. 4–11, extracted lines 133–641, with Lemma 4.1 and Proposition 4.2 proofs in Appendix A.2 and A.4, lines 1927–2021.
- **Limits:** No-interference and partial-interference assumptions are design/model restrictions. Effects under one treatment strategy do not identify effects under another strategy. Two-stage design results are not universal observational estimators.

### Pair 7 — missingness mechanisms and identification limits

- **A:** `def-full-observed-and-missing-data`; `def-mcar-mar-and-mnar`; `thm-binary-outcome-mnar-nonidentification`.
- **B:** `ex-complete-case-selection-and-mnar-bounds`.
- **Purpose and definitions:** Define full data $W=(Y,X,V)$, observation indicator $R$ (or a pattern vector for multivariate records), and observed data. MCAR means observation is independent of all full data; MAR means missing components are conditionally independent of $R$ given the observed components and measured auxiliary variables; MNAR is the complement. These definitions depend on the chosen full-data/observed-data partition.
- **Results and hypotheses:** Under unrestricted nonparametric MNAR, the full-data distribution is generally not identified by its observed-data distribution. For binary $Y$ and response indicator $R$, let $a=P(R=1,Y=1)$ and $b=P(R=1,Y=0)$, with $a+b>0$. The observed law permits $E(Y)=\theta$ throughout $[a,1-b]$; every interior value can be generated by response probabilities $P(R=1\mid Y=1)=a/\theta$ and $P(R=1\mid Y=0)=b/(1-\theta)$. Under MAR in this one-variable example, $\theta=a/(a+b)$. Parametric MNAR restrictions can identify parameters only conditional on those restrictions.
- **Proof route:** Construct the response probabilities just given to preserve the same observed respondent masses for each admissible $\theta$, while varying the unseen nonrespondent outcomes. For MAR, the response mechanism cancels from the respondent distribution, giving the respondent proportion as the population proportion in the simple example.
- **B examples/counterexamples:** MCAR subsampling; MAR response depending on fully observed $X$; MNAR response depending on unseen $Y$; complete-case regression's special cases versus its general selection bias; a competing event such as death that changes whether the outcome is well-defined, rather than merely hiding it.
- **Source anchors:** NRC, *The Prevention and Treatment of Missing Data in Clinical Trials*, ch. 4 printed pp. 47–58 and 70–77, and complete ch. 5 pp. 83–106; Mao, *Missing Data: Foundations and Methods*, ch. 1 §§1.1–1.7, extracted lines 11–175.
- **Limits:** MAR/MNAR are generally not distinguishable from observed data without restrictions or external information. Complete-case analysis is not generally unbiased under MAR; special regression estimands/models can have exceptions. Death and truncation require a scientific estimand decision before coding them as missingness.

### Pair 8 — missing-data weighting and sensitivity analysis

- **A:** `alg-response-probability-weighting`; `thm-missing-at-random-weighting-identity`; `def-pattern-mixture-sensitivity-parameter`.
- **B:** `ex-ipw-instability-and-mnar-sensitivity-curve`.
- **Purpose and definitions:** For a fully observed covariate vector $X$, let $\pi(X)=P(R=1\mid X)$. Define complete-case selection weights $R/\pi(X)$, distinguish known probabilities from estimated ones, and define a pattern-mixture sensitivity model, for example $Y\mid X,R=0\stackrel d=Y_1+\Delta(X)$ when $Y_1$ has the respondent conditional law. In that stated shift model, $\Delta=0$ yields conditional equality; it does not make a zero mean shift equivalent to general MAR without the shift-model restrictions.
- **Results and hypotheses:** If $Y\perp R\mid X$, $\pi(X)>0$ on target support, and $E|Y|<\infty$, then $E[RY/\pi(X)]=E[Y]$. The conditional mean version is also identified by respondent outcomes and the target $X$ law. With $\pi$ estimated, these population identities do not alone prove finite-sample or asymptotic properties. Under MNAR, vary $\Delta$ or a selection-model parameter over scientifically defensible values and recompute the target; the observed law does not estimate that sensitivity parameter without additional information.
- **Proof route:** Condition on $X$: $E[RY\mid X]=\pi(X)E[Y\mid R=1,X]$; divide by positive $\pi(X)$, then use MAR to replace the respondent conditional mean by $E[Y\mid X]$, and integrate. Sensitivity analysis substitutes the assumed nonrespondent distribution into the full-data mixture $P(Y\mid X)=P(Y\mid R=1,X)P(R=1\mid X)+P(Y\mid R=0,X)P(R=0\mid X)$.
- **B examples/counterexamples:** A weighted binary mean; near-zero response probabilities and large weights; two MNAR sensitivity curves with identical observed-data fit; auxiliary measured predictors improving response modeling but not proving MAR.
- **Source anchors:** H&R ch. 8 §§8.1–8.6 and Technical Points 8.1–8.2, printed pp. 109–123, plus §12.6, pp. 172–173; NRC ch. 4 pp. 47–58 and 70–77, ch. 5 pp. 83–106; Mao ch. 1 §1.12, extracted lines 228–259.
- **Limits:** Weighting changes representation only under the stated response model and positivity. Extreme weights signal weak support and instability. Sensitivity curves report dependence on untestable assumptions; they do not test MAR. No multiple imputation, EM, Bayesian, or doubly robust extension is proposed here.

### Pair 9 — measurement error and misclassification

- **A:** `def-measurement-error-models`; `thm-classical-error-attenuation-in-simple-linear-regression`; `thm-berkson-error-slope-in-simple-linear-model`.
- **B:** `ex-classical-berkson-differential-error-and-validation`.
- **Purpose and definitions:** Distinguish latent true $X$, observed $X^*$, classical additive error $X^*=X+U$, Berkson error $X=X^*+U$, general linear error, and categorical misclassification via sensitivity/specificity or a transition matrix. Define differential error conditionally on other variables in the measurement process; avoid treating “nondifferential” as a universal direction-of-bias guarantee.
- **Results and hypotheses:** In the single-covariate linear model $Y=\alpha+\beta X+\varepsilon$, if $X^*=X+U$, $U$ is independent of $X$ and $\varepsilon$, and $\varepsilon$ is independent of $(X,U)$ with mean zero, with finite second moments, the population slope from regressing $Y$ on $X^*$ is
  \[
  \beta\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(U)}.
  \]
  Under the simple Berkson model $X=X^*+U$, with $U$ and $\varepsilon$ independent of $X^*$ and of each other, the same linear slope is $\beta$. Neither result extends automatically to multiple correlated covariates, nonlinear outcomes, differential error, or nonlinear estimands. Without a validation/calibration/replicate design or a sufficiently restrictive known error law, the joint law of true exposure and error is generally not identified from $X^*$ alone.
- **Proof route:** For classical error, compute $\operatorname{Cov}(Y,X^*)/\operatorname{Var}(X^*)$ using the independence and mean-zero assumptions; the numerator is $\beta\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and the denominator is $\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(U)$. For Berkson error, $\operatorname{Cov}(Y,X^*)=\beta\operatorname{Var}(X^*)$ under its stated model. For nonidentification, show distinct latent/error decompositions with the same observed $X^*$ law. A validation subset, calibration variable, or replicate may add identifying information only with its own design/model assumptions.
- **B examples/counterexamples:** A noisy predictor attenuation calculation; Berkson assignment of a reported group mean; outcome error in linear regression; differential misclassification with bias in either direction; calibration and validation subsamples.
- **Source anchors:** H&R ch. 9 §§9.1–9.6, printed pp. 125–135; Keogh et al. (STRATOS), §§1–4.3.5, PMC full text extracted lines 104–373.
- **Limits:** “Classical error attenuates toward zero” is only a simple linear-regression result under the listed conditions. Nondifferential misclassification does not in general guarantee attenuation for adjusted, nonlinear, multivariable, or causal effects. Correction algorithms and their inferential properties require additional models and belong in separately scoped material.

Three complete PDFs were extracted page-for-page with PyMuPDF (`fitz`); the remaining source texts were lawfully available full HTML articles or course/report chapter readers and were fully extracted as HTML text. Full-text acquisition does not mean the entire book/article was read: each record distinguishes the complete source from the focused text actually read and explicitly lists unread sections.

The strongest independent cross-checks are H&R plus Pearl for SCM/DAG/adjustment/IV/mediation; H&R plus Kang for the binary encouragement/LATE decomposition; and NRC plus Mao for missingness identification/weighting. H&H supplies the complete partial-interference two-stage estimator proofs, while Aronow–Samii provides an independent design-based proof under a general but correctly specified exposure mapping. H&R plus STRATOS supply distinct causal and epidemiologic treatments of measurement error. The NRC report is a consensus clinical-trial methods source rather than a theorem-proof text; Mao supplies an explicit nonidentification construction and the IPW expectation derivation.

Remaining boundaries are deliberate: the interference pairs give their finite-population inverse-probability identities and proofs locally, without assuming a dependency on the models-02 sampling/survey pages; no survival-specific censoring or competing-risk theory is claimed (a competing event may change the estimand rather than act as missingness); no semiparametric efficiency/asymptotics, Bayesian imputation, EM, multiple imputation, double robustness, or ML nuisance estimation is proposed. IV partial-identification results are mentioned only as limitations and observable implications, not developed into a full bounds chapter. Empirical plausibility judgments must remain separate from every mathematical identification claim.
