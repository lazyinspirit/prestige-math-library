# Foundations source dossier 01

Commission scope: statistical experiments and models; identifiability; estimators and randomization; loss, risk, and decision theory; domination and likelihood; sufficiency, completeness, and exponential families; exact finite-sample inference.

This dossier supplies source evidence and a dependency-ordered A/B-pair proposal. It does not author library items, pages, or plans. The proposed foundations depend on probability, measure, and conditional-distribution suppliers already in the library. They do not rely on categories 2–4 or on numerical methods.

## Findings and recommended boundary

An experiment can be introduced as a measurable sample space together with a parameter-indexed family of probability measures. A model is identifiable precisely when distinct parameter values induce distinct laws; an estimand can remain identifiable even when the parameterization is not. The sampling family alone does not select an estimator or decision: an action space and a loss function complete the decision problem. A decision rule can be deterministic or randomized, with the latter represented by a probability kernel from observed data to actions. Its risk is expected loss under each fixed parameter value. Domination compares whole risk functions across parameters; admissibility means no other rule dominates the rule.

For a dominated experiment, a likelihood is the density of the observation with the data held fixed. It is not a probability distribution over parameter values. Its ratios and maximizing values are meaningful only after specifying a common dominating measure and a version of the density. A common-support assumption or a carefully stated null-set convention is needed before treating a pointwise likelihood as a unique function of the data.

Sufficiency is most safely defined by a parameter-independent conditional law of the full data given the statistic. A regular conditional kernel exists for standard-Borel sample spaces, using the already published library theorem. A complete discrete proof of the factorization criterion is available. The sources state the general dominated factorization criterion, but their proofs either cover only discrete data or refer elsewhere. For the scaffold, either prove the general version under explicit standard-Borel/reference-measure hypotheses, or present the fully proved discrete and common-support versions and label the broader statement as an unproved extension. Do not silently claim the broad theorem from the lecture notes alone.

For a full-rank exponential family, the canonical statistic is sufficient by factorization and complete by uniqueness of moment-generating functions on an open natural-parameter set. Berkeley’s lecture note supplies a vector proof route independent of the UIC notes’ one-dimensional sketch. The full-rank/open-set and no-affine-constraint hypotheses matter: the curved family (N(\theta,\theta^2)) in the UIC notes has a minimal sufficient statistic that is not complete.

Exact inference can be introduced through coverage and pivot inversion. If (Q(X,\theta)) has the same continuous distribution for every \(\theta\), and (q_L,q_U) enclose probability (1-\alpha), then inverting (q_L\le Q(x,\theta)\le q_U) gives a confidence set with exact coverage. Discrete pivots generally give coverage at least nominal; attaining equality can require randomization. Exact normal-mean and uniform-endpoint examples are fully supported by the read notes. I recommend leaving the normal unknown-variance (t) pivot out of this foundational pair unless the scaffold adds a complete proof of sample-mean/sample-variance independence and the chi-square result: both independent notes read here explicitly defer that proof.

“Randomization” here means randomized inference/decision procedures. Experimental treatment assignment belongs in the later models/experiments category; this dossier does not make it a prerequisite.

## Proposed dependency-ordered A/B pairs

IDs are stable proposals, not authored or validated items. “A” denotes a reusable statement/definition supplier. “B” denotes an example/counterexample application and is not a cross-page supplier.

### F01 — Statistical experiment, model, and identifiability

- **A — `def-statistical-experiment-and-identifiability`.** Define an experiment as \((\mathcal X,\mathcal A,\{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\})\), with measurable observation space and parameter-indexed probability measures. Define identifiability by injectivity of \(\theta\mapsto P_\theta\), and estimand identifiability by \(P_\theta=P_{\theta'}\Rightarrow\psi(\theta)=\psi(\theta')\). State that the law is the object in the model, not a realized sample.
- **B — `ex-statistical-model-identifiability`.** Give an identifiable Bernoulli/normal parameterization and a nonidentifiable reparameterization such as \(X\sim N(\theta^2,1)\), where \(\theta\) is not identifiable but \(\psi(\theta)=\theta^2\) is. An example with rank-deficient normal regression can be added only if the fixed-design/rank hypotheses are stated.
- **Dependencies.** Existing probability-measure, measurable-space, random-element, and law definitions. No category-2 design assumptions.
- **Evidence and route.** Martin §1.3.6 says the law-index map \(\theta\mapsto F_\theta\) must be one-to-one to learn \(\theta\), and §1.4.1 gives data/model/loss/prior components. Geyer §6 defines identifiable parameterization and gives the canonical-parameter criterion in regular full exponential families. The example proof is direct equality/non-equality of distributions.
- **Caveat.** Martin §1.4.1, PDF p.23, says the data take values in sample space “\(\Theta\)” where context requires the sample space \(\mathcal X\). Do not copy this typo.

### F02 — Estimators and randomized decision rules

- **A — `def-estimator-and-randomized-decision-rule`.** Define an estimator/decision rule as a measurable map from observed data to an action; define a randomized rule as a probability kernel \(\delta(x,da)\) on the action space, measurable in \(x\). Distinguish the observed estimate/action from the rule before observing data. State that a randomized rule can be implemented with auxiliary randomness independent of \(\theta\), when an implementation is needed.
- **B — `ex-estimator-and-randomization`.** Show a point estimator as a data map, and a randomized binary action as a probability of rejection. Include a finite-sample boundary/tie example where a coin flip attains a specified probability exactly. Do not include the Neyman–Pearson optimality theorem here; that belongs to foundations-02.
- **Dependencies.** Existing measurable-function and probability-kernel definitions. Use an action space with a stated measurable structure.
- **Evidence and route.** Martin §5.1 defines deterministic and randomized rules and uses an auxiliary coin for tests; Hoff pp.2–6 defines estimator and test function, where \(\delta(x)\) is the probability of rejecting. MIT Note 6 pp.14–15 gives a randomized rule obtained by resampling from the conditional distribution given a sufficient statistic.

### F03 — Loss, risk, domination, and admissibility

- **A — `def-loss-risk-and-decision-criteria`.** Define nonnegative (or integrable) loss \(L(\theta,a)\), risk \(R(\theta,\delta)=E_\theta[L(\theta,\delta(X))]\), randomized risk by integrating loss against \(\delta(X,da)\), and domination/admissibility by pointwise comparison over every \(\theta\). State average-risk and maximum-risk criteria and define minimax through \(\inf_\delta\sup_\theta R(\theta,\delta)\). An average-risk reference measure is a decision criterion, not a posterior-model dependency. Give squared, absolute, and 0–1 loss as distinct choices.
- **B — `ex-loss-risk-and-dominance`.** Compute risk for a sample-mean decision under squared loss; give a constant rule that has zero risk at one parameter value but poor risk elsewhere, to show why admissibility does not mean uniformly best. A constant-risk rule illustrates the minimax criterion directly. For convex actions, show randomization can be removed by replacing the action distribution with its barycenter and applying Jensen. Contrast binary 0–1 testing, where randomization can matter.
- **Dependencies.** Existing expectation and conditional-expectation/Jensen items. For the barycenter claim, require a convex action set, a finite action barycenter, convex loss on that set, and integrability (or a nonnegative extended-risk argument with a finite comparison).
- **Evidence and route.** Martin §5.1 defines actions/loss/rules/risk and §5.2 defines domination/admissibility; Theorem 5.1 proves conditional Jensen/Rao–Blackwell risk reduction. Hoff §§4.1–4.3 defines decision problem and risk. Its Theorem 1 on PDF p.10 is not reusable: the printed display compares \(E_{P_2}L\) with the identical \(E_{P_2}L\), so the claimed preference representation is false as printed.
- **Hypothesis correction.** Martin Theorem 5.2, PDF p.104, says any randomized rule is no worse than some deterministic rule if the loss is convex. Its proof takes an expectation of actions, so the statement also needs a convex action space and an existing finite barycenter; finite risk/integrability must be stated. The theorem does not eliminate randomized tests with a nonconvex binary action space.

### F04 — Dominated model and likelihood

- **A — `def-dominated-statistical-model-and-likelihood`.** Define domination by a supplied \(\sigma\)-finite measure \(\mu\) with \(P_\theta\ll\mu\), densities \(p_\theta=dP_\theta/d\mu\), and the likelihood \(L_x(\theta)=p_\theta(x)\) for fixed observed data. Say explicitly that a likelihood is not a probability distribution on \(\Theta\). Explain the version/common-null-set caveat and that multiplying by a positive data-only factor preserves comparisons and maximizers.
- **B — `ex-dominated-likelihoods-and-ratios`.** Work a Poisson or Bernoulli sample under counting measure and a normal sample under Lebesgue measure. Show the likelihood is the sample PMF/PDF held fixed as a function of \(\theta\), and show a change between equivalent dominating measures changes it by a factor independent of \(\theta\). Include a zero-denominator caveat for likelihood ratios.
- **Dependencies.** Existing probability-measure and RN/density integration items. The common dominating measure must be fixed before likelihood comparisons are stated.
- **Evidence and route.** Martin §2.2 defines exponential-family densities relative to a \(\sigma\)-finite dominator; §3.2 defines likelihood; Tebbs §9.3 p.121 distinguishes the joint PMF/PDF from the same function with the data fixed; Geyer §3 states that parameter-free terms may be dropped from log likelihood. Scale-invariance follows directly from the Radon–Nikodym change-of-measure formula for equivalent dominators.

### F05 — Sufficient statistic and factorization

- **A — `def-sufficient-statistic-and-factorization`.** Define sufficiency through a common conditional-law kernel independent of \(\theta\), with standard-Borel/conditional-kernel hypotheses or an explicitly supplied kernel. State the factorization implication for a fixed common dominator. For the fully proved equivalence, restrict the theorem to a countable sample space or to a standard-Borel common-support model with a reference law dominating every member. Present a general common-\(\mu\) equivalence only with an added proof and exact null-set hypotheses.
- **B — `ex-sufficient-statistics`.** Give a Bernoulli sample sum, Poisson sample sum, normal sample sum (known variance), and uniform endpoint/order-statistic examples. Include one counterexample showing that the full data and the order statistics are sufficient but differ in reduction size. Keep all distribution and support hypotheses explicit.
- **Dependencies.** Existing `def-conditional-law-given-a-random-element`; its regular conditional distribution existence supplier applies to standard-Borel targets. Existing IID and law definitions support examples. Existing conditional Jensen/expectation items support the Rao–Blackwell extension if included later.
- **Evidence and route.** Martin §2.3 defines sufficiency by a kernel and states the Neyman–Fisher theorem under common \(\sigma\)-finite domination, but its proof is expressly only a sketch and sends the full proof to Keener (2010), §6.4 (not read). MIT Note 6 pp.8–9 and Berkeley Fithian, factorization section, give a complete discrete proof in both directions. Tebbs p.118 gives the Poisson conditional law directly; pp.124–130 applies factorization, including parameter-dependent support. Geyer §8.4 states the general criterion but gives no proof.
- **Proof route for a safe initial theorem.** In the discrete case, set \(g_\theta(t)=P_\theta(T=t)\) and \(h(x)=P(X=x\mid T=T(x))\) for the sufficiency-to-factorization direction; the reverse direction cancels \(g_\theta(t)\) from the conditional mass on each fiber. Handle zero-probability fibers with a fixed filler kernel. For a common-support continuous model dominated by a reference \(P_{\theta_0}\), use \(f_\theta=dP_\theta/dP_{\theta_0}\), compute the conditional law under \(P_\theta\) by Bayes’ change-of-measure formula, and show sufficiency is equivalent to \(f_\theta\) being measurable with respect to \(\sigma(T)\). This yields \(p_\theta(x)=g_\theta(T(x))h(x)\) with \(h=p_{\theta_0}\). The unrestricted dominated theorem remains a proof obligation.
- **Limit.** Do not say a statistic is sufficient merely because the likelihood “depends on it” without presenting the factorization/common-support conditions. A naive continuous formula \(f_{Y\mid T}(y\mid t)=f_Y(y)/f_T(t)\) is generally not a conditional density for a lower-dimensional statistic; Tebbs prints this formula in a general implementation remark, so do not reuse it.

### F06 — Exponential family, completeness, and complete sufficiency

- **A — `def-exponential-family-and-complete-statistic`.** Define the dominated exponential-family form \(p_\eta(x)=h(x)\exp\{\eta^\top T(x)-A(\eta)\}\), its natural parameter set, and the meanings of full-rank/regular. Define completeness with the condition \(E_\eta|g(T)|<\infty\) and \(E_\eta g(T)=0\) for every parameter value implying \(g(T)=0\) almost surely under every family member. State the full-rank exponential-family theorem only with an open natural-parameter set and no affine constraint on \(T\).
- **B — `ex-exponential-family-completeness`.** Factor Poisson/Bernoulli/normal likelihoods; identify canonical sufficient statistics and mean/variance derivatives of the cumulant; exhibit the curved \(N(\theta,\theta^2)\) counterexample to completeness and the uniform-maximum example where support varies and the sufficient statistic is complete outside the regular exponential-family case.
- **Dependencies.** F04 and F05, plus existing expectation/conditional-law interfaces. The full-rank theorem uses the factorization sufficiency direction, but the completeness proof is independent after that reduction.
- **Evidence and route.** Geyer §§3–6 gives the exponential-family form, common support/nondegeneracy, cumulant derivatives, and canonical identifiability criterion. Martin §§2.2–2.3.3 gives factorization, minimality, completeness, and a one-dimensional Laplace-transform proof sketch. Berkeley Fithian “Completeness, Ancillarity, and Basu’s Theorem,” §§1.1–1.4, gives a vector proof by equality of MGFs on an open neighborhood and uniqueness; this independently supports the full-rank complete-sufficient claim. Tebbs §9.4 opening states completeness and its role, but the read range does not prove completeness of the general exponential family.
- **Proof route.** Sufficiency is the factorization step. For completeness choose an interior \(\eta_0\), tilt the induced law of \(T\) under \(P_{\eta_0}\), and let \(\nu(dt)=g(t)P_{\eta_0}^T(dt)\). The zero-expectation equations imply the Laplace/MGF of this finite signed measure vanishes on a neighborhood of zero. MGF uniqueness gives \(\nu=0\); since the exponential-family members are mutually absolutely continuous on their common support, \(g(T)=0\) under every member. Explicitly handle the zero-total-mass case before normalizing positive/negative parts.
- **Caveats.** “Complete” does not imply “sufficient”; the constant statistic is complete. “Complete sufficient implies minimal sufficient” needs integrability for the conditional-expectation argument. Fithian’s note is marked “Under construction,” contains a placeholder in an unrelated Laplace example, and its complete-sufficiency proof omits an explicit sentence for the zero-mass branch; the missing branch is immediate, but it should be supplied. Martin’s uniform-max density on PDF p.39 prints support \(0<t<n\); it should be \(0<t<\theta\). Do not copy either defect.

### F07 — Exact confidence procedure by pivot inversion

- **A — `def-exact-confidence-procedure-and-pivot`.** Define a confidence set \(C(X)\) for \(\psi(\theta)\) by its repeated-sampling coverage under each fixed \(\theta\). Define a pivot \(Q(X,\theta)\) as a statistic whose sampling law is parameter-free. If continuous quantiles \(q_L,q_U\) satisfy \(P(q_L\le Q\le q_U)=1-\alpha\), define the inverted set \(C(x)=\{\theta:q_L\le Q(x,\theta)\le q_U\}\); prove exact coverage by event equivalence. State that discreteness generally changes equality to a conservative bound unless a randomized boundary is used.
- **B — `ex-exact-pivot-confidence-intervals`.** Work (i) the known-variance normal mean interval and (ii) the uniform \((0,\theta)\) maximum interval. For the latter, derive \(F_{M/\theta}(u)=u^n\) directly from \(P(M\le u\theta)=\prod_iP(Y_i\le u\theta)\). Give the exact two-sided coverage calculation and explain the frequentist interpretation after data are observed.
- **Dependencies.** Existing IID/law/expectation and quantile/distribution prerequisites. No tests, Bayesian posterior, asymptotics, or category-2 confidence-set results are needed.
- **Evidence and route.** Tebbs §8.4 defines coverage/pivots and works the normal-known-variance, exponential-scale, and uniform-endpoint inversions. Žitković §§10.1–10.3 gives the same construction independently, plus exact normal/exponential examples; p.16 poses the uniform maximum derivation as an exercise, not a solved example. The exact normal known-variance sampling law is proved by MGF in Tebbs §7.3 and stated in both units.
- **Limit.** The t interval for unknown normal variance is excluded from this pair pending a full proof of the independence/chi-square result. Both Tebbs and Žitković explicitly defer it. Žitković p.6 has `0.95` where its own `α=0.1` and later `0.9` require `0.9`.

## Source reading and evidence limitations

All PDF files below were obtained from author/institution-hosted public course pages, extracted in full with PyMuPDF (`fitz`), and kept only in the ignored source cache. Both Berkeley units were fetched from the instructor’s public course reader and extracted with Python’s `html.parser`; inline mathematical markup remains in LaTeX form. Hashes and exact source/read ranges are in `sources.json`. Full-text acquisition is not represented as full-document reading: page ranges below identify the complete in-scope lecture units actually read.

The UIC notes point to Keener (2010), Brown (1986), and Lehmann–Casella for more complete proofs. Those books were not acquired or read. The factorization theorem’s unrestricted measure-theoretic equivalence and the classical uniqueness proof for minimal sufficiency therefore remain explicit proof obligations; the safe discrete/common-reference versions above have complete read proof routes. No access control was bypassed.

The full USC STAT 513 notes were acquired and extracted but not substantively read or cited. Their central chapter is hypothesis testing, which the assignments place in foundations-02. They are recorded as acquired-but-unread in `sources.json` for an accurate audit trail.

The distinction between exact and asymptotic statements is kept explicit. UIC’s MLE/LR results in §3.3 are asymptotic and not used as exact inference. The t interval and other normal-variance results are not used until the independence/chi-square proofs are supplied. The pivot inversion theorem itself is proved by event equivalence and needs only a parameter-free continuous pivot law.
