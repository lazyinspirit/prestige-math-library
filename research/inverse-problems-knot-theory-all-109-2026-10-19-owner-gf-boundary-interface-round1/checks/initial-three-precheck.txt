Those computations have no finite-volume premise.\n\n[F2] The upper half-space and sphere compactification and Möbius action are explicit in [[lem-complete-curvature-minus-one-manifolds-are-kleinian-quotients]]. In particular an affine map $z\\mapsto\\alpha z+\\beta$ extends as $(z,Y)\\mapsto(\\alpha z+\\beta,|\\alpha|Y)$.\n\n[F3] The actual pared cylinder and all-torus-boundary axioms imply either the ball with empty $P$, or strictly negative Euler characteristic for every component of $\\partial_0M$. They also make each annular cusp generator primitive in its full stabilizer. [[lem-pared-cylinder-axioms-force-negative-boundary-and-full-peripheral-centralizers]]\n\n[F4] A bounded entire function is constant by [[thm-liouville-bounded-entire-function]], whose Cauchy-estimate proof applies on circles of every radius.\n\n[A1] Full choice is [[def-axiom-of-choice]].\n\n\n\n1.1 If the limit set is empty, a nonidentity element would be parabolic or loxodromic by [F1], and its iterates would already give one or two limit points. Thus the group is trivial. If the limit set is a singleton, every group element fixes it, and a nonidentity element cannot be loxodromic, since its two fixed points are orbital limits. Normalize the point to infinity; [F1] gives a discrete rank-one or rank-two translation lattice. If the limit set has two points, every group element preserves that pair. An element interchanging them is conjugate to $z\\mapsto c/z$ and has order two, so is excluded. All elements fix each endpoint and are diagonal. The discrete torsion-free diagonal argument of [F1] gives an infinite cyclic group, whose generator can be inverted to have $|a|>1$. These alternatives give all listed normal forms without assuming finite generation. [F1, F2, given, construct, algebra]\n\n2.1 The limit sets of the displayed forms are respectively empty, $\\{0,\\infty\\}$ and $\\{\\infty\\}$, directly from their iterates and properness on the complement. Their discontinuity domains are therefore the indicated sphere, punctured plane and plane. In the cyclic loxodromic case use $z=e^w$: the covering $\\mathbb C\\to\\mathbb C^*/\\langle a\\rangle$ has deck translations $2\\pi i$ and one logarithm of $a$. Their real-linear independence follows from $\\operatorname{Re}\\log a=\\log|a|>0$, and their lattice quotient is a complex torus. A different logarithm gives the same lattice. For rank-one translations, $z\\mapsto\\exp(2\\pi iz/\\omega)$ has exactly the given translation fibers and is locally biholomorphic, proving the quotient is $\\mathbb C^*$. The rank-two quotient is its defining translation torus. All actions are free on their discontinuity domains. [F1, F2, step 1.1, construct, algebra]\n\n2.2 For primitive generators $T_\\omega,T_{\\omega'}$, an isomorphism sends $T_\\omega$ to $T_{\\omega'}^\\epsilon$, $\\epsilon\\in\\{1,-1\\}$. The affine map $A(z)=(\\epsilon\\omega'/\\omega)z+\\beta$ satisfies $AT_\\omega A^{-1}=T_{\\omega'}^\\epsilon$ by subtraction of its two affine values. Its extension in [F2] sends the horoball $Y>T$ to $Y>|\\omega'/\\omega|T$ and gives the actual isometry of the marked rank-one cusp quotients and their collars. Thus no finite-area annulus or opposite disk is needed for this local matching. Loxodromic multipliers are conjugacy invariants, and rank-two translation lattices must be related by a complex similarity for such an affine realization; no arbitrary gluing of those data was inferred. [F2, F3, step 1.1, given, construct, algebra]\n\n3.1 In the loxodromic case the boundary fundamental group has basis $2\\pi i,\\log a$, and its map to $\\Gamma$ sends $m(2\\pi i)+n\\log a$ to $a^n$. Its kernel is the nontrivial meridian subgroup $2\\pi i\\mathbb Z$, so the boundary is not $\\pi_1$-injective. For the two parabolic cases $\\mathbb C$ is the simply connected cover, so the boundary group maps isomorphically to the full translation group. In particular $\\langle z\\mapsto z+1\\rangle$ is a genuine elementary example with incompressible conformal boundary $\\mathbb C^*$. That boundary has a compactification by two points and Euler characteristic zero. Its universal conformal cover is $\\mathbb C$, not a disk: a conformal equivalence to the disk would give a bounded nonconstant entire function, contradicting [F4]. Thus it cannot be a cofinite disk-hyperbolic model. None of the displayed domains is a quasidisk, since its boundary has zero, one or two points instead of a Jordan circle; their closures also leave no opposite disk. [F1, F2, F4, step 1.1, step 2.1, construct, algebra]\n\n4.1 Apply [F3] to a putative marked pared model. In the nonball case every nonempty component of $\\partial_0M$ has negative Euler characteristic, while each elementary conformal boundary above is connected and has nonnegative Euler characteristic. A boundary homotopy equivalence must match components and preserve their finite CW Euler characteristics; replacing the interior of a compact collared surface by a smaller compact collar core is a homotopy equivalence. Hence it cannot match any of these elementary boundaries. If $\\partial_0M$ is empty, it cannot be homotopy equivalent to a nonempty elementary conformal boundary either. In the ball case [F3] gives $P=\\varnothing$ and trivial fundamental group, and [F2]'s compactified ball gives its trivial-group realization. A solid torus with a longitudinal annular $P$ has its torus boundary only partly in $P$, so it is not a counterexample to the all-torus-boundary model premise. This distinguishes the elementary example of step3.1 from the correct full model. [F2, F3, step 2.1, step 3.1, given, construct, algebra]\n\n5.1 Finally suppose $\\Omega=\\varnothing$ in the nonelementary geometrically finite case. Its limit set is the whole sphere, whose closed hyperbolic convex hull is all of $\\mathbb H^3$: every model point lies on a complete geodesic joining two ideal points. Therefore every positive-radius convex-hull neighborhood is $\\mathbb H^3$ itself, and the defining finite-volume hypothesis is exactly finite total quotient volume. There are no conformal-boundary components to uniformize, so its empty product is a point. Elementary groups were already shown to have nonempty discontinuity. This proves the distinct branches and local realizations while leaving every global gluing and nonelementary quasidisk obligation explicit. [A1, F2, step 1.1, step 2.1, given, construct, algebra] ∎"

In particular, the homotopy fixes the annulus boundary maps; a homotopy merely allowing those maps to move through $M$ is not the stated premise.\n\n[F2] Under AC, closed orientable surfaces have genus $g\\ge0$ and Euler characteristic $2-2g$ by [[thm-classification-of-compact-connected-surfaces]]. Capping $b$ collared boundary circles adds $b$ to the finite Euler count. Jordan curves on the sphere have disk complementary closures by the planar [[lem-jordan-schoenflies-extension-for-plane-curves]], transported through a sphere chart. Smooth collars exist by [[thm-collar-neighborhood-theorem]]. In the given finite PL branch, boundary half-space charts and surface-rim charts are the local collars to which [[lem-compact-locally-collared-pl-subpolyhedron-has-pl-collar]] applies; its finite local-push proof supplies actual PL collars. Full-dimensional embedded interiors are open by [[thm-invariance-of-domain]], including for the underlying maps of the given PL objects.\n\n[F3] A discrete torsion-free Möbius group has no nonidentity elliptic elements. A subgroup fixing one parabolic point consists of a translation lattice of rank one or two: the affine commutation and discreteness calculations are proved in [[lem-local-thin-components-of-torsion-free-kleinian-quotients]], Proof3.1–4.1, without any volume hypothesis.\n\n[A1] Full choice is [[def-axiom-of-choice]].\n\n\n\n1.1 Cap each of the finitely many boundary circles of a component $S_0$ of $S$ by a disk using its given surface collar. The capped oriented surface has genus $g$, so $\\chi(S_0)=2-2g-b$, where $b$ is its boundary count, by [F2]. If this is nonnegative, the possible pairs are $(g,b)=(0,0),(0,1),(0,2),(1,0)$. In the first and last cases $S_0$ is a sphere or torus. In the middle cases it is a disk or annulus: the capped surface is a sphere; the disk-region conclusion of [F2] identifies the complement of one cap as a disk, and the complement of two disjoint caps as an annulus. For the latter, join the caps by a simple arc in their complement, slit along it and use the disk-region parametrization; identifying the two slit edges gives a rectangle with its opposite edges identified. These are statements about the underlying collared surfaces and apply unchanged to the supplied PL branch. Thus the four possibilities are actual surface types, not just Euler counts. [A1, F2, given, construct, algebra]\n\n1.2 Fix a base point in $Q$, a nontrivial peripheral loop $\\alpha$ representing $a$, and any based loop $h$ in $M$ commuting with $a$. Fill the nullhomotopic commutator square $\\alpha h\\alpha^{-1}h^{-1}$. Its two sides labeled $h$ have the identical parametrized map, so identifying these sides gives a map of an annulus with both boundary loops equal to $\\alpha$ and with the transverse base-point path equal to $h$. Its fundamental-group map is injective because $a$ has infinite order by peripheral injectivity. Apply the relative-cylinder premise. The final connected image is in $Q$, since it contains the fixed boundary loops there. The transverse path, with its endpoints fixed throughout, is therefore homotopic to a based loop in $Q$. Thus $h$ lies in the peripheral image. The reverse inclusion holds because $\\pi_1(Q)$ is abelian. This proves the exact centralizer assertion, including possible roots or finite-index supergroups. [F1, given, construct, algebra]\n\n2.1 A disk component is impossible. Its boundary is a rim of an annular component $Q$ of $P$ and represents a generator of $\\pi_1(Q)$ up to sign. The disk in $\\partial M$ makes this element trivial in $\\pi_1(M)$, contrary to the assumed injection. A closed torus component of $S$ is also impossible: it would be an entire torus component of $\\partial M$ outside $P$, contrary to the all-torus-boundary hypothesis. [F1, step 1.1, given, contradiction]\n\n2.2 Suppose $S_0$ is an annulus. Each rim borders an annular component of $P$ and is essential there. The inclusion of $S_0$ in $M$ is $\\pi_1$-injective, because its generator is already nontrivial of infinite order in such an injective peripheral annulus. If its two rims border different components of $P$, the relative-cylinder property would homotope a connected annulus into $P$ with boundary in those different components. No such connected image exists. If they border the same annulus $Q$, the two annuli $S_0,Q$ exhaust a closed torus component of $\\partial M$: their four collars match along both rims and their union is open and closed in that boundary component. This torus must lie entirely in $P$, again a contradiction. Thus annular components are excluded by the actual hypotheses, rather than by an extra stability convention. [F1, F2, step 1.1, given, contradiction]\n\n2.3 In the representation case, normalize a nontrivial peripheral element to $T(z)=z+\\omega$, $\\omega\\ne0$. Any commuting Möbius transformation fixes its unique fixed point $\\infty$, hence is affine, and direct multiplication with $T$ makes its linear coefficient one. Conversely every translation commutes with $T$. Every element of $\\Gamma$ fixing $\\infty$ is a translation: a coefficient of nonunit modulus would conjugate $T$ to nontrivial translations tending to the identity, while a unit coefficient different from one would be elliptic. Both contradict discreteness and torsion-freeness, as in [F3]. Consequently the full parabolic stabilizer equals the centralizer of $T$, and step5.1 identifies it with $\\rho\\pi_1(Q)$. [F3, step 1.2, given, construct, algebra]\n\n3.1 Finally suppose a component is a sphere. It is an entire component of $\\partial M$. Push it a short distance into $M$ using the boundary collar, smoothly or in the supplied PL product as appropriate. The pushed sphere bounds a parametrized ball by irreducibility. Its interior cannot contain a boundary point of $M$: an interior chart of the ball followed by a boundary half-space chart of $M$ would give an injective continuous map from an open three-ball to $\\mathbb R^3$, whose image is open by [F2] but lies in a closed half-space and contains a point of its boundary plane. This is impossible. The ball therefore lies on the side away from the original boundary. Removing the short open boundary collar from a connected manifold preserves connectedness, as the collar coordinate pushes any path in that collar to its inner level. The ball interior is both open and closed on the corresponding side of the pushed sphere, so it is that whole side. Hence $M$ is the union of this ball and its boundary sphere collar, which is a ball by radial extension of that supplied collar. The construction uses no change of category. Now $\\pi_1(M)=0$; injectivity excludes every annulus or torus component of $P$, so $P=\\varnothing$. Otherwise steps1.1–3.1 exhaust the nonnegative Euler cases and give $\\chi(S_0)<0$ for every component. [F1, F2, step 1.1, step 2.1, step 2.2, given, construct]\n\n4.1 A discrete additive subgroup of $\\mathbb C$ is a lattice of rank one or two by [F3]. Since $\\rho$ and each peripheral inclusion are injective, its rank here is one for an annulus and two for a torus. In the rank-one case the image of the annulus generator generates the WHOLE stabilizer, so it cannot be a proper power of another stabilizer element. The rank-two image likewise is not a proper finite-index sublattice. Base-path changes conjugate all the displayed groups together. The proof used the actual relative-cylinder and all-torus-boundary clauses, and no geometric finiteness or quasifuchsian assertion. All additional constructions were finite. [A1, F1, F3, step 3.1, step 1.2, step 2.3, algebra] ∎"
