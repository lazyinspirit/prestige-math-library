## PART 2 — The mathematics, with exact hypotheses, proof strategies, prerequisites

Throughout, "F" is the base and "E"/"K"/"L" the extension; I keep each source's own letters
when quoting it.

### 2.1 Aut(K/F), fixed fields, Dedekind/Artin

**Aut(K/F)** = the group of field automorphisms of K fixing F pointwise. For G ≤ Aut(K/F),
the **fixed field** K^G = {x ∈ K : σx = x ∀σ ∈ G} is a subfield containing F
(Clark §8.1; Milne p. 36; Ash 6.1.1).

**Dedekind's / Artin's lemma on independence of characters** (Milne Thm 5.14; Conrad
`linearchar` Thm 2.1; Ash 6.1.6 "Dedekind's Lemma"; Clark 6.§1; Lang VI §4).
> Let F be a field and G a group (or monoid). Any finite set {χ₁,…,χ_m} of *distinct*
> homomorphisms G → F^× is linearly independent over F: Σa_iχ_i = 0 as a function on G
> forces all a_i = 0.

*Proof strategy.* Induct on m. Take a shortest nontrivial relation Σ_{i=1}^{m} a_iχ_i = 0
with all a_i ≠ 0. Pick g₀ with χ₁(g₀) ≠ χ_m(g₀). Evaluate the relation at g₀g and subtract
χ_m(g₀) times the relation at g; the χ_m-term cancels and the χ₁-term survives with
coefficient a₁(χ₁(g₀) − χ_m(g₀)) ≠ 0, producing a shorter relation. Contradiction.
*Prerequisites:* nothing beyond field axioms and a group. **This is the cheapest place to
start the whole development** — it is used for Artin's theorem, Hilbert 90, the cyclic
extension classification, and the normal basis theorem.

**Corollary** (Milne 5.15): distinct field homomorphisms σ₁,…,σ_m : E → F are linearly
independent over F (apply the lemma with G = E^×).

**Artin's theorem on [K : K^G].** There are two versions in the literature and they are
*not* the same theorem:

- **Inequality form** (Milne Thm 3.4): G a finite group of automorphisms of E ⟹
  [E : E^G] ≤ (G : 1). *Proof:* linear algebra. If α₁,…,α_n ∈ E with n > m = |G|, the
  homogeneous system Σ_j σ_i(α_j)X_j = 0 (i = 1..m) has more unknowns than equations, so
  has a nontrivial solution in E; take one with fewest nonzero entries, normalise c₁ = 1,
  and apply σ_k to see all c_i lie in E^G (else subtracting gives a shorter solution).
  The first equation is then an E^G-linear relation among the α_i. *Prerequisites:* solving
  a homogeneous linear system over a field. **No character independence needed.**
- **Equality form** (Milne Cor 3.11; Clark Prop 8.9; Conrad `galoiscorr` Thm 5.2 + 5.3):
  [E : E^G] = |G| and Gal(E/E^G) = G, so E/E^G is Galois with group exactly G.

**The finiteness question is a real flashpoint.** Conrad states Artin's theorem in two
steps: Thm 5.2 *assumes* [E : E^H] < ∞, and Thm 5.3 then proves the finiteness is
automatic (via the "bounded separable degree ⟹ finite" argument + the primitive element
theorem). Conrad remarks that his development only ever needs 5.2 and footnotes that other
books (D&F pp. 569–571, and another reference) prove it "by solving systems of linear
equations" — i.e. Milne's route. Clark footnotes: "In many standard treatments of finite
Galois theory, the finiteness of K/K^G is an additional assumption. Our source for this
stronger version is Lang's Algebra." **The library should state which version it proves.**
Milne's linear-algebra route gives the inequality with no finiteness assumption at all and
is the shortest path to the equality; it is what I would build.

Milne Cor 3.5: for any finite group G of automorphisms of E, G = Aut(E/E^G). Proof is the
sandwich [E : E^G] ≤ |G| ≤ |Aut(E/E^G)| ≤ [E : E^G] (last step = Milne 2.14a, the bound on
the number of F-homomorphisms of a finite extension).

**Prerequisite chain for the equality form:** Milne 2.14a (#{F-homs E → L} ≤ [E:F] for E/F
finite) ← Milne 2.12 (counting homs out of a splitting field) ← Milne 2.4 (extending a
homomorphism along a simple extension) ← Milne 2.1 (homs out of F[α] ↔ roots of the minimal
polynomial in the target).

### 2.2 Separable polynomials, separable extensions, separable degree

**Separable polynomial.** Conrad `separable1` Thm 2.1/Def: f ∈ K[X] nonzero is separable
⟺ gcd(f, f′) = 1 ⟺ f has no repeated root in a splitting field. Milne Def 2.22 adopts the
equivalent conditions of Prop 2.21. **Convention clash:** some books call f separable when
every *irreducible factor* is separable, so that (X−1)² is "separable"; Milne and Conrad
both require distinct roots outright. Milne 2.20 gives the irreducible case, 2.21 the general
one; his Def 2.22 says "a polynomial is separable if it is nonzero and satisfies the
equivalent conditions" of 2.21 — i.e. distinct roots.

**Formal derivative criterion.** f irreducible ⟹ (f separable ⟺ f′ ≠ 0). Consequences:
in char 0 every irreducible is separable; in char p an irreducible f is inseparable ⟺
f(X) = g(X^p) for some g (Milne after Def 3.6; Conrad `separable1` Thm 2.8;
Clark Prop 5.2). *Prerequisite:* Milne Lem 2.19 (α is a multiple root ⟺ f(α) = f′(α) = 0),
which needs only the product rule for the formal derivative.

**Perfect field.** Milne Def 2.23: char 0, or char p with F = F^p. Milne Prop 2.24: F is
perfect ⟺ every irreducible in F[X] is separable. Clark Prop 5.3: char p, F perfect ⟺
Frobenius x ↦ x^p is surjective. Finite fields are perfect (Frobenius is injective on a
field, hence bijective on a finite one). Clark: an imperfect field has an infinite
descending chain F ⊋ F^p ⊋ F^{p²} ⊋ …, which reproves that an imperfect field is infinite.

**Separable degree.** Clark §5.2: for K/F, [K:F]_s := #{F-algebra embeddings K ↪ F̄}.
- Clark Thm 5.5: **multiplicative in towers**, [L:F]_s = [L:K]_s[K:F]_s. *Proof:* every
  embedding σ_i of K extends in exactly [L:K]_s ways to L, so the count is independent of i.
  *Prerequisite:* the extension theorem for embeddings into an algebraically closed field.
- Clark Cor 5.6: [K:F]_s ≤ [K:F] (dévissage into simple extensions; a hom out of
  F[t]/(P) is determined by a root of P and P has at most deg P roots).
- Clark Prop 5.7: [F(α):F]_s = #distinct roots of min poly of α; = [F(α):F] ⟺ min poly is
  separable.
- Clark Thm 5.8: K/F finite is separable ⟺ [K:F]_s = [K:F].
- Clark Cor 5.21: [K:F]_s = [SepCl_K(F) : F] divides [K:F]; **define** [K:F]_i :=
  [K:F]/[K:F]_s = [K : SepCl_K(F)]. Clark Cor 5.14: [K:F]_i is a power of p (char p) —
  purely inseparable degree is always a p-power. In char 0, [K:F]_i = 1 always.

**Conrad's embedding-count theorem** (`separable1` Thm 3.8) is the cleanest formulation for
a library that wants to avoid fixing an algebraic closure early:
> L/K finite of degree n, σ : K → F a field embedding. (a) σ has at most n extensions to an
> embedding L → F. (b) If L/K is inseparable, strictly fewer than n. (c) If L/K is
> separable, there is a field F′ ⊇ F with exactly n extensions of σ to L → F′.
*Proof:* induct on [L:K] via a tower L ⊇ K(α) ⊇ K; the number of extensions to K(α) is the
number of roots of (σπ)(X) in the target, ≤ deg π = [K(α):K], with strictness exactly when
σπ is inseparable — which is preserved under an embedding (`separable1` Cor 2.7).

**Transitivity** (Conrad `separable1` Thm 3.13; Clark Cor 5.9; Conrad `separable2`
Thm 1.7): E/L/K with E/L and L/K separable ⟹ E/K separable. **This is genuinely nontrivial
in char p** and Conrad devotes `separable2.pdf` to four different proofs (trace, nilpotents
in K̄ ⊗ L, derivations, and a direct argument). *Recommended proof for the library:*
via separable degree multiplicativity (Clark), which makes it a one-line count.

**Generation criterion** (Conrad `separable1` Thm 3.5; Clark, Milne 3.15): L = K(α₁,…,α_r)
is separable over K ⟺ each α_i is separable over K.

**Purely inseparable extensions** (Clark §5.3). K/F algebraic is purely inseparable ⟺ there
is only one F-embedding K ↪ F̄ ⟺ every irreducible in F[t] with a root in K is purely
inseparable ⟺ K is generated over F by roots of purely inseparable polynomials ⟺
SepCl_K(F) = F. Clark Prop 5.4: the irreducible purely inseparable monics are exactly
t^{p^a} − α with α ∈ F \ F^p. Clark Cor 5.15: **a purely inseparable extension is normal**
(immediate from the one-embedding characterisation). Clark Prop 5.17: separable ∧ purely
inseparable ⟹ trivial. Clark Prop 5.18: K/SepCl_K(F) is always purely inseparable; **the
dual is false** — K need not be separable over its purely inseparable closure (Clark
Example 5.16 gives a char-2 K/F with *no* nontrivial purely inseparable subextension, which
is the crisp asymmetry between the two notions). Clark Cor 5.20: K = F_sF_i does hold when
K/F is **normal**.

**All of the separability theory that mentions p is vacuous in char 0.** Precisely: in
char 0 every algebraic extension is separable, [K:F]_i = 1, purely inseparable = trivial,
perfect = all fields, Frobenius does not exist. Every statement above marked "char p" needs
that hypothesis; every statement not so marked holds in all characteristics.

### 2.3 The primitive element theorem — two genuinely different theorems

**(A) Finite-separable form** (Conrad `separable1` Thm 3.6; Ash 3.5.12; Milne Thm 5.1;
Clark Cor 7.3).
> Every finite separable extension is simple.
Milne's version is **sharper and worth adopting**: if E = F[α₁,…,α_r] is finite over F and
α₂,…,α_r are separable over F (α₁ need not be), then E = F[γ]. *Proof (Milne).* Reduce to
r = 2, E = F[α, β] with β separable. Let f, g be the minimal polynomials, α = α₁,…,α_s the
roots of f and β = β₁,…,β_t those of g in a splitting field L of fg containing E. For j ≠ 1
the equation α_i + Xβ_j = α + Xβ has the single solution X = (α_i − α)/(β − β_j); since F is
infinite, choose c ∈ F avoiding all of them, and set γ = α + cβ. Then g(X) and f(γ − cX)
both lie in F[γ][X] and have β as their **only** common root, so gcd = X − β computed in
L[X], which equals the gcd computed in F[γ][X] (Milne 2.17). Hence β ∈ F[γ], hence
α ∈ F[γ]. *Finite base fields are handled separately* by Milne 4.19: every extension of
finite fields is simple because E^× is cyclic. *Prerequisites:* minimal polynomials, gcd
stability under field extension, cyclicity of finite subgroups of K^×.

**(B) Finitely-many-intermediate-fields form** (Milne Prop 5.3 + Rem 5.4; Clark Thm 7.2 —
this is Clark's *actual* primitive element theorem, with (A) as a corollary; Lang FT
pp. 243–244 is Clark's source).
> For K/F finite: K/F is simple ⟺ there are only finitely many intermediate fields.
*(⟸)* over an infinite F: reduce to K = F[α,β]; among the infinitely many F[α + cβ] two
coincide, say for c₁ ≠ c₂, forcing (c₁ − c₂)β in that field, hence β, hence α. Over a
finite F everything is finite anyway. *(⟹)* K = F[γ] with minimal polynomial f; for each
intermediate E let g_E be the minimal polynomial of γ over E and E′ the subfield generated
by g_E's coefficients; then [K:E′] = [K:E] forces E = E′, so E ↦ g_E is injective into the
monic divisors of f in K[X], of which there are finitely many.

**Note the direction of dependence differs by source.** Conrad proves (A) directly and uses
it inside the proof of Artin's theorem 5.2/5.3; Clark proves (B) first and derives (A) via
the Alon–Tarsi lemma (a nonvanishing-polynomial argument giving the *generic* linear
combination Σs_ix_i, s_i drawn from any infinite subset S ⊆ F). Milne proves (A) directly
and (B) as a separate proposition. **A library that wants both should prove (B) and derive
(A) from Galois theory (finitely many subgroups ⟹ finitely many intermediate fields), which
is Milne's Rem 5.4 — but note this makes (A) depend on the correspondence, whereas Conrad's
proof of Artin uses (A). Watch for a circularity here.** Milne's direct proof of 5.1 breaks
the cycle and is the safe backbone.

### 2.4 Normality, splitting fields, the extension theorem

**Definitions.** Milne Def 3.7: E/F **algebraic** is normal if the minimal polynomial of
every element of E splits in E[X]. Clark Thm 4.16 gives four equivalents (for K/F
algebraic, F̄ an algebraic closure of K):
> (i) every F-embedding σ : K ↪ F̄ has σ(K) = K; (ii) K/F is the splitting field of a set
> S ⊆ F[t]; (iii) every irreducible f ∈ F[t] with a root in K splits in K;
> (iv) K contains every F-conjugate of each of its elements.
Conrad `galoiscorr` Thm 3.18 gives the finite case: normal ⟺ splitting field of *a single*
polynomial. **Note the set-vs-single distinction:** for infinite algebraic extensions you
need a set.

**Does normality presuppose algebraicity?** Milne's Def 3.7 says "algebraic and the minimal
polynomial …" (redundantly). Clark's Thm 4.16 is stated for algebraic K/F. Lang V §3 is
"Splitting fields and normal extensions" and also works algebraically. **Every source here
restricts normality to algebraic extensions.** Clark separately (§8.8, Thm 8.32) discusses
"a characterization of normal extensions" reaching further.

**Isomorphism-extension theorem.** Ash 3.2.5 "Isomorphism Extension Theorem"; Conrad
`splittingfields` Thm 3.1/4.1; Clark Thm 4.22 "The Extension Theorem"; Milne Prop 2.4 (simple
case) and Thm 6.6 (into an algebraic closure).
> σ : K → K′ a field isomorphism, f ∈ K[T], L a splitting field of f over K, L′ a splitting
> field of σf over K′. Then σ extends to an isomorphism L → L′, in at most [L:K] ways, with
> **exactly** [L:K] ways when f is separable (Conrad `splittingfields` Cor 4.2).
*Proof:* induct on [L:K], adjoining one root at a time, using Milne 2.4 (homs out of K(α)
into a target ↔ roots of σ(min poly) in the target). *Prerequisites:* K[X]/(π) ≅ K(α) and
uniqueness of the minimal polynomial.

**Isaacs' theorem** (Clark Thm 4.23, from Isaacs, Amer. Math. Monthly 87 (1980), 543–544):
for algebraic K₁/F, K₂/F, we have K₁ ≅_F K₂ ⟺ the sets of f ∈ F[t] having a root in K₁ and
in K₂ coincide. This implies Gilmer's theorem (Clark Thm 4.9) and hence Conrad's
`algclosure` Theorem 2. A striking, cheap, and citable result.

### 2.5 Existence of an algebraic closure, and exactly how much choice it needs

**Artin's construction** (Conrad `algclosure`; Milne "Third proof of the existence of
algebraic closures"; Clark §4.2 Thm 4.7; Lang V §2).
> Let P = the set of nonconstant monic polynomials in K[X], A = K[t_f]_{f∈P} the polynomial
> ring on one indeterminate per f ∈ P, and I = ({f(t_f) : f ∈ P}) ⊆ A.
> **Lemma.** I is a proper ideal. *Proof (choice-free):* an element of I involves finitely
> many f₁,…,f_n; build a finite extension L/K in which each f_i has a root (iterate
> K[X]/(irreducible factor)); the substitution A → L sending t_{f_i} ↦ a root of f_i and all
> other t_f ↦ 0 sends the element to 0, so it is not 1.
> Take a maximal (or merely prime) ideal m ⊇ I and set K′ = A/m. Every monic nonconstant
> f ∈ K[X] has the root t̄_f in K′, and K′ is algebraic over K.
> **Then no iteration is needed:** K′ is already algebraically closed.

The last sentence is the nontrivial part, and it is *exactly* Conrad's `algclosure`
**Theorem 2** = Milne **Prop 6.5** = Clark **Thm 4.9 (Gilmer)**:
> If L/K is algebraic and every nonconstant f ∈ K[X] has a *root* in L, then every
> nonconstant f ∈ L[X] has a root in L; so L is an algebraic closure of K.
*Proof.* First suppose K perfect. Let π ∈ K[X] be irreducible with splitting field K_π;
by the primitive element theorem K_π = K(α); let m be the minimal polynomial of α over K.
By hypothesis m has a root β ∈ L, and K(α) ≅_K K(β), so π splits in K(β) ⊆ L. Hence every
irreducible in K[X] splits in L, and then any π̃ ∈ L[X] divides some m ∈ K[X] which splits
in L, so π̃ splits. For general K in char p, replace K by
F = {x ∈ L : x^{p^n} ∈ K for some n}, show F is a perfect subfield of L (using that
X^{p^{n+1}} − a has a root in L and injectivity of Frobenius), show L is perfect, and show
every g ∈ F[X] has a root in L (raise coefficients to p^n, use the K-hypothesis, take a
p^n-th root in L = L^{p^n}). *Prerequisites:* primitive element theorem (perfect case),
Frobenius injectivity, perfect closure. **This is a nice, self-contained, buildable page.**

**Choice strength — the precise facts, with a real citation.** (Milne Rem 6.4, 6.7,
Warning 6.8; **Banaschewski, *Algebraic closure without choice*, Z. Math. Logik Grundlag.
Math. 38 (1992), 383–385**, abstract quoted below.)

- **Zorn's Lemma / full AC is *not* needed.** Banaschewski's abstract, verbatim:
  > "This note shows that for the proof of the existence and uniqueness of the algebraic
  > closure of a field one needs only the Boolean Ultrafilter Theorem."
  He notes BUT is *strictly weaker* than AC (citing Halpern), and that in Artin's
  construction "the proof that J is proper is choice-free, its main ingredient being the
  existence, for any finitely many u₁,…,u_n in K[x], of an extension of K in which each u_i
  has a zero. On the other hand, the existence of a maximal ideal P ⊇ J is based on Krull's
  Lemma" — and Krull's lemma for the *prime* case follows from BUT.
- **It is *not* provable in ZF alone.** Banaschewski again, verbatim:
  > "the existence of algebraic closures is indeed not provable in Zermelo-Fraenkel set
  > theory (ZF) alone: Läuchli [8] provided a model of ZF with urelements in which there is
  > a field without algebraic closure from which Pincus [10] then derived a model of ZF of
  > the same kind."
- **Uniqueness also fails in ZF.** Läuchli constructed an algebraic closure of ℚ in a
  choice-free model with no real-closed subfield; W. Hodges, *Läuchli's algebraic closure of
  ℚ*, Math. Proc. Cambridge Philos. Soc. (1976), works this out, and the consequence is that
  "there is, up to isomorphism, at most one algebraic closure of ℚ" is **not** a theorem of
  ZF.
- Milne's own accounting matches: Rem 6.4 says the ultrafilter principle is "all we shall
  need in this chapter"; his existence proofs use "a prime ideal … whose existence is ensured
  by the ultrafilter principle"; Thm 6.6 (uniqueness) is proved by Zorn but **Rem 6.7 gives
  a proof from the ultrafilter principle alone** (compactness of ∏_f H_f with the finite
  intersection property, H_f the finite nonempty set of F-isomorphisms between the splitting
  fields of f in Ω and Ω′).
- **Milne Warning 6.8 is the point to put in front of a reader:** "Even for a finite field
  F, there will exist uncountably many isomorphisms from one algebraic closure to a second,
  none of which is to be preferred over any other. Thus it is (uncountably) sloppy to say
  that *the* algebraic closure of F is unique."
- **Countable fields need no choice at all.** Milne, opening of Ch. 6: if F is countable,
  list the monic irreducibles f₁, f₂, …, set E₀ = F, E_i = a splitting field of f_i over
  E_{i−1}, and take Ω = ⋃E_i. Similarly Milne Prop 4.24 constructs 𝔽̄_p explicitly as
  ⋃𝔽_{p^{i!}} — with Rem 4.25 noting that even *forming that union* needs the Axiom of Union
  or an explicit quotient-by-∼ construction on a disjoint union.
- Milne Aside 7.31: Zorn's lemma is needed for Prop 7.29 (Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) has nonopen finite-index
  subgroups) — "it is consistent with ZF+DC (dependent choice)" that it fails.

**Recommendation to the scaffolder.** The library's choice-strength ledger should say:
Artin's construction of Ω needs *a prime ideal containing a proper ideal*, i.e. BPIT/BUT,
not Zorn; the uniqueness-up-to-(non-canonical)-isomorphism also needs only BUT; neither is
a ZF theorem; and the standard textbook proofs (Milne Thm 6.6, Conrad `zorn2` Thm 1.1)
*use* Zorn because it is convenient, not because it is necessary.

### 2.6 The fundamental theorem of Galois theory

**Definition of Galois — the flashpoint.** Four conditions, equivalent **for finite
extensions**:
> (a) |Aut(L/K)| = [L:K]; (b) L^{Aut(L/K)} = K; (c) L/K is normal and separable;
> (d) L is the splitting field over K of a **separable** polynomial in K[X].
Conrad `galoiscorr` Thm 4.1 and Def 4.4 (adopts these as the definition); Clark Thm 8.10
("Omnibus Theorem"); Milne Thm 3.10 (with (c) as Def 3.9 and (b) restated as
"E finite over F and F = E^{Aut(E/F)}", plus the extra equivalent "F = E^G for *some* finite
group G of automorphisms of E"); Ash 6.1.4/6.1.5 + 6.2.1; Lang VI §1.
- **These are NOT equivalent without finiteness.** Clark makes the distinction explicit:
  he calls K/F **weakly Galois** if K^{Aut(K/F)} = F, and **Galois** if
  K^{Aut(K/L)} = L for *every* intermediate L. He notes "the terminology 'weakly Galois' is
  not standard" and that for algebraic extensions the two coincide (Clark Thm 8.29/8.32),
  but for transcendental ones they need not: Clark Prop 8.7 shows F(t)/F is weakly Galois
  ⟺ F is infinite, and Clark Ex 8.5 asserts ℂ/ℚ and ℂ/ℚ̄ are Galois in his sense.
  **Milne restricts "Galois" to finite by definition (Def 3.9), then re-defines it for
  infinite algebraic extensions in Ch. 7.** Conrad restricts to finite (Def 4.4).
- Milne's equivalence "(c) F = E^G for some finite group G of automorphisms" is the one that
  makes Artin's theorem a corollary and is worth carrying.
- **Gal notation.** Milne, Conrad, Clark all write `Gal(E/F)` only when E/F is Galois, and
  `Aut(E/F)` otherwise. Ash 6.1.1 writes `Gal(E/F)` for the automorphism group of an
  arbitrary extension E/F ("Let G = Gal(E/F) be the Galois group of the extension E/F"),
  which is a genuine divergence — **Ash's Galois group of a non-Galois extension is just
  Aut**. The library should follow Milne/Conrad/Clark and reserve Gal.
- Conrad Cor 4.2: for any finite L/K, |Aut(L/K)| **divides** [L:K]; Cor 4.3: strict
  inequality exactly when L/K is inseparable or not normal.

**Fundamental theorem** — I give Conrad `galoiscorr` Thm 5.6 verbatim in structure since it
is the most complete, then note Milne's extra clause.
> **(Galois.)** Let L/K be a finite Galois extension, G = Gal(L/K). The inclusion-reversing
> maps F ↦ Gal(L/F) and H ↦ L^H between intermediate fields of L/K and subgroups of G are
> mutually inverse, and when F and H correspond (F = L^H, H = Gal(L/F)):
> (a) |H| = [L:F] and [F:K] = [G:H];
> (b) two intermediate fields F, F′ with subgroups H, H′ are K-isomorphic ⟺ H and H′ are
>     conjugate in G; in particular Gal(L/σ(F)) = σGal(L/F)σ^{-1} for σ ∈ G;
> (c) F/K is Galois ⟺ H ⊴ G, in which case the restriction map G → Gal(F/K), σ ↦ σ|_F, is
>     **surjective with kernel H**, so G/H ≅ Gal(F/K).
Milne Thm 3.17 states the same with (a) order-reversal, (b) (H₁ : H₂) = [E^{H₂} : E^{H₁}],
(c) the conjugation clause, (d) H ⊴ G ⟺ E^H normal (hence Galois) over F, with
Gal(E^H/F) ≅ G/H. Clark Thm 8.20 embeds it in an abstract Galois-connection framework
(§8.3: closed sets, Prop 8.13 σG_Yσ^{-1} = G_{σY}, Cor 8.16 normal ⟺ stable), then proves
that for K/F finite Galois **every** subgroup is closed.

**Two proof routes, and which is cheaper.**
- **Route 1 — Artin + counting** (Milne, Clark, Ash). Prove Artin's [E:E^G] = |G|
  (Milne Thm 3.4 + Cor 3.5 + Cor 3.11). Then: for H ≤ G, Gal(E/E^H) = H is *immediately*
  Artin. For intermediate M, E/M is Galois (Milne Cor 3.13: E is the splitting field of the
  same separable f over M), so E^{Gal(E/M)} = M. That is the bijection in two lines.
  (b)/(a) follow from Artin + degree multiplicativity; (c) from τ fixes M ⟺ στσ^{-1} fixes
  σM; (d) from the restriction homomorphism G → Aut(E^H/F) with kernel H and
  (E^H)^{G/H} = F, invoking Thm 3.10(c). **Prerequisites: Artin's theorem, degree
  multiplicativity, and the fact that E/F Galois ⟹ E/M Galois. Nothing else.**
- **Route 2 — embedding counting.** Prove #{K-embeddings L → K̄} = [L:K]_s, characterise
  Galois by [L:K]_s = [L:K] and stability of the image, and read the correspondence off the
  orbit/stabiliser structure. This is Clark's underlying machinery (Thm 8.10's proof of
  (iv) ⟺ (ii)) and Conrad's `separable1` Thm 3.8.

**Route 1 is cleaner to build with no extra machinery.** It needs neither an algebraic
closure nor separable degree — Milne gets the whole finite theory in Ch. 3 using only Ch. 2,
and pushes algebraic closures to Ch. 6. **This ordering (finite Galois theory *before*
algebraic closures) is a genuine structural choice and I recommend it**, because the
algebraic-closure page then depends on the primitive element theorem, not the reverse.
Clark takes the opposite order (closures in Ch. 4, Galois in Ch. 8) and must therefore carry
the closure existence early, with its choice apparatus.

**Left/right action convention.** All four sources compose automorphisms as maps and write
σ(x); Ash 6.1.1 explicitly warns "Throughout the chapter, the composition τ ∘ σ of two
automorphisms will be written as a product τσ." The correspondence is order-*reversing* in
every source. No source in this harvest uses a right action or writes x^σ. Milne 3.17(c)
writes E^{σHσ^{-1}} = σ(E^H); Clark Prop 8.13(b) writes σX^H = X^{σHσ^{-1}} — same content.

**Companion results worth their own items.**
- **Natural irrationalities** (Milne Prop 3.19; Clark Thm 8.12): E/F Galois, L/F any
  extension inside a common field ⟹ EL/L is Galois and σ ↦ σ|_E : Gal(EL/L) → Gal(E/E∩L)
  is an isomorphism. Cor: [EL:F] = [E:F][L:F]/[E∩L:F] (Milne 3.20) when L/F is finite.
- **Composites** (Milne Prop 3.21): Gal(E₁E₂/F) ≅ Gal(E₁/F) ×_{Gal(E₁∩E₂/F)} Gal(E₂/F).
- **Milne Rem 3.18(a)**: Gal(E/M₁⋯M_r) = ⋂Gal(E/M_i); (b) the Galois/normal closure of M in
  E corresponds to the normal core ⋂_σ σHσ^{-1}.
- **Conrad `galoiscorr` Thm 6.1**: for L/K finite Galois and γ ∈ L, [K(γ):K] = the index of
  the stabiliser of γ — the practical tool for finding primitive elements (Milne Rem 5.2:
  γ = α₁ + c₂α₂ + ⋯ is primitive iff moved by every nontrivial element of G).

### 2.7 Finite fields

(Milne 4.19–4.25; Conrad `finitefields`; Ash 6.4; Clark; Lang V §5; Isaacs Ch. 21;
D&F §14.3; Artin §15.7.)

- **𝔽_q^× is cyclic.** Conrad `finitefields` Lem 1.6, `cyclotomic` Thm 1.1 (stated for any
  finite subgroup of K^× for K a field), Conrad `separable1` Lem 3.9, Milne Exercise 1-3.
  *Proof:* a finite abelian group in which x^m = 1 has at most m solutions for every m is
  cyclic; the "at most m roots" step **needs K to be a field (an integral domain)** — see
  §4. Conrad's Appendix A proves the group-theoretic input (the maximal order in a finite
  abelian group is a multiple of every element order).
- **Existence.** 𝔽_{p^n} = the splitting field of X^{p^n} − X over 𝔽_p; the set of roots is
  a subfield because (a−b)^q = a^q − b^q and X^{p^n} − X is separable (derivative = −1).
  Milne Prop 4.20; Conrad Thm 2.2.
- **Uniqueness.** Any field with q = p^n elements is a splitting field of X^q − X over 𝔽_p
  (every element satisfies it, by Lagrange in E^×), so any two are isomorphic.
  Milne Prop 4.20; Conrad Thm 2.7.
- **Frobenius and the Galois group.** 𝔽_q/𝔽_p is Galois with Gal cyclic of order n generated
  by σ : a ↦ a^p, since 𝔽_q^{⟨σ⟩} = {a : a^p = a} = 𝔽_p and then Artin's theorem.
  Milne Prop 4.20; Conrad Thm 4.1; general base: Conrad Thm 5.6, Gal(𝔽_{q^n}/𝔽_q) = ⟨x ↦ x^q⟩
  cyclic of order n.
- **Subfield lattice.** Milne Cor 4.21 and Prop 4.23: 𝔽_{p^m} ⊆ 𝔽_{p^n} ⟺ m | n; exactly one
  subfield of each such order; the poset of finite subfields of 𝔽̄_p ≅ (ℤ_{≥1}, |).
  Conrad Thm 2.8, Thm 5.2. *This is a direct read-off of the subgroup lattice of a cyclic
  group under the correspondence — a good showcase item.*
- **Every extension of finite fields is simple** (Milne 4.19) — take a generator of E^×.
- **Counting irreducibles.** Conrad `rootirred` Thm 6.1 (Gauss):
  X^{p^n} − X = ∏_{d|n} ∏_{π monic irred, deg π = d} π(X), each factor to the first power.
  Taking degrees: **p^n = Σ_{d|n} d·N_p(d)**, and by Möbius inversion
  **N_q(n) = (1/n) Σ_{d|n} μ(d) q^{n/d}**.
  **Does it need Möbius inversion?** For the *general* closed form, yes — Conrad explicitly
  says "A formula for N_p(n) … can be derived from (6.1) using the Möbius inversion formula,
  which we omit." For particular n it does not: Conrad Ex 6.3 gives N_p(2) = (p²−p)/2,
  N_p(3) = (p³−p)/3, N_p(12) = (p¹²−p⁶−p⁴+p²)/12 by hand. So the library can prove the
  *recursion* p^n = Σ_{d|n} dN_p(d) with pure Galois theory, and the closed form only after
  it has Möbius inversion available (a number-theory or combinatorics dependency).
  **The mere existence of an irreducible of every degree n over 𝔽_q** does NOT need
  inversion: Conrad Thm 5.1 gets it from the existence of 𝔽_{q^n} plus simplicity.
- **Trace and norm on finite fields.** Conrad `finitefields` Thm 4.6 / Thm 5.7:
  Tr_{𝔽_{q^n}/𝔽_q}(a) = a + a^q + ⋯ + a^{q^{n-1}}, N(a) = a^{1+q+⋯+q^{n-1}} = a^{(q^n−1)/(q−1)};
  both are surjective, and N : 𝔽_{q^n}^× → 𝔽_q^× is surjective (Clark Cor 6.8).
- **Dedekind's theorem** (Milne Thm 4.28): for monic f ∈ ℤ[X] and p ∤ D(f), the factorization
  type of f mod p is the cycle type of a Frobenius element in G_f ⊆ S_m. The practical tool
  for computing Galois groups over ℚ (Milne "Computing Galois groups over ℚ"; D&F §14.8).

### 2.8 Cyclotomic extensions

(Milne 5.8–5.13; Conrad `cyclotomic`; Clark §9.1; Ash 6.5; Lang VI §3; D&F §13.6, §14.5;
Artin §16.10; Isaacs Ch. 20.)

**Setup.** Milne Prop 5.8. Let char F ∤ n and E = splitting field of X^n − 1 over F. Then
(a) μ_n(E) is cyclic of order n so a primitive nth root ζ exists; (b) E = F[ζ]; (c) E/F is
Galois and σ ↦ [i] with σζ = ζ^i is an **injective** homomorphism Gal(E/F) ↪ (ℤ/n)^×,
independent of the choice of ζ. *The map need not be surjective:* Milne notes F = ℂ gives
image {1} and F = ℝ gives {±1}; Conrad `cyclotomic` Thm 2.8/2.10 computes the image over
𝔽_q as the cyclic subgroup ⟨q mod n⟩.

**Φ_n.** Φ_n(X) = ∏(X − ζ) over primitive nth roots of 1; deg Φ_n = φ(n);
X^n − 1 = ∏_{d|n}Φ_d(X), which gives the inductive computation. **Integrality:** X^n − 1 is
monic in ℤ[X], so every monic factor in ℚ[X] lies in ℤ[X] (Milne Prop 1.14 / Gauss's lemma),
hence Φ_n ∈ ℤ[X] (Conrad Thm 5.2, Clark).

**Irreducibility over ℚ — the standard proof, stated precisely** (Milne Thm 5.10; Clark
Thm 9.8 "Gauss–Kronecker"; both are the Dedekind 1857 argument).
> Let f be a monic irreducible factor of Φ_n in ℚ[X], necessarily in ℤ[X]. It suffices to
> show: ζ a root of f ⟹ ζ^p is a root of f for every prime p ∤ n (since any i coprime to n
> is a product of such primes). Suppose not: write Φ_n = fg, then ζ is a root of g(X^p), so
> f and g(X^p) share a root, hence a nontrivial common factor in ℚ[X] (Milne 2.17), hence
> in ℤ[X]. Reduce mod p: f̄ and ḡ(X^p) = ḡ(X)^p share a factor of degree ≥ 1 in 𝔽_p[X]
> (the "freshman's dream" a^p = a on 𝔽_p), so f̄ and ḡ share a factor, so X^n − 1 has a
> multiple root in 𝔽̄_p. But p ∤ n makes X^n − 1 separable over 𝔽_p. Contradiction.
*Prerequisites:* Gauss's lemma / monic factors of a monic ℤ-polynomial; gcd stability;
Frobenius on 𝔽_p[X]; the derivative criterion. Clark's write-up is the same argument phrased
via "let q be an irreducible factor of f̄; then q | ḡ^p so q | ḡ, so q² | Φ̄_n, contradicting
separability".
Milne also gives an **alternative proof** via Frobenius elements at unramified primes
(needs algebraic number theory — out of scope). Conrad `cyclotomic` Thm 5.3 gives the
easier partial result that Φ_{p^r}(T+1) is Eisenstein at p, which handles prime-power n
elementarily; Milne Lem 1.42 does the n = p case that way.

**Consequence.** Gal(ℚ(ζ_n)/ℚ) ≅ (ℤ/n)^× (Milne Lem 5.9 + Thm 5.10; Conrad Thm 2.5;
Clark Cor 9.9). Consequences worth items: every finite abelian group is a Galois group over
ℚ (Clark Cor 9.12, via the subgroup structure of (ℤ/n)^× and Dirichlet-free Lemma 9.11);
there are infinitely many primes p ≡ 1 mod n (Clark Thm 9.10, by Φ_n's prime divisors);
ℚ(μ_m) ∩ ℚ(μ_n) = ℚ(μ_{(m,n)}) (Conrad Thm 3.4); ℚ(μ_m) = ℚ(μ_n) ⟺ m = n or {m,n} = {odd k,
2k} (Conrad Thm 4.1).

**Constructibility.** Milne Thm 5.12: the regular n-gon is constructible ⟺ n = 2^k p₁⋯p_s
with distinct Fermat primes p_i. Ash §6.8 problems 4–9 give the same. Depends on Milne
Thm 3.24 / Cor 1.38 (constructible ⟹ degree a power of 2, plus the Galois converse).

**Kronecker–Weber — statement only, flagged out of scope.**
> Every finite abelian extension of ℚ is contained in a cyclotomic field ℚ(ζ_n).
Milne FT states it only in a footnote to Exercise 3-2: "The Kronecker-Weber theorem says
that every abelian extension of ℚ is contained in a cyclotomic extension." **It is not
proved anywhere in FT.** Milne's *Class Field Theory* notes
(`https://www.jmilne.org/math/CourseNotes/CFT.pdf`) state and prove it (they also carry
"The Local Kronecker–Weber Theorem" as a numbered section). **Proving it requires class
field theory or at minimum ramification theory of local fields — far out of scope for a
Galois-theory level.** Record it as a `rem-` item with `proved_here: false` if wanted at
all, or omit.

### 2.9 Kummer theory, Hilbert 90, Artin–Schreier

**Hilbert's Theorem 90.** Two statements, and the library should carry both.
- **Cohomological / Noether form** (Milne Thm 5.23): E/F Galois with group G ⟹
  H¹(G, E^×) = 0, i.e. every crossed homomorphism f : G → E^× (f(στ) = f(σ)·σ(f(τ))) is
  principal (f(σ) = σγ/γ). *Proof:* by independence of characters (Milne Cor 5.15),
  Σ_{τ∈G} f(τ)τ is not the zero map on E, so pick α with β := Σ_τ f(τ)τα ≠ 0; then
  σβ = f(σ)^{-1}β, so f(σ) = β/σβ. *Prerequisites: only Dedekind independence.*
  Milne's Notes: "The corollary is Satz 90 in Hilbert, *Theorie der Algebraischen
  Zahlkörper*, 1897. The theorem was discovered by Kummer in the special case of
  ℚ[ζ_p]/ℚ, and generalized to Theorem 5.23 by Emmy Noether."
- **Elementary / cyclic form** (Milne Cor 5.25; Conrad `linearchar` Thm 4.1;
  Clark Thm 8.28; Ash §6.7): E/F **cyclic** with Gal = ⟨σ⟩, α ∈ E^×. Then
  **N_{E/F}(α) = 1 ⟺ α = β/σβ for some β ∈ E^×**, and **Tr_{E/F}(α) = 0 ⟺ α = σβ − β**.
  Conrad proves both directly by an explicit "resolvent" f(x) = αx + ασ(α)σ(x) + ⋯ (norm
  case) / f(x) = αx + (α+σα)σ(x) + ⋯ (trace case), using character independence to find x
  with f(x) ≠ 0; the additive case additionally needs **Tr ≢ 0**, which needs separability
  (Clark Thm 6.10). He also reproves the additive form from the normal basis theorem.
  **The additive form is the engine of Artin–Schreier theory and is often omitted;
  include it.**
  Milne's Exercise 5-2 uses the multiplicative form on ℚ[i]/ℚ to classify Pythagorean
  triples; Conrad works the same example out.

**Cyclic extensions with μ_n ⊆ F.** (Milne Prop 5.27; Conrad `linearchar` Thm 5.1 + 5.5;
Clark Thm 9.17 + Prop 9.18; Ash 6.7.2 + 6.7.3; Lang VI §6.)
> Let F contain a primitive nth root of unity ζ (so char F ∤ n).
> (⟸) If E = F[α] with α^n ∈ F and no smaller power of α in F, then E/F is Galois with
> **cyclic** group of order n, via the injective χ_a : σ ↦ σα/α ∈ μ_n, independent of the
> choice of nth root; surjectivity comes from minimality of n.
> (⟹) If E/F is cyclic of degree n, then E = F[α] with α^n ∈ F. *Proof:* σ a generator;
> by Dedekind independence Σ_{i=0}^{n-1} ζ^i σ^i is not the zero function, so there is γ with
> α := Σζ^iσ^iγ ≠ 0; then σα = ζ^{-1}α, so α^n ∈ F and no smaller power is.
> **Degree formula** (Conrad Thm 5.5, Clark 9.18b): [K(ⁿ√a) : K] = the order of a in
> K^×/(K^×)^n.
> **Uniqueness** (Milne Prop 5.28; Conrad Thm 5.9): F[a^{1/n}] = F[b^{1/n}] ⟺ a = b^rc^n with
> (r,n) = 1, c ∈ F^× ⟺ a and b generate the same subgroup of F^×/F^{×n}.
Ash 6.7.3 proves the (⟹) direction identically, with a explicitly = θ^n.

**Kummer theory proper.** (Milne Thm 5.30; Conrad `linearchar` Thm 5.12; Ash 6.7.5 —
**Ash uses the word "Kummer extension" for any finite abelian Galois extension**, a
different and looser convention than Conrad's Def 5.7, see §3.)
> **(Milne 5.30.)** Fix F containing a primitive nth root of 1 and an algebraic closure Ω.
> E ↦ B(E) := F^× ∩ E^{×n} is a bijection
> {finite abelian extensions of F of exponent n inside Ω} ↔ {subgroups B of F^× containing
> F^{×n} with finite index}, inverse B ↦ F[B^{1/n}], and [E:F] = (B : F^{×n}).
*Proof:* from 1 → μ_n → E^× → E^{×n} → 1 and Hilbert 90 one gets
F^× ∩ E^{×n} / F^{×n} ≅ Hom(G, μ_n), and for G abelian of exponent n, |Hom(G,μ_n)| = |G|;
then a two-sided inequality argument forces equality throughout.
Milne Rem 5.32: there is a **perfect pairing**
(B(E)/F^{×n}) × Gal(E/F) → μ_n, (a,σ) ↦ σa^{1/n}/a^{1/n}.
Conrad's Thm 5.12 is the concrete version: L/K abelian with μ_n ⊆ K and exp Gal = n ⟺
L = K(ⁿ√a₁,…,ⁿ√a_m). *Prerequisites for the cohomological version:* group cohomology H¹ and
the long exact sequence; Conrad's and Ash's versions avoid cohomology entirely (Ash 6.7.5
decomposes the abelian group into cyclic factors and applies 6.7.3 to each fixed field).
**Recommendation: build Conrad/Ash's cohomology-free version; the H¹ version can be a later
remark once the library has group cohomology.**

**Irreducibility of X^n − a.** (Milne Aside 5.29 citing Lang VI §9 Thm 9.1 p. 297;
Clark Thm 9.21; Conrad `simpleradical` Thm 2.1, 3.1.)
> **X^n − a is irreducible in F[X] if a ∉ F^p for every prime p | n, and additionally
> a ∉ −4F⁴ when 4 | n.** The 4|n exception is real and is the classic trap
> (e.g. X⁴ + 4 = (X²−2X+2)(X²+2X+2) over ℚ, and −4 = −4·1⁴).
> Prime case (Conrad `simpleradical` Thm 3.1): for p prime and a ∈ K^×, T^p − a is
> irreducible ⟺ a ∉ K^p. Clark's proof of the prime case uses a norm argument:
> if α has degree d < p then N(α)^p = a^d, and gcd(d,p) = 1 gives a ∈ F^p.

**Artin–Schreier extensions in characteristic p.** (Conrad `linearchar` Thm 5.13;
Clark Thm 9.25 + 9.26 + Prop 9.27; Milne Aside 5.29(b); Ash §6.7 problems 8–13;
Lang VI §6.) Write ℘(x) = x^p − x (an additive homomorphism F → F with kernel 𝔽_p).
> **Existence.** char F = p, a ∈ F. Then X^p − X − a is irreducible in F[X] ⟺ a ∉ ℘(F).
> When irreducible, its splitting field F(α) (α any root) is **cyclic of degree p**, with
> generator α ↦ α + 1; the full root set is {α + i : i ∈ 𝔽_p}.
> **Converse.** Every cyclic extension of degree p in characteristic p is F(℘^{-1}(a)) for
> some a ∈ F \ ℘(F). *Proof:* Tr(−1) = −p = 0, so by the **additive** Hilbert 90 there is
> α ∈ K with σ(α) = α + 1; then a := ℘(α) ∈ F and K = F(α).
> **Uniqueness** (Clark Prop 9.27): K(℘^{-1}(a)) = K(℘^{-1}(b)) ⟺ a and b generate the same
> cyclic subgroup of K/℘(K).
> **Over a finite field** (Conrad Ex 5.15): X^p − X − a has a root in 𝔽_q ⟺
> Tr_{𝔽_q/𝔽_p}(a) = 0.
Clark's Thm 9.25 proof of irreducibility is a nice degree argument: all roots generate the
same field, so all irreducible factors have the same degree d, and dr = p forces d = p.

### 2.10 Solvable by radicals — and the definitions differ

**Milne's definition** (Ch. 3, before Thm 3.28):
> f(X) = 0 is *solvable in radicals* if there is a tower F = F₀ ⊆ F₁ ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ F_m with
> (a) F_i = F_{i−1}[α_i] with α_i^{m_i} ∈ F_{i−1}, and (b) F_m contains a splitting field
> for f.
No requirement that any F_i/F_{i−1} be Galois; no requirement that roots of unity be present.

**Ash's definition** (6.8.1): the same tower, but he then says "It is convenient (and legal)
to assume that n₁ = ⋯ = n_r = n" (replace each n_i by the product), calls E a **radical
extension**, and defines f solvable by radicals ⟺ f splits over *some* radical extension of
F. He records: "A radical extension is always finite, but it need not be normal or
separable."

**Clark** would have defined it in §9.8, which is an **empty stub**. Do not cite Clark here.

**Lang** VI §7 is "Solvable and radical extensions"; **D&F** §14.7 is "Solvable and Radical
Extensions: Insolvability of the Quintic"; **Artin** §16.12 "Quintic Equations";
**Isaacs** Ch. 22 "Roots, Radicals, and Real Numbers".

**Galois's theorem.**
> **(Milne Thm 3.28 / Thm 5.34; Ash 6.8.4.)** Let F have characteristic **0** and f ∈ F[X].
> Then f = 0 is solvable in radicals ⟺ the Galois group G_f is a solvable group.

*Proof of ⟸ (Milne 5.34).* G_f solvable. Let F′ = F[ζ] for ζ a primitive nth root of 1 with
n = (deg f)!. Base change shrinks the Galois group (Milne Lem 5.33: Gal over F′ embeds in
Gal over F), so G = Gal over F′ is solvable, with G = G₀ ⊳ G₁ ⊳ ⋯ ⊳ G_m = 1 and cyclic
quotients. Let E be a splitting field over F′, F_i = E^{G_i}. Each F_i/F_{i−1} is cyclic,
and μ_n ⊆ F′, so Prop 5.27 gives F_i = F_{i−1}[α_i] with α_i^{[F_i:F_{i−1}]} ∈ F_{i−1}.
Done.
*Proof of ⟹ (Milne 5.34).* Given a radical tower F = F₀ ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ F_m ⊇ splitting field. Let
n = r₁⋯r_m and let Ω/F be Galois containing F_m and a primitive nth root ζ. Let Ẽ = the
Galois closure of F_m[ζ] in Ω. By Milne 3.18(a), Ẽ is the compositum of the σF_m[ζ], so is
generated over F by ζ and all the σα_i. Adjoin them one at a time: each step adjoins an rth
root of an element of the previous field, so by Prop 5.8 (cyclotomic, abelian) and Prop 5.27
(cyclic once ζ is present) each step is abelian. So Ẽ/F is a solvable extension and G_f is
a quotient of Gal(Ẽ/F).
*Ash's route (6.8.3–6.8.4)* is the same but organised as a lemma "the normal closure of a
radical extension is radical" (Prop 6.8.2) plus a diamond-diagram application of natural
irrationalities. Ash's write-up is the more careful one about **where the roots of unity get
adjoined** and is worth following.

**Characteristic p fails, for two separate reasons** (Milne Rem 3.29):
> (a) f need not be separable, so it need not have a Galois group at all;
> (b) X^p − X − a = 0 need not be solvable in radicals even though it is separable with
> abelian (cyclic of order p) Galois group.
> "If the definition of solvable is changed to allow extensions defined by polynomials of the
> type in (b) in the chain, then the theorem holds for fields F of characteristic p ≠ 0 and
> separable f ∈ F[X]."
**This is a hard hypothesis the library must state.**

**Insolvability of the quintic — concrete witnesses.**
- **Criterion** (Milne Prop 4.16; Conrad `galoisaspermgp` Thm 3.2): f irreducible of prime
  degree p in ℚ[X] that splits in ℂ with **exactly two nonreal roots** ⟹ G_f = S_p.
  *Proof:* p | [E:ℚ] = |G_f| gives an element of order p, which in S_p must be a p-cycle
  (Cauchy + p prime); complex conjugation is a transposition; and S_p is generated by any
  transposition and any p-cycle (Milne Lem 4.15, whose proof uses p prime twice).
- **Concrete quintics with G = S₅ and exactly 3 real roots** (I verified the arithmetic):
  - **f(X) = X⁵ − 6X + 3.** Eisenstein at 3 (3 | −6, 3 | 3, 9 ∤ 3) ⟹ irreducible.
    f′ = 5X⁴ − 6 vanishes at X = ±(6/5)^{1/4} ≈ ±1.0466; f(1.0466) ≈ −2.02 < 0 and
    f(−1.0466) ≈ +8.02 > 0, so the local max is positive and the local min negative ⟹
    exactly 3 real roots, hence exactly 2 nonreal. **G_f = S₅, not solvable.**
  - **f(X) = X⁵ − 4X + 2.** Eisenstein at 2. f′ = 5X⁴ − 4 at X = ±(4/5)^{1/4} ≈ ±0.9457;
    f(0.9457) ≈ −1.03 < 0, f(−0.9457) ≈ +5.03 > 0 ⟹ exactly 3 real roots. **G_f = S₅.**
  - **Ash's example (6.8.5): f(X) = X⁵ − 10X⁴ + 2.** Eisenstein at 2. f′ = 5X³(X − 8);
    critical points 0 and 8, f(0) = 2 > 0, f(8) = −8190 < 0 ⟹ exactly 3 real roots.
    **G_f = S₅.** Ash asserts G = S₅ by his §6.6 Problem 3.
  - Milne's Example 4.17 constructs an infinite family for every prime p ≥ 5:
    g(X) = (X² + m)(X − n₁)⋯(X − n_{p−2}) with m > 0 even and n₁ < ⋯ < n_{p−2} even, and
    f(X) = g(X) − 2/n for odd positive n with 2/n < min_{g′(x)=0}|g(x)|; then nf(X) is
    Eisenstein at 2 and f has exactly two nonreal roots.
- **Milne Rem 4.18 is the counterexample seed:** X⁵ − 5X³ + 4X − 1 has Galois group S₅ but
  **all roots real** — "the reader shouldn't think that, in order to have Galois group S_p, a
  polynomial must have exactly two nonreal roots."
- **The general quintic.** Milne Thm 5.38–5.40: the general polynomial of degree n over F
  has Galois group S_n, i.e. F(X₁,…,X_n)/F(p₁,…,p_n) is Galois with group S_n
  (Cor 5.39). Ash §6.8 Problems 10–11. **This is the statement that says there is no general
  radical formula, and Ash makes the point explicitly:** "The significance of Galois'
  solvability theorem is not simply that there are some examples of bad polynomials. The key
  point is there is no general method for solving a polynomial equation over the rationals by
  radicals, if the degree of the polynomial is 5 or more."
- Recognising S_n / A_n more generally: Conrad `galoisSnAn` Thm 2.1 (transitive + a
  transposition + a p-cycle with p > n/2 ⟹ S_n) and Thm 2.2 (the A_n analogue with a
  3-cycle). Milne Lem 4.31 (transitive H ⊆ S_n with a transposition and an (n−1)-cycle).

### 2.11 The Galois-theoretic (Artin) proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra

(Milne Thm 5.6; Conrad `galoisappn` Thm 2.1; Artin *Algebra* §15.10.)

**Exact list of non-algebraic inputs.** Conrad states them as four numbered facts:
> (1) Every odd-degree polynomial in ℝ[X] has a real root; in particular no irreducible in
>     ℝ[X] has odd degree > 1. *(Intermediate value theorem.)*
> (2) Every complex number has a square root in ℂ. *(Polar form, or the explicit algebraic
>     formula below.)*
> (3) The first Sylow theorem (existence of Sylow subgroups). *(Pure group theory.)*
> (4) A nontrivial finite p-group has a subgroup of index p. *(Pure group theory.)*
Milne compresses (1)+(2) to: "positive real numbers have square roots" and "every polynomial
of odd degree with real coefficients has a real root", both immediate from the IVT — and
then **derives (2) algebraically from the first**: for α = a + bi, choose real c, d with
c² = (a + √(a²+b²))/2 and d² = (−a + √(a²+b²))/2, and signs so that cd has the sign of b;
then (c + di)² = α. **This is the cleanest split: the only analytic input is the
intermediate value theorem, used twice — for square roots of nonnegative reals and for odd-
degree real polynomials.** Everything else is algebra and finite group theory.

**Proof skeleton** (Milne's phrasing; Conrad's is the same up to the enlargement step).
1. Define ℂ = ℝ[X]/(X² + 1) = ℝ[i]. Show every α ∈ ℂ has a square root in ℂ (algebraic
   formula above).
2. It suffices to show every f ∈ ℝ[X] splits in ℂ (Milne 1.45). Let E be a splitting field
   of f(X)(X² + 1) over ℝ. Since char ℝ = 0, the polynomial is separable, so E/ℝ is Galois
   (Milne Thm 3.10). Let G = Gal(E/ℝ), H a Sylow 2-subgroup.
3. M = E^H has odd degree (G : H) over ℝ. Every α ∈ M has odd degree over ℝ (degree
   multiplicativity), so its minimal polynomial has odd degree, so has a real root, so is
   linear by irreducibility. Hence M = ℝ and **G = H is a 2-group**.
4. So Gal(E/ℂ) is a 2-group of order 2^{m−1}. If nontrivial it has a subgroup N of index 2
   (fact (4)), and E^N is a **quadratic** extension of ℂ, hence of the form ℂ(√d) for
   d ∈ ℂ^× not a square (Milne Lem 3.25, char ≠ 2). But every element of ℂ has a square root
   in ℂ. Contradiction. Hence Gal(E/ℂ) = 1 and E = ℂ.
5. So every f ∈ ℝ[X] splits in ℂ; ℂ is algebraically closed (Milne 1.45), and
   **Cor 5.7:** ℂ = ℝ̄, and the algebraic numbers form an algebraic closure of ℚ.
Conrad's step 2 differs: he takes an arbitrary finite E/ℂ, notes E/ℝ is finite and char 0,
and *enlarges* E/ℝ to a finite Galois K/ℝ; then [K:ℝ] is even because ℝ ⊆ ℂ ⊆ K. Same
conclusion.

*Prerequisites, itemised for the library:* degree multiplicativity; splitting fields exist;
char-0 separability; the fundamental theorem of Galois theory (only the correspondence and
degree clause); Milne Lem 3.25 (a quadratic extension in char ≠ 2 is F(√d)); Sylow I; and
"a nontrivial finite p-group has a subgroup of index p" (equivalently, finite p-groups are
solvable / have a central series). **Note: the two group-theory inputs are the only
dependencies outside field theory, and both belong to a group-theory level.**

Milne's Notes: "The elegant argument given here is a simplification by Emil Artin of earlier
proofs (see Emil Artin, *Algebraische Konstruction reeller Körper*, Hamb. Abh., Bd. 5 (1926),
85–90; translation available in *Exposition by Emil Artin*, AMS/LMS 2007)." Milne also
footnotes that this is "not strictly a theorem in algebra: it is a statement about ℝ whose
construction is part of analysis" — the right honesty note for a reader.

**Related.** Conrad `artinschreier` Thm 3.1: if K̄/K is a finite nontrivial extension then
[K̄:K] = 2 and K is real closed (the *other* Artin–Schreier theorem); Cor 3.2: a subfield
K ⊆ ℝ with [ℝ:K] < ∞ must be ℝ. Clark Thm 4.3 states a purely algebraic criterion for
K[√−1] to be algebraically closed, proved as his Thm 14.31.

### 2.12 Norm, trace, discriminant, cubics and quartics, normal basis

**Norm and trace.** (Milne "Norms and traces" §; Conrad `tracenorm`, `tracenorm2`;
Clark Ch. 6; Lang VI §5; Isaacs Ch. 23.)
For E/F finite and α ∈ E, let α_L : E → E be multiplication by α. Define
Tr_{E/F}(α) = Tr(α_L), N_{E/F}(α) = det(α_L), χ_{α,E/F}(X) = char poly of α_L.
- **Milne Prop 5.44 / Conrad `tracenorm` Thm 5.9 / Clark Cor 6.5:**
  χ_{α,E/F}(X) = f(X)^{[E:F[α]]} where f is the minimal polynomial of α.
  *Proof:* first for E = F[α] via Cayley–Hamilton (χ_α(α) = 0 so f | χ_α, both monic of the
  same degree); then block-diagonalise on the basis {β_iγ_k}.
- **Milne Cor 5.45:** Tr = m Σα_i, N = (∏α_i)^m with m = [E:F[α]] and α_i the roots of f.
- **Milne Rem 5.47 / Clark Thm 6.7 / Conrad `tracenorm2` Thm 5.1:** for E/F **separable**,
  Tr = Σ_{σ∈Σ}σα and N = ∏_{σ∈Σ}σα over the F-embeddings Σ into an algebraic closure; for
  E/F Galois, over G. **In general** (Bourbaki, Algèbre V §8) Tr = p^e·Σσα and N = (∏σα)^{p^e}
  with p^e = [E : SepCl] — **so Tr ≡ 0 identically whenever E/F is inseparable**, which is
  Clark's Thm 6.10 (iv)⟹(iii) contrapositive.
- **Clark Thm 6.10:** the trace form T(x,y) = Tr(xy) is nondegenerate ⟺ Tr ≢ 0 ⟺ Tr is onto
  ⟺ E/F is separable. *Proof (iv)⟹(i):* primitive element + Vandermonde,
  det(Tr(α^{i+j−2})) = det(VVᵀ) = (∏_{i>j}(α_i − α_j))² ≠ 0.
- **Transitivity** (Milne Prop 5.48; Conrad `tracenorm2` Thm 4.1/4.3; Clark Prop 6.6):
  Tr_{M/F}∘Tr_{E/M} = Tr_{E/F} and likewise for N.

**Discriminant.** Δ(f) = ∏_{i<j}(r_j − r_i) (depends on the numbering), D(f) = Δ(f)²
(does not). Conrad `galoisaspermgp` Def 4.1 uses disc f = ∏_{i<j}(r_j − r_i)².
- **Milne Prop 4.1 / Conrad Thm 4.7:** σΔ = sign(σ)Δ, so σD = D and hence D ∈ F.
- **Milne Cor 4.2(b) / Conrad Thm 4.7:** **char F ≠ 2**: the fixed field of A_n ∩ G_f is
  F[Δ(f)], and G_f ⊆ A_n ⟺ D(f) is a square in F.
- **Conrad Rem 4.8 is essential:** "Theorem 4.7 is completely false in characteristic 2:
  discriminants of polynomials are always squares in characteristic 2 but S_n can occur as a
  Galois group." His witness: over F(u) with char F = 2, T³ + uT + u is separable irreducible
  with discriminant u² (a square) and Galois group S₃. **Milne Aside 4.4** gives the char-2
  substitute, the **Berlekamp discriminant** D = Σ_{i<j} α_iα_j/(α_i² + α_j²), with
  G_f ⊆ A_n ⟺ ∃δ ∈ F with δ² + δ = D (Berlekamp, J. Algebra 38 (1976), 315–317) — an
  Artin–Schreier condition, pleasingly.
- **Milne Rem 4.3:** for F ⊆ ℝ, sign D(f) = (−1)^s with 2s = the number of nonreal roots; so
  s odd ⟹ G_f ⊄ A_n. The converse fails.
- **Explicit formulas** (Conrad `galoisaspermgp` §4): disc(T²+aT+b) = a² − 4b;
  disc(T³+aT+b) = −4a³ − 27b²; disc(T⁴+aT+b) = −27a⁴ + 256b³;
  disc(T⁵+aT+b) = 256a⁵ + 3125b⁴; and
  disc(T^n + aT + b) = (−1)^{n(n−1)/2}((−1)^{n−1}(n−1)^{n−1}a^n + n^n b^{n−1}) — which Milne
  Example 5.50 derives from Prop 5.49, disc f = (−1)^{m(m−1)/2}N_{F[α]/F}(f′(α)).
  Also disc(T⁴ + aT² + b) = 16b(a² − 4b)² (Conrad Ex 4.5).

**Cubics** (Conrad `galoisaspermgp` Thm 4.6, 4.9; `cubicquartic` §2; Milne Ex 4.7;
Ash 6.6.3; Artin §16.8).
> char K ≠ 2, f a separable cubic with a root r and discriminant D: the splitting field is
> **K(r, √D)**. If moreover f is irreducible: G_f ≅ A₃ if D is a square in K, ≅ S₃ otherwise.
For all characteristics including 2, use `cubicquarticallchar` Thm 2.3/2.6.

**Quartics and the resolvent cubic** (Conrad `cubicquartic` §3; Milne Lem 4.9; Ash;
Artin §16.9; D&F §14.6).
> For monic f = X⁴ + aX³ + bX² + cX + d with roots r₁,…,r₄, the **cubic resolvent** is
> R₃(X) = (X − (r₁r₂+r₃r₄))(X − (r₁r₃+r₂r₄))(X − (r₁r₄+r₂r₃))
>       = **X³ − bX² + (ac − 4d)X − (a²d + c² − 4bd)**.
> Special case a = b = 0: f = X⁴ + cX + d ⟹ R₃(X) = X³ − 4dX − c².
> **Thm 3.4:** f and R₃ have the same discriminant.
> **Thm 3.6 (char ≠ 2, f irreducible):**
> | disc f a square in K | R₃ in K[X] | G_f |
> |---|---|---|
> | no  | irreducible | S₄ |
> | yes | irreducible | A₄ |
> | no  | reducible   | D₄ or ℤ/4ℤ |
> | yes | reducible   | V (Klein four) |
> §4 then separates D₄ from ℤ/4ℤ.
Milne Lem 4.9's resolvent cubic for X⁴+bX³+cX²+dX+e is the same object in his letters.
Worked table from Conrad: X⁴−X−1 → S₄; X⁴+2X+2 → S₄; X⁴+8X+12 → A₄; X⁴+3X+3 → D₄ or ℤ4;
X⁴+36X+63 → V. **X⁴ − 2 over ℚ → D₄** (Milne Ex 4.13, resolvent cubic X³ + 8X, M = ℚ[i√2];
also Conrad `galoiscorr` Ex 3.19 and Milne Ex 3.23 for the analogous X⁵−2).

**Normal basis theorem** (Milne Def 5.17 + Thm 5.18; Conrad `linearchar` §3; Clark Thm 8.25;
Lang VI §13).
> Every finite Galois extension L/K has a **normal basis**: a K-basis of L of the form
> {σ(γ) : σ ∈ Gal(L/K)} for a single γ ∈ L.
Conrad splits it: **Thm 3.6** for K infinite (Waterhouse's proof, via Lem 3.4 that the
n-tuples (σ₁(e_i),…,σ_n(e_i)) form an L-basis of L^n, plus a determinant/Zariski-density
argument) and **Thm 3.7** for cyclic extensions (which covers all finite base fields, since
finite-field extensions are cyclic). Clark Prop 8.22 does the cyclic case (via the structure
of K[G]-modules / the minimal polynomial of σ being t^n − 1), Thm 8.24 the infinite case.
Conrad notes he'd like a uniform proof and does not have one. Conrad Thm 3.8 uses the normal
basis to reprove the additive Hilbert 90. Milne Lem 5.19 (a nonzero polynomial cannot vanish
on S^m for S infinite) is the density input.
**Examples worth carrying** (Conrad Ex 3.1–3.3): {1, i} is NOT a normal basis of ℂ/ℝ (not
conjugate); {i, −i} is conjugate but not a basis; **{1+i, 1−i} is a normal basis**.
{ζ_p, ζ_p², …, ζ_p^{p−1}} is a normal basis of ℚ(ζ_p)/ℚ but {1, ζ_p, …, ζ_p^{p−2}} is not.
For ℚ(√2,√3)/ℚ, the four conjugates of √2+√3 sum to 0 so are **dependent**; the conjugates of
1+√2+√3+√6 form a normal basis (coefficient matrix has determinant 16).

## PART 4 — Hypothesis-sensitivity and counterexample seeds

These are the places where a commonly quoted statement becomes **false** if a hypothesis is
dropped. Each is a candidate `ex-`/counterexample item.

1. **"A finite subgroup of K^× is cyclic" needs K to be a field** (or at least a domain).
   The proof (Conrad `finitefields` Lem 1.6, `cyclotomic` Thm 1.1) rests on "X^m − 1 has at
   most m roots", which fails in a ring with zero divisors. **Counterexample:**
   (ℤ/8)^× ≅ ℤ/2 × ℤ/2 is a finite subgroup of the unit group of a *ring* and is not cyclic;
   likewise the quaternion group of order 8 sits inside the units of the Hamilton
   quaternions (a division ring, not a field) and is not cyclic.
2. **The primitive element theorem needs separability — or finitely many intermediate
   fields.** **Counterexample (Milne Ex 5.5; Clark Cor 7.5):** k algebraically closed of
   characteristic p, F = k(X^p, Y^p) ⊂ E = k(X, Y). Then [E:F] = p² but α^p ∈ F for every
   α ∈ E, so every simple subextension has degree ≤ p; hence E/F is **not simple**, and it
   has infinitely many intermediate fields F[X + cY], c ∈ k, all distinct.
   **Precision warning:** the *one-variable* extension 𝔽_p(t)/𝔽_p(t^p) **IS simple** (it is
   generated by t) even though it is inseparable — being inseparable does not by itself
   destroy simplicity. Sources sometimes blur this. Use the two-variable example.
3. **The Galois correspondence in its naive form needs finiteness.** For infinite Galois
   extensions the map H ↦ Ω^H is a bijection only onto **closed** subgroups (Milne Thm 7.13);
   **counterexample:** Milne Prop 7.29 — Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) has non-open subgroups of finite index, so
   there are subgroups that are not closed and hence not of the form Gal(Ω/M).
   Milne Ex 7.17: Gal(𝔽̄_p/𝔽_p) ≅ Ẑ, and the subgroup ⟨Frobenius⟩ ≅ ℤ is a proper dense
   subgroup with the same fixed field 𝔽_p as the whole group.
4. **|Aut(L/K)| = [L:K] fails for inseparable or non-normal L/K, and cannot be repaired by
   enlarging L.** Conrad Cor 4.3 + the discussion after it: for K = 𝔽_p(u),
   L = 𝔽_p(u^{1/p}), |Aut(L/K)| = 1 < p = [L:K], and Cor 4.10 says a finite inseparable
   extension **cannot** be embedded in a finite Galois extension.
5. **ℚ(∛2)/ℚ is separable but not normal** (Milne Ex 3.8a; Clark Ex 4.18 generalises: ℚ[ⁿ√2]/ℚ
   is non-normal of degree n for every n ≥ 3). Aut(ℚ[∛2]/ℚ) = 1 (Milne Ex 3.3), so this is
   **also the standard "degree n with trivial automorphism group" example**: degree 3, trivial
   Aut. Its Galois closure is ℚ(ζ₃, ∛2) of degree 6 with group S₃ (Clark Ex 8.3).
6. **𝔽_p(T)/𝔽_p(T^p) is normal but not separable** (Milne Ex 3.8b). Another "trivial Aut"
   example: Milne Ex 3.3, for a ∈ F \ F^p, F[α] with α^p = a is a splitting field of X^p − a
   (since X^p − a = (X−α)^p) yet Aut = 1.
7. **The discriminant test G_f ⊆ A_n ⟺ disc f is a square requires char K ≠ 2.**
   Conrad Rem 4.8's witness: char F = 2, over F(u) the polynomial T³ + uT + u is separable
   irreducible with disc = u² (a square) and Galois group S₃.
8. **The cubic/quartic Galois-group tables require char K ≠ 2.** Use
   `cubicquarticallchar.pdf` for char 2.
9. **Galois's solvability theorem requires char 0.** Milne Rem 3.29's two witnesses:
   (a) inseparable f has no Galois group; (b) **X^p − X − a is separable with cyclic (hence
   solvable) Galois group of order p, yet is not solvable by radicals** in the naive sense.
10. **X^n − a irreducible needs the 4|n side condition.** a ∉ F^p for all p | n is not
    enough: with 4 | n and a ∈ −4F⁴ it can factor. **X⁴ + 4 = (X² − 2X + 2)(X² + 2X + 2)**
    over ℚ, and 4 ∉ ℚ², so the naive criterion would wrongly predict irreducibility.
    (Clark Thm 9.21; Milne Aside 5.29a citing Lang VI §9.1.)
11. **Hilbert 90's additive form needs Tr ≢ 0, i.e. separability.** Conrad `linearchar` §4
    flags this explicitly. Clark Thm 6.10: Tr ≡ 0 on any inseparable extension.
12. **Artin's theorem's "separable + bounded degree ⟹ finite" step fails without
    separability.** Conrad Rem 5.4: E = 𝔽_p(u₁^{1/p}, u₂^{1/p}, …) over F = 𝔽_p(u₁, u₂, …) has
    [E:F] = ∞ but [F(α):F] ≤ p for every α ∈ E.
13. **"K/F inseparable ⟹ K has a nontrivial purely inseparable subextension" is FALSE.**
    Clark Example 5.16 (from [Mo96, p. 48]): char 2, F = k(x,y), u a root of t² + t + x,
    S = F(u), K = S(√(uy)). K/F is not separable, yet it has no nontrivial purely inseparable
    subextension. (The dual statement — K/SepCl_K(F) is purely inseparable — *is* true,
    Clark Prop 5.18.) This is the crispest asymmetry between separability and pure
    inseparability.
14. **Gal(F[ζ_n]/F) ↪ (ℤ/n)^× need not be onto.** Milne after Prop 5.8: over ℂ the image is
    trivial; over ℝ it is {±1}; over 𝔽_q it is ⟨q mod n⟩ (Conrad Thm 2.10). Surjectivity is
    exactly the irreducibility of Φ_n over F, and is special to ℚ (and to fields "linearly
    disjoint from cyclotomy", Conrad Thm 4.4).
15. **"Exactly two nonreal roots" is sufficient but not necessary for G_f = S_p.**
    Milne Rem 4.18: X⁵ − 5X³ + 4X − 1 has all roots real and Galois group S₅.
16. **Being Galois with abelian group does not make an extension "Kummer".**
    Conrad Ex 5.8: ℚ(⁶√−3)/ℚ is Galois — but with group S₃, so not even abelian; the point is
    that the root-of-unity hypothesis is what makes Kummer theory work, and ℚ ⊉ μ₆.
    Contrapositively, Conrad also gives K = ℚ(i): K(⁸√2)/K **is** cyclic of degree 8 without
    μ₈ ⊆ K. So "μ_n ⊆ K" is sufficient, not necessary, for cyclicity.
17. **"The" algebraic closure.** Milne Warning 6.8: even for a finite field there are
    uncountably many isomorphisms between two algebraic closures, none canonical.
18. **Worked positive examples to carry.** ℚ(√2, √3)/ℚ with group V₄ = ℤ/2 × ℤ/2 and its
    three quadratic subfields ℚ(√2), ℚ(√3), ℚ(√6) — Conrad `galoiscorr` §5, Ex 3.7 (also
    ℚ(√2,√3) = ℚ(√2+√3)), `galoiscorrexamples`. **X⁴ − 2 over ℚ** with group D₄ and the full
    10-subgroup/10-subfield lattice — Milne Ex 4.13, Conrad `galoiscorr` Ex 3.19,
    `galoisaspermgp` Ex 4.15. **ℚ(ζ₇)/ℚ** with cyclic group of order 6 — Milne Ex 3.22.
    **X⁵ − 2 over ℚ** with group of order 20 (F₂₀ = ℤ/5 ⋊ ℤ/4) — Milne Ex 3.23.

## PART 5 — Gaps in this harvest that the scaffolder should know about

1. **Möbius closed form for N_q(n).** Conrad explicitly omits it ("which we omit"); Milne
   Cor 4.22 gives only divisibility. The recursion p^n = Σ_{d|n} d N_p(d) **is** proved
   (Conrad `rootirred` Thm 6.1). If the library wants
   N_q(n) = (1/n)Σ_{d|n}μ(d)q^{n/d}, it must supply Möbius inversion from a number-theory or
   combinatorics dependency, or prove it inline. **No source in this harvest proves the
   closed form.**

   theory, solvable extensions, solvability by radicals, computing Galois groups). Do not
   cite Clark for those headings.

   copy). Only the section titles. Every D&F result the commission might want is available in
