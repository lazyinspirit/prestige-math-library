6.1 Work on $[0,T]$, choosing the construction on $[0,T+1]$ to allow small time translations. Put $u=\dot x=-\xi$ and $e(t)=E(x(t))$. The discrete bound $E(\bar x_\tau(t))\le E(x_0)$, uniform convergence and lower semicontinuity give $m\le e(t)\le E(x_0)$ for every $t$, where $m=\inf_HE$. As a lower-semicontinuous function of time, $e$ is measurable and bounded. Extend $u$ and $\xi$ by zero outside $(0,T+1)$. Time translations are continuous in $L^2(\mathbb R;H)$: this is immediate for interval step functions because the measure of the symmetric difference of an interval with its translate tends to zero, and holds for every $L^2$ function by the density in [F1] and the isometry of translations. For $0<a<b<T$ and sufficiently small $h>0$, put $q_h(r)=(x(r+h)-x(r-h))/(2h)=(2h)^{-1}\int_{-h}^h u(r+s)ds$ for $a<r<b$. Cauchy–Schwarz in the averaging variable and translation continuity give $q_h\to u$ in $L^2(a,b;H)$ and $\sup_h\|q_h\|_{L^2(a,b;H)}<\infty$. Indeed, $\int_a^b\|q_h-u\|_H^2dr\le(2h)^{-1}\int_{-h}^h\int_a^b\|u(r+s)-u(r)\|_H^2drds\to0$. [F1, F5, step 2.1, step 4.1]

6.2 For each sufficiently small fixed $h$, the subgradient inequalities at $x(r-h)$ and $x(r+h)$ hold for almost every $r\in(a,b)$ and imply $\langle\xi(r-h),q_h(r)\rangle_H+\lambda h\|q_h(r)\|_H^2\le(e(r+h)-e(r-h))/(2h)\le\langle\xi(r+h),q_h(r)\rangle_H-\lambda h\|q_h(r)\|_H^2$. Both pairings converge in $L^1(a,b)$ to $g(r)=\langle\xi(r),u(r)\rangle_H$, since translations of $\xi$ and $q_h$ converge in $L^2$ and Cauchy–Schwarz bounds their products. The quadratic remainder has $L^1$ norm at most $|\lambda|h\|q_h\|_{L^2(a,b;H)}^2\to0$. The sandwich therefore implies convergence of the entire centered energy quotient to $g$ in $L^1(a,b)$: if $L_h\le d_h\le U_h$, then $|d_h-g|\le|L_h-g|+|U_h-g|$ almost everywhere. 6.3 For every $0<a<b<T$ the exact integral identity is $\int_a^b(e(r+h)-e(r-h))/(2h)dr=(2h)^{-1}(\int_{b-h}^{b+h}e(r)dr-\int_{a-h}^{a+h}e(r)dr)$. Thus [F9] makes $\widetilde e$ absolutely continuous and [F10] gives $\widetilde e'=g$ almost everywhere, while the preceding equality implies $e=\widetilde e$ on $B$. This proves an absolutely continuous representative by an integral identity, without inferring it from an almost-everywhere derivative. [F5, F8, F9, F10, step 6.2]
