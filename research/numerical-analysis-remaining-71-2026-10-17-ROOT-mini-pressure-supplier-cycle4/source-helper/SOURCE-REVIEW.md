## 1. Heywood–Walsh: a genuine nonlinear initial-pressure warning, with precise limits

John G. Heywood and Owen D. Walsh, *A counter-example concerning the pressure in the Navier-Stokes equations, as t → 0+*, Pacific J. Math. **164** (1994), 351–359.

Full PDF: https://msp.org/pjm/1994/164-2/pjm-v164-n2-p06-s.pdf

Saved as `heywood-walsh.pdf`; text extraction is `heywood-walsh.txt`. Read all mathematical pages, printed pp.351–359 (PDF pp.2–10). The PDF has cover and journal matter, making 13 pages total.

Exact hypotheses and conclusion: a bounded C² domain in R² or R³, zero boundary velocity, unforced **nonlinear** Navier–Stokes, and initial velocity u0 in J1(Ω), the H¹_0-solenoidal closure. The theorem, printed p.352/PDF p.3, gives a solution whose mean-zero pressure has

$$\limsup_{t\downarrow0}\|p(t)\|_{L^2}=\infty,$$

and whose actual full negative-Sobolev derivative norm likewise has unbounded limsup, while the velocity Dirichlet norm is continuous at zero.

The mechanism is explicit and important. On printed pp.354–355, one starts with an a in J1∩H² whose stationary Stokes pressure is nonzero. Approximate a in H¹ by a compactly supported smooth solenoidal φ. The velocity a−φ can be arbitrarily small in H¹, but the linear pressure generated by Δ(a−φ) agrees with that of Δa, since Δφ is compactly supported and solenoidal. Scaling gives arbitrarily large initial pressure with arbitrarily small H¹ velocity. The nonlinear pressure is treated by the quadratic scaling and, in the recursive perturbation, by a cross-term estimate. Printed pp.355–356 then construct an infinite sequence of smooth initial perturbations with rapidly decreasing H¹ norms, and use a positive-time continuous-dependence estimate to retain arbitrarily large pressure values at times tending to zero. Section 4 proves that dependence estimate; it uses the smooth-domain stationary Stokes H² estimate.

This is stronger than a merely linear or pressure-free observation: it treats actual unforced NS and actual pressure. Its limitations remain decisive:

- C² is not the required polyhedral-domain class. Its stationary Stokes regularity is used in the proof, so transfer cannot be assumed.
- Unbounded limsup does not imply failure of L^(4/3) time integrability. The recursively chosen intervals on which pressure is large have no lower measure bound strong enough to yield the required divergence of the integral.
- It does not prove that the exact original PE06 pressure statement is false. Even t^(-1/2), which blows up, belongs to L^(4/3)(0,T).

Thus no counterexample item is proposed. This source does establish that pointwise pressure estimates cannot be inferred merely from the corresponding velocity Dirichlet control, even for genuine nonlinear NS on smooth bounded domains.

## 2. Wolf: maximal regularity explicitly requires zero initial data

Jörg Wolf, *A note on the pressure of strong solutions to the Stokes system in bounded and exterior domains*, arXiv:1506.03675v2 (13 June 2015); journal DOI 10.1007/s00021-017-0341-6.

Full PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.03675

Saved as `1506.03675.pdf`; extraction `1506.03675.txt`. Read all 13 pages, including the proof of Theorem 1 and references.

Equations (1.1)–(1.4), PDF p.1, impose u(0)=0. Definition 1.1 and Theorem 1, p.2, treat the strong Stokes solution with initial value zero. Theorem 1 requires boundary C^(2+k), forcing f in L^s_t W^(k,q)_x, and derives the corresponding additional spatial regularity of ∇p. This is a boundary spatial-regularity statement under stronger domain/data assumptions, not the target energy-pressure statement.

The introduction, p.2, expressly states: “our result relies essentially on the fact that the initial data is zero,” then points to Heywood–Walsh and its pressure blowup despite continuity of the velocity gradient norm.

Lemma 3.1 on PDF p.5 states the Giga–Sohr mixed-norm maximal-regularity estimate, for C² bounded/exterior domains, half-space or whole space, and **v(0)=0**:

$$\|v_t\|_{L^sL^q}+\|\nabla^2v\|_{L^sL^q}+\|\nabla\pi\|_{L^sL^q}\le C\|g\|_{L^sL^q}.$$

The proof then uses that initial-value-zero estimate throughout the localization/differentiation argument, pp.5–12. One cannot insert arbitrary u0∈H_sigma into it, or ignore the homogeneous Stokes component after splitting off the forcing solution. The required original-domain and initial-data arguments are not present.

Wolf supplies an actual full source for the zero-initial-data restriction; this restriction was not inferred from an abstract. The maximal-regularity lemma itself is cited to Giga–Sohr rather than proved there; it is therefore not a ready locally proved canonical supplier.

## 3. Wolf: the energy-level regular term is accompanied by a temporal harmonic term

Jörg Wolf, *On the local pressure of the Navier-Stokes equations and related systems*, arXiv:1611.01482v1 (4 November 2016).

Full PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.01482

Saved as `1611.01482.pdf`; extraction `1611.01482.txt`. Actually inspected introduction and notation pp.2–6, Theorem 6.1 and its projection proof pp.18–20, and Section 7/Theorem 7.2 with proof pp.23–24. This is **not** a claim to have checked every intermediate spatial-projection theorem in all 31 pages.

The introduction already displays the decomposition

$$p=\partial_t p_h+p_0.$$

Theorem 6.1 constructs it by applying a spatial pressure projection to the actual time-distribution equation. The temporal derivative remains on the harmonic potential generated by the velocity. In the notation adapted to our sign convention, if E* is the bounded H^-1 pressure projection and u_t+F is a gradient distribution, then

$$u_t+F=\partial_t(E^*u)+E^*F.$$

The scalar potentials of E*u and E*F give p_h and p_0, with the corresponding signs. It is the derivative of the **whole** expression that matters. Knowing E*F in L^(4/3)_tH^-1 does not establish the same regularity for ∂t(E*u).

Theorem 7.2 and its proof use precisely the energy interpolation

$$u\in L^{8/d}_tL^4_x,\qquad u\otimes u\in L^{4/d}_tL^2_x.$$

Together with the viscous tensor and the L² forcing tensor, this gives a regular pressure component p_0 at exponent 4/d, hence 4/3 in dimension three. The theorem nonetheless retains ∂t p_h in the full pressure. It does not claim that the entire pressure is p_0.

Scope details: Section 7 permits distributional bounded-energy solutions and variable viscosity, and uses a force written as div of a tensor. Its displayed definition does not impose the original no-slip boundary condition. The additional global description there assumes a bounded C¹ domain. Thus one must not identify its general distributional examples or its global C¹ theorem with the exact original polyhedral no-slip claim. In particular, the potential-flow example on p.3 allows arbitrary temporal factors only because it omits boundary conditions; the text explicitly says that under smooth no-slip it becomes trivial. This example cannot refute the target claim.

The preprint contains apparent typographical inconsistencies (for example, f/g labels are swapped in one proof display, and a displayed “symmetric” gradient uses a minus sign). The structural distribution/projection identity above has been checked directly and does not rely on those typographical displays. No item is proposed based on an uncorrected copied formula.

## 4. New exact missing interface exposed by this source route

Write the original actual equation as

$$u_t=g-\nabla p,$$

where energy bounds give g∈L^(4/3)_tV*. A pressure projection E* gives

$$\nabla p=E^*g-\partial_t(E^*u).$$

The regular component E*g is controlled. To close the original claim by this route one must control the actual harmonic-gradient derivative ∂t(E*u) in L^(4/3)_tV*, or find a valid alternative argument with the same consequence. Applying E* to an arbitrarily chosen extension of the restricted derivative does not establish this identity with that extension as the actual derivative.

This is a reformulation of the already-local exact full-derivative criterion, not a completed new theorem. It does not add a stronger assumption to the original claim. No new prerequisite ID is ready.
